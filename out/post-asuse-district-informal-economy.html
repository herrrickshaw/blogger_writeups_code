India’s First District Map of the Informal Economy: Ten Districts Hold a Tenth of It, and Two of Their Four States Fall Below Average Output per Worker

On 10 September the National Statistical Office put out district-level estimates from its Annual Survey of Unincorporated Sector Enterprises for 2025. It is the first time a large national survey of this part of the economy, the shops, workshops and service businesses that are not companies, has been published below state level. The survey year is January to December 2025, and it covers manufacturing, trade and other services but not construction.

The headline finding is concentration. Ten districts hold about a tenth of the sector’s establishments, workers and output, and fifty hold nearly a third. The ten are in four states: Gujarat, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal. Those four states are not alike. Two of them have workers who add more value than the national average and two have workers who add less, which means that a district’s rank by size says little about how well its businesses do.

The state backdrop

MoSPI’s own dataset gives the national picture the district release breaks down. In 2025 India had about 7.92 crore establishments in this sector. Uttar Pradesh (1.09 crore) and West Bengal (1.04 crore) together hold more than a quarter of them. Output per worker averaged ₹1,56,539 across market establishments, but ₹1,19,380 in rural areas and ₹1,89,284 in urban, a gap of 1.6 times.

The two largest states are both below the national figure on output per worker: West Bengal at ₹1,18,060 is 25% below the national figure and Uttar Pradesh at ₹1,25,993 is 20% below. Telangana is 26% above, Maharashtra 22% above and Gujarat 8% above. Odisha and Madhya Pradesh are the lowest of the twelve, at 36% and 28% below. These are state averages; the district release is where the spread inside each state should show.

West Bengal’s eight districts above 5 lakh establishments hold at least 39% of the state’s total, the most concentrated of the six states. Uttar Pradesh, by contrast, has one such district in a state of 1.09 crore establishments; its informal economy is spread widely. Reported shares for individual districts appear to be each district’s share of its own state: 23.5% for Rangareddy in Telangana, 19.4% for Surat in Gujarat and 16.0% for North 24 Parganas in West Bengal. Those fit the table: Rangareddy’s 23.5% of Telangana’s 29.4 lakh is about 6.9 lakh, which is above 5 lakh as it should be. The press note itself was not available for this piece, so the definition is an inference.

On output per worker, 280 of the 757 districts are above the national average and 331 fall between ₹1 lakh and ₹1.5 lakh. That leaves about 146 districts either below ₹1 lakh or in the narrow band just under the national figure. Women are at least a third of the workforce in 237 districts and more than half in 25. The districts with the highest shares of female-led businesses are in Telangana, Manipur and Meghalaya, and nationally about 27% of proprietary establishments are owned by women, up from 26.2% in 2023-24.

How far to trust a district number

A district estimate comes from a survey designed to be reliable for states, so each district’s sample is small. One analysis of the release warns that estimates for neighbouring districts should not be ranked against each other with confidence. The district figures include the district’s large cities, so city and district totals cannot be added. The release covers 757 of the 770 districts in the survey’s sampling frame, which is not the same as today’s administrative map. And “unincorporated” does not mean unregistered: a firm can be registered for GST or on the Udyam portal and still be an unincorporated proprietorship. The figures here are from press coverage and MoSPI’s dataset, not the district report, which was not available to this blog.

What would fix this

  • The National Statistical Office should publish the district tables as downloadable data with the relative standard error of every estimate, and flag districts whose error is large, so a rank of one district over its neighbour is not read as a finding.
  • The Ministry of Statistics should publish a crosswalk from the 770 sampling-frame districts to current administrative districts, naming the 13 not covered, so district totals reconcile to state totals.
  • The National Statistical Office should publish output per worker by district for manufacturing, trade and services separately, and for rural and urban areas, since the national average hides a 1.6 times rural–urban gap and a district’s average mixes very different activities.
  • The National Statistical Office should state whether the district release will be repeated each year and publish a district comparison with the 2023-24 survey, so that change in a district can be seen rather than a single year’s ranking.
  • The Ministry of Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises should publish, for each district, registrations on the Udyam portal as a share of the survey’s estimate of establishments, so districts where few informal firms are registered can be targeted by credit and formalisation schemes.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
