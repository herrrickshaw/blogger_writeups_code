Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 22 states and union territories have enacted right-to-service laws, according to two 2025 answers to Parliament; sources read name 27, so the 22 is not a complete list and none of the answers names them.
  • Rs 500 to Rs 10,000 is the range of per-case caps in the 27 laws whose penalty clause was read: Karnataka Rs 500, West Bengal and Chhattisgarh Rs 1,000, Goa Rs 2,500, nineteen laws Rs 5,000, and four laws Rs 10,000; Bihar’s Act leaves the amounts to its Rules, which give Rs 5,000, and Assam’s first stage caps at Rs 5,000 with Rs 7,000 and Rs 10,000 at later stages.
  • 2010 is the year Madhya Pradesh enacted the first such law, followed by eight state laws dated 2011 in a 2012 PRS table, including Bihar, Rajasthan and the first Delhi Act.

What it means: A citizen in Karnataka or Chhattisgarh is paid for a delay and a citizen in Delhi is not, while 27 laws print a per-case cap between Rs 500 and Rs 10,000, and Assam allows Rs 5,000, Rs 7,000 and Rs 10,000 at three stages.

What to do: Each state should print the delay rate, the cap and the payee in the Schedule entry for each service so that a rule change is visible to applicants.

What would fix this

  1. State right-to-service commissions: publish an annual report with applications received, delivered in time, late, rejected and pending, by department.
  2. State governments: print the delay rate, the cap and the payee beside each service in the Schedule.
  3. Department of Administrative Reforms and Public Grievances, Government of India: keep one dated list of the states and union territories with a right-to-service law, with the Act, its year and whether it is in force.
  4. PRS Legislative Research and state legislative departments: post searchable text of the Acts, since the Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan files are page scans.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Who is covered, and what is counted
  • Delhi 2026: substantive government servants and employees of local bodies and authorities the Government owns, controls or substantially finances; casual, daily-wage and (unless the contract says otherwise) contract staff are excluded; only services in the Schedule are covered (section 1(4) and (5)).
  • Karnataka: services in the Schedule, with the penalty falling on the designated officer or the subordinate who defaults; nothing applies where the Government has banned or restricted the service (section 9).
  • Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan: services, designated officers, first appeal officers, second appellate authorities and time limits notified by the State Government under section 3 of each Act; Rajasthan’s Act defines a public authority to include bodies owned, controlled or substantially financed by the State.
  • Haryana, West Bengal, Jharkhand, Odisha, Goa and Gujarat: each Act applies to services notified by its State Government. Gujarat’s begins with a complaint to a grievance redressal officer and a penalty for mala fide action; Goa’s defines an authority to include non-governmental bodies that received a government grant of Rs 5 lakh or more; West Bengal’s Commission is created only if the State considers it necessary.
  • Bihar: services, designated public servants and appellate authorities notified by the Government under section 3; the penalty may be charged to the designated public servant, the appellate authority or staff (section 7(3)).
  • What is counted as a law: the 2012 PRS table counts Acts and, for Madhya Pradesh, a Vidheyak (Bill) of 2010; the 2025 PRS review counts Bills passed and an Ordinance; the Wikipedia table mixes Acts, Bills and a 2015 Maharashtra Ordinance. This piece counts a state or union territory once whatever the number of its laws, counts a Regulation made by the President for Lakshadweep as a law, and does not know how the PIB count treats Bills, Ordinances or Regulations.
  • Stipulated time limits: each Act fixes limits in a Schedule or notification, which this piece did not read for any state.
What this piece could not establish
  • Which 22. Two 2025 answers to Parliament (Rajya Sabha reply, PIB release dated 3 April 2025, and Lok Sabha unstarred question 4216 answered 26 March 2025) say the Right to Service Act has been enacted by 22 States and Union Territories, with Commissions in eight, and print no list. The names found reach 27, so at least five are left out, and which five was not established. The eight Commission names the answer prints (Assam, Chandigarh, Maharashtra, Punjab, Haryana, Meghalaya, Uttarakhand, West Bengal) must all be inside the 22, so the five left out come from the other 19 names, which narrows the question but does not answer it. The only list found that totals exactly 22 is a 2019 table by the Centre for Budget and Governance Accountability (22 states marked as having a law), which marks Meghalaya as having none and does not list Chandigarh, Ladakh or Lakshadweep, so it cannot be the list behind the 2025 answer. A 2022 scoping study by SAFAR marks 24 states with a law (it counts Andhra Pradesh and Meghalaya, and not Sikkim). A February 2024 DARPG annual report carries a chart titled Status of RTS Act across States/UTs whose extracted text shows only the numbers 7, 23 and 6; if those are counts of 36 States and UTs they would imply 30 with an Act, but the chart image could not be viewed and the numbers are unconfirmed. DARPG’s own monthly NeSDA reports (2024 to 2026) discuss Right to Service Commissions in Haryana, Bihar, Assam, Meghalaya and Chandigarh but print no list of the 22, and the DARPG pages that should hold one returned no text. A journal article from the National Law University Assam lists Telangana (2016) and Tamil Nadu (2019) among states with an Act; the next bullet explains why neither is counted. The reason may be an older count, a different treatment of Union Territory Regulations, or both; no source says. Every one of the 27 has an Act, a gazette, an official commission page or an official press note behind it, so the shortfall is in the count, not in the evidence for a law.
  • Names not counted among the 27. The Andaman and Nicobar Islands (Right to Public Services) Regulation, 2026 appears only as a draft with a blank number, dated July 2026. One law journal lists a Telangana Right to Services Act, 2016 and a Tamil Nadu Public Services Guarantee Act, 2019, but its footnotes point to a petition and a newspaper piece asking for such a law, and no Act was found for either. For Telangana, a Deccan Chronicle report of 1 August 2022 says the Act is still on paper, a South First list of August 2025 names the Right to Public Services Act among the Congress government’s unkept promises, and a V6 Velugu piece of 31 October 2026 still urges one. For Tamil Nadu, a Times of India piece of November 2022 and a Hindustan Times report of September 2021 describe a promised law, and a DT Next column of 2 November 2026 says the State Budget 2026 commits to introducing one and that the state is preparing to draft it. Neither state is counted, and both stay open to a primary-source check of their legislature lists. Nagaland is a different case: the Nagaland Right to Public Services Act, 2026 (Act 11 of 2026) was passed on 3 September 2026 and published in the Nagaland Gazette of 13 October 2026 (PRS copy, text not extractable); two news reports of the Bill and Act give Rs 250 a day up to Rs 5,000, a lump sum of Rs 500 to Rs 5,000 and 30-day appeal windows, and it comes into force on a date to be notified. It post-dates the 2025 answers to Parliament, so it is not one of the 27 names and cannot be among the five the count of 22 leaves out; it is not included in the cap counts because its penalty clause was read only in news reports.
  • The six Acts of Haryana, West Bengal, Gujarat, Goa, Jharkhand and Odisha were read as English text in search excerpts of the official gazettes and department pages, not as full documents. The Goa Act was read only from reproductions of its Gazette on commercial and open-data sites, and a Bill on the Goa Government site, so its clauses should be checked against the Goa Gazette; its section 7 (fine or compensation up to Rs 5,000) and section 12 (Rs 50 a day up to Rs 2,500) were not reconciled. Odisha’s section 8 prints no aggregate cap for the delay penalty in the excerpt. Haryana’s Commission penalty of Rs 20,000 and compensation of Rs 5,000 come from a district page. The Gujarat Act has no per-day rate, and a slide of its Administrative Reforms and Training Division says it was implemented from 1 April 2016 after staged commencement.
  • The five Acts of Uttar Pradesh, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Punjab and Kerala were read as English text in search excerpts of official copies, not as full documents. Uttarakhand’s delay-penalty rate and cap were cut off in the excerpt, so only its lump sum of Rs 500 to Rs 5,000 is stated; its Act was amended in 2014 and 2023, and the second-appeal window is the 2023 text. Himachal Pradesh prints one lump sum for failure or delay and no per-day rate. The Punjab Commission’s power of Rs 10,000 a case comes from the 2014 amendment as reproduced on a Chandigarh Housing Board site. Kerala’s Rs 15,000 fine falls on a first appellate authority, not on the officer.
  • The eleven laws read last (Jammu and Kashmir, Assam, Meghalaya, Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh, Lakshadweep, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra and Mizoram, with Chandigarh and Ladakh read through the Punjab and Jammu and Kashmir Acts they use) were read as English text in search excerpts of official copies. Assam’s section 9 has been rewritten three times: the 2019 text (Commission only, Rs 250 a day, total Rs 25,000, which no Commission could apply until one was set up), Ordinance 2 of 2024 (first appellate authority at least Rs 100 a day up to Rs 5,000, second up to Rs 7,000) and the Bill passed on 30 August 2024, which became Assam Act XXII of 2024 on assent on 25 September 2024 and was said to repeal the Ordinance. The gazette copy of Act XXII has no readable text layer (its fonts do not extract), so the in-force wording is taken from the gazette text of Ordinance 2 of 2024, read in full, and the Bill as introduced; the two agree on every rupee amount (Rs 5,000, Rs 7,000 and Rs 10,000 as totals, Rs 500 to Rs 5,000 and Rs 1,000 to Rs 5,000 for appellate authorities that delay), but the Bill words some limits without the phrase per day, and the enacted Act may differ from both. The enacted Act was not read. Later 2025 amendments (Ordinance II and Act II of 2025) change only section 8B on Commissioners’ tenure. The Assam Right to Public Services Rules, 2026 (5 August 2026) repeal the 2012 Rules, whose Rs 50 a day and Rs 2,000 figures therefore no longer apply, and refer to penalties imposed by appellate authorities under section 9, which fits the 2024 structure. A state portal page also cites the 2024 Amendment Act as the current law. Meghalaya’s Act says up to Rs 20,000 for repeated instances while the Commission’s slides say Rs 25,000; the Act text is used. Sikkim’s base penalty clause was cut off in the excerpt. Arunachal Pradesh’s first-appeal window was not visible. Chandigarh’s section 9 amounts come from the Punjab Bill of 2011 as introduced, because the enacted text was not read there; the 2017 Chandigarh Gazette scan is consistent with it but too garbled to quote, and later Punjab amendments were not checked. Ladakh’s section 11 was read in third-party reproductions of the Jammu and Kashmir Act, not in the Ladakh copy. Lakshadweep’s Regulation was amended by Regulation 15 of 2026, which was only partly read. Chhattisgarh’s section 4(4) is a cost payable to the applicant, not a penalty to the treasury. Chandigarh’s Punjab Act was extended with ‘some minor modifications’ that were not read, and Ladakh’s adapted Jammu and Kashmir text did not show its penalty clause. Mizoram’s Act was read only from sections 1 to 11(1)(a) in a public Scribd copy of the Mizoram Gazette, because the Mizoram government site refused automated requests (HTTP 403) and was not retried; the delay rate and the rest of section 11 come from an official press note in the Mizo language and a 2015 news report. The Gazette of 20 April 2015 publishes the Act, section 1(3) leaves commencement to a notification, and the press note and the news report give 1 December 2015; a state department slide that says the Act came into force on 20 April 2015 appears to give the publication date.
  • Date conflicts. The fetched PIB page carries a date of 3 April 2025, while Wikipedia cites a PIB release of 22 June 2025 for the same count. Wikipedia gives Bihar’s enactment as 25 July 2011 and a Kerala Calling article as 25 July 2012; the Act itself is titled 2011. Kerala’s Act 18 of 2012 was repealed by the Kerala Right to Public Service Act, 2025 (Act 12 of 2025, PRS copy of the digitally signed Gazette, with a news report of 5 November 2025 saying it was notified); PRS ARSL 2025 lists the measure as a Bill, and the 2025 Act’s appeal windows and Commission were not read.
  • Machine-readable text of the Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan Acts. The PRS PDFs are page scans with no text layer, so sections 6 to 8 of each were read by viewing the page images. The Madhya Pradesh amendments (Acts 22 of 2011 and 5 of 2012, and later amendments listed in PRS reviews) and the rest of the Rajasthan Act were not read for changes to the penalty.
  • Bihar’s rupee amounts come from rule 8 of the Bihar Right to Public Services Rules, 2011 (Rs 250 a day, total at most Rs 5,000; lump sums of Rs 500 to Rs 5,000), read in English and Hindi copies that agree on a third-party site and in a legal-database copy; the gazette itself and any amendment to the Rules after 2011 were not seen, because the Act leaves the rates to rules that can be changed by notification. A 2015 student article puts the penalty at Rs 500 a day, which conflicts with the Rules and is not used.
  • The Rajasthan Right to Hearing Act, 2012 is a different Act from the Rajasthan Guaranteed Delivery of Public Services Act, 2011; a search excerpt about a Rs 500 to Rs 5,000 fine on a Public Hearing Officer relates to the former and is not used here.
  • Pending applications: no pending or disposal figure was found for any of the states beyond Karnataka and Delhi, and none is given.
  • Whether each law is in force today, which Schedules exist, and how many services each state has notified (other than the Karnataka and Delhi counters in the single-state posts).
  • The 2012 PRS brief is used only for the Delhi 2011 cost of Rs 10 an application; amendments after the dates of the texts read may have changed any figure, and amendments to the Madhya Pradesh Act (Acts 22 of 2011 and 5 of 2012 and later) were not read.
