Main finding
Imports exceeded exports in every year from FY2018-19 to FY2025-26; the services surplus cuts the gap roughly in half.
The goods deficit widened from -$184.0bn to -$334.3bn over the 8 fiscal years. India's services surplus (RBI data, below) narrows the FY2024-25 gap from -$283.5bn to -$94.6bn; in the COVID year the combined position was nearly balanced. By chapter, mineral fuels and gems and jewellery carry the largest deficits, while electrical machinery grew steadily on both sides: exports +324.1%, imports +101.5%.
Updated 14 November 2026. This piece now combines the HSN chapter trade-balance analysis with the companion piece on import and export growth trends by HSN chapter, both built from the same TRADESTAT pull for FY2018-19 to FY2025-26. The combined piece sets each chapter's eight-year growth beside its contribution to the deficit, so the chapters that make the gap large can be told apart from the chapters that are making it grow.

The goods deficit widened from $184.0bn to $334.3bn in eight years

FY2020-21's much narrower gap is the COVID-19 import crash (India's imports fell further than its exports during lockdowns), a one-off rather than a structural improvement; the deficit resumed widening the very next year. FY2025-26 has now closed, but its figures are still provisional (the source was last updated 19 May 2026).

First half of 2026-27 (Commerce Ministry release of 15 October 2026, not in the chart or table): merchandise exports for April–September 2026 were $259.89bn, up 18.5%, and imports $437.53bn, up about 16%, so the goods deficit widened to $177.64bn from $156.88bn a year earlier. Services exports for the half-year are estimated at $216.31bn. In September alone the combined goods-and-services deficit was $13.30bn. Mineral fuels and gems and jewellery are the largest deficit chapters in FY2025-26

Top 8 deficit and top 8 surplus chapters, by net trade balance.

Mineral fuels (HS27) alone accounts for a -$147.5bn deficit: India imports far more crude oil and refined fuel than it exports, because its energy needs depend on imports. Pearls, gems & jewellery (HS71) is the second-largest deficit chapter, at -$81.1bn, driven mainly by gold and rough-diamond imports that are only partly re-exported as cut stones and jewellery. Pharmaceuticals, vehicles, cereals and apparel are the strongest surplus chapters, all export-led.

Electrical machinery imports doubled; mineral fuels imports grew 21.2%

Same 12 chapters as above, re-ranked by compound annual growth rate (CAGR), not size.

Fertilisers (HS31) post the highest eight-year CAGR among India's dozen largest imports, +118.9% since FY2018-19 (11.8% a year), but that number describes an endpoint, not a trend. The chapter sat flat near $6.7bn for three years, jumped to $12.8bn in FY2021-22 and peaked at $15.3bn in FY2022-23, fell back to $8.3bn by FY2024-25, then rose again to $14.6bn. It is the most volatile chapter here rather than the fastest-growing one (see the shape check). Electrical machinery (HS85), which covers semiconductors, components and consumer electronics, has doubled (+101.5%, 10.5% CAGR), and edible oils (HS15) are close behind (+97.9%). Mineral fuels, the largest import by far, grew only 21.2% over the same eight years.

Eight-year growth rates hide reversals in a third of these chapters

A compound annual rate is two numbers, eight years apart. It is a growth rate only if the six years in between sit on the line.

Ranking chapters by CAGR assumes each one grew. Splitting the window at FY2021-22, the pandemic-and-commodity-shock hinge, shows that for a third of these chapters the two halves point different ways, and the eight-year rate describes neither:

Across all 24 chapters in the two top-12 lists, 10 finished at their eight-year peak; their CAGRs mean what they appear to mean. Four are round trips, ending 20% or more below a mid-window peak, and ten sit between. Iron & steel exports are the extreme case: 0.8% a year is arithmetically correct but says nothing, because the series more than doubled to $22.9bn in FY2021-22 and gave all of it back. Electrical machinery exports grew 324%; gems and jewellery exports fell 29.9%

Top 12 export chapters by FY2025-26 value, same treatment as the import side.

Electrical machinery (HS85) is the standout export: from $12.7bn to $54.0bn, +324% (22.9% CAGR), by far the fastest-growing chapter on either side of the ledger, consistent with India's electronics-assembly export push. Pearls, gems & jewellery (HS71) is the one major export chapter moving the other way: -29.9% since FY2018-19, by far the steepest decline in either top-12 list (HS61 knitted apparel and HS62 non-knitted apparel also dipped slightly, -2.3% and -2.0%).

The largest deficit chapters are not the fastest-growing ones

Mineral fuels and gems and jewellery are the two largest deficit chapters (chart above), but the eight-year history shows neither is where the deficit's momentum comes from. Mineral fuels imports grew a modest 21.2% over eight years, which this analysis reads as largely price-driven rather than a structural shift (quantities were not pulled to test that), and gems and jewellery imports (+69.1%) grew roughly in line with the other large deficit chapters.

The chapters that grew fastest, fertilisers, electrical machinery and edible oils, point to where new import dependency is building: energy-intensive fertiliser production, electronics components not yet made domestically at scale, and edible oil consumption outpacing domestic oilseed output. Of the three, only electrical machinery grew steadily; fertilisers and edible oils did most of their growing before FY2021-22 (shape check). Electrical machinery is also growing fast on both sides of the ledger, imports of components and exports of assembled goods, which is consistent with India importing parts for final assembly and re-export rather than either pure import substitution or pure domestic manufacturing.

DPIIT's 2025-26 Annual Report (295 pages) supports this from a different data system, industrial licensing filings rather than customs data. Its Industrial Entrepreneur Memorandum (IEM) Part-A investment intentions (Appendix IV) show proposed investment in Electricals Equipment rising from ₹65,979 crore (2021) to ₹233,829 crore (2024), and in Fertilizers from ₹1,112 crore (2021) to ₹41,127 crore (2024): domestic capacity-building is scaling up fastest where import growth is fastest. Its "24 champion sub-sectors" programme names electronics and aluminium among sectors chosen partly for "need for import substitution". The report's industry sections (watches, toys, rubber goods) cite HS-code figures from the "Department of Commerce Trade portal", the same DGCI&S lineage as TRADESTAT.

Adding services cuts the FY2024-25 deficit from $283.5bn to $94.6bn

Goods balance (red) + services net (teal, stacked on top) = overall balance (marker).

Goods balance (deficit) Services net (surplus) Overall balance

Services data is India's "Non-factor Services" category from RBI's Balance of Payments "Invisibles" series (via the MoSPI connector's RBI dataset), principally software, IT/BPO, business and travel services, converted from ₹ Crore to US$ billion with fiscal-year-average exchange rates from this repo's currency segment. DPIIT does not publish services-trade statistics; its main data product is a goods import-monitoring system. DGCI&S tracks services exports through annual "Service Exports Reporting Form" (SERF) reports, but those are PDF/Excel releases, not a queryable database like TRADESTAT, so RBI's series is used here. The two have not been cross-checked line by line.

FY2025-26 (not in the chart): this services series stops at FY2024-25, but the RBI released full-year FY2025-26 balance-of-payments data in June 2026. Press reports put the current-account deficit at about $25.2bn (0.6% of GDP), against $22.9bn in FY2024-25 (Outlook Business). A Commerce Ministry estimate from April 2026, as reported by India Briefing, put the combined goods-and-services deficit at about $119bn, wider than FY2024-25's $94.6bn.

The deficit and the rupee

Even with services included, India ran a current-account-style deficit every year from FY2018-19 to FY2024-25: it needed to buy more foreign currency than it earned. That is structural downward pressure on the rupee, independent of any single shock, and the backdrop the RBI has been intervening against, spending down reserves (from a $728.5bn peak to a $666.9bn trough) to slow a depreciation rather than to engineer a competitive devaluation. Reserves have since passed that peak: RBI weekly data, as reported by Devdiscourse, show about $734.6bn in the week ended 2 October 2026.

It also means the rupee's real depreciation (see this repo's effective-buying-power segment) is not noise: a widening gap between what India earns and spends abroad, even net of its strongest export sector, is a fundamental that currency markets price in over time.

FY2025-26 totals checked against three more official sources

The TRADESTAT-derived FY2025-26 totals were checked against the Commerce Ministry's Trade Intelligence and Analytics (TIA) Portal (trade-analytics.commerce.gov.in), DGFT's Trade Statistics & Analytics hub and a PIB press release for the same year.

Imports match almost to the dollar: TIA reports FY2025-26 merchandise imports of $776,013.65 million against this piece's $776,013.6 million; both trace back to DGCI&S. HS27 exports match exactly at $55,871.2 million. PIB states FY2025-26 merchandise exports of $441.78bn and a merchandise trade deficit of $333.19bn, within a rounding error of this piece's $441.7bn and -$334.3bn. Correction (14 November 2026): an earlier version reported a TIA export total of $451.07bn for FY2025-26, about $9.3bn above the other two sources. That figure is the official total for FY2022-23 (Department of Commerce, Annual Report 2025-26), read against the wrong year. The FY2025-26 export total is $441.78bn (PIB, 15 April 2026), which matches TRADESTAT. DGFT's "Import Export Data Bank" link resolves to tradestat.commerce.gov.in, so it is not an independent source. DPIIT has no trade-balance series; its investment data are covered above.

Errata & methodology caveats
  • The HSN-chapter charts are merchandise (goods) trade only, from DGCI&S; the services section adds RBI's Balance of Payments services surplus.
  • Services figures are converted from ₹ Crore with fiscal-year-average USD/INR rates computed from this repo's monthly currency series (Apr–Mar average), not RBI's official FY-average rate, which may differ slightly.
  • Services data run FY2015-16 to FY2024-25; goods data FY2018-19 to FY2025-26, so goods-plus-services is shown only for the 7 overlapping years.
  • 2-digit HS chapter level (99 possible chapters, 98 populated): coarser than TRADESTAT's 8-digit detail, but chapter values sum exactly to the national total.
  • Growth and CAGR run FY2018-19 → FY2025-26 (7 growth periods). FY2025-26 is provisional (source updated 19 May 2026), and so is every growth rate that ends in it.
  • Sparklines use an independent y-scale per chapter: compare magnitude with the labelled values and growth with the CAGR chart.
  • How this was pulled: TRADESTAT's forms are a Laravel/Livewire app; a plain HTTP GET returns 405 until a session cookie and CSRF token are set. A form POST then returns two fiscal years per query, so 8 queries covered both directions.
  • Figures are in US$ Million as reported by TRADESTAT, which also offers ₹ Crore and quantity views not pulled here.

What would fix this

  • DGCI&S should publish its Service Exports Reporting Form data as a queryable database alongside TRADESTAT and reconcile it each year with RBI’s Balance of Payments services figures, so goods and services can be combined from the Commerce Ministry’s own sources.
  • DGCI&S should provide a documented, open bulk download for TRADESTAT that needs no browser session and returns the full chapter-level export and import series for every fiscal year at once, instead of two fiscal years per session-bound query.
  • DGCI&S should mark each fiscal year in TRADESTAT as provisional or final, with the date of the last revision, so an endpoint such as FY2025-26 is not mistaken for a settled year when growth rates are computed.
  • DGCI&S should publish quantity alongside value at chapter level for volatile chapters such as fertilisers and mineral fuels, so price and volume can be told apart.
  • The Department of Commerce should show the annual series or peak year beside any multi-year growth rate it cites for a trade chapter, so round trips like iron and steel exports are not presented as steady growth.
  • RBI should publish its Non-factor Services series in US dollars, with its official fiscal-year average exchange rate, in the same machine-readable release as the rupee series.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

  • Final FY2025-26 figures. TRADESTAT and PIB both treat FY2025-26 as provisional; later revisions may move the chapter totals slightly.
  • Price against volume. Quantity data were not pulled, so the reading of mineral fuels import growth as price-driven, and the causes of the fertiliser and edible-oil swings, are untested.
  • A FY2025-26 goods-plus-services balance on the same basis as earlier years. The services series stops at FY2024-25; the later figures are press reports, and all FY2025-26 goods values are provisional.
  • Whether DPIIT investment intentions become capacity that reduces electrical-equipment or fertiliser imports.
  • Which products inside a chapter drive its growth; 8-digit tariff-line detail was not used.
