ICRA Rates Most of India's Economy Stable. Three Sectors Got Hit by the Same Shock

Skip to article content
Text Size

The short version.

  • Surveying ICRA's most recent (2025-2026) sector outlook notes across fourteen sectors of the Indian economy, eleven carry a Stable or Positive outlook: banking, NBFCs, auto components, cement, steel, power/renewables, real estate, telecom, roads, and hospitality all show moderating but still-positive growth, frequently citing the same tailwind — government infrastructure capex (cement and steel both cite India's roughly ₹12.2 lakh crore infrastructure allocation directly as their demand driver).
  • Three sectors were downgraded to Negative in 2026, and it's the same cause each time: aviation, fertilisers, and integrated oil refining and marketing were all cut within weeks of each other in ICRA's own commentary, citing the West Asia geopolitical conflict and evolving US tariff developments — not a sector-specific failure in any of the three.
  • Aviation's numbers are the starkest: an estimated ₹32,000-34,000 crore industry-wide net loss for FY2026, projected to widen to ₹36,000-38,000 crore in FY2027, with ICRA explicitly stating airlines are unlikely to fully pass through higher fuel, lease and operating costs via fares.
  • Textiles (cotton spinning) sits in a middle category worth naming separately: not downgraded to Negative, but flagged Cautious on projected margin contraction, driven by a genuinely sector-specific problem (falling domestic cotton output, acreage 20% below its 2021 peak) rather than the geopolitical shock hitting the other three.
  • Reading across all fourteen sectors together also surfaces a strategic pattern this piece lays out explicitly as its own synthesis, not an ICRA finding: what actually separates a bankable project from an exposed one is less about which sector it sits in than four structural features — contracted revenue certainty signed before financial close, a real cost-pass-through mechanism, access to shared rather than dedicated infrastructure, and diversification away from a single macro trigger.
  • A significant caveat runs through this entire piece: icra.in was blocked from direct access in this research environment, so every figure below traces to a search-indexed summary of ICRA's own report page or PDF, or to business-press coverage that names ICRA as its source — not a primary document read in full. The pattern a single-sector report can't show: three downgrades, one shared cause

Read any one of ICRA's sector notes in isolation and it reads as sector-specific analysis — margin pressure in cement, execution slippage in roads, forex exposure in aviation. Line up several sector notes from the same window of 2026 and a different picture appears: three sectors that have almost nothing in common operationally — aviation, fertiliser manufacturing, and integrated oil refining and marketing — were all downgraded to a Negative outlook within a similar timeframe in 2026, and in ICRA's own stated reasoning, for the same underlying cause: the West Asia geopolitical conflict and evolving US tariff developments. That's not three sectors independently discovering three different problems; it's one external shock working through three different transmission channels — fuel costs for airlines, raw-material costs (sulphur, ammonia, phosphoric acid) for fertiliser makers, and marketing-margin pressure for oil marketing companies — landing on ICRA's rating desk as three separate downgrades in the same period. Seeing that pattern requires reading across sectors, not within one, which is the actual point of doing this exercise rather than reading a single ICRA note on its own.

The shared "West Asia conflict and evolving US tariff developments" framing for the aviation, fertiliser and oil refining/marketing downgrades is drawn from Informist Media's coverage explicitly grouping ICRA's revisions to these three sectors together, cross-checked against each sector's own ICRA report page (icra.in) reached via search index; icra.in itself was blocked from direct fetch in this research environment throughout this piece, so no figure below rests on a primary document read in full.

Where ICRA sees stability: financial services

Banking carries a Stable outlook into FY2027, with credit growth expected to moderate to 11.0-11.7% from 15.9% in FY2026 — a deliberate slowdown, not a warning sign, in ICRA's framing, with net interest margins under near-term pressure from elevated funding costs before an expected easing later in FY2027. Comfortable capitalisation and manageable asset quality are cited as the buffers holding the outlook at Stable despite the growth deceleration. NBFCs carry a similarly constructive read: retail assets-under-management growth projected at 16-18% for FY2027 (down only modestly from 17-19% in FY2026), with credit costs actually expected to ease slightly (2.0-2.2%, from 2.1-2.3%) and cost of funds falling 10-30 basis points — net profitability is expected to improve specifically because credit-cost relief outpaces any margin or opex pressure.

Banking's FY2027 credit-growth figures (11.0-11.7%, down from 15.9%) and NIM commentary are from an ICRA press release dated 22 April 2026 and a rationale document dated 8 April 2026 (both icra.in-hosted, reached via search index). NBFC AUM growth, credit-cost and cost-of-funds figures are from ICRA's April 2026 and January 2026 Non-Banking Financial Companies research notes, similarly reached via search index rather than direct fetch.

Industrials: margin pressure almost everywhere, government capex the common answer

Auto components carry a Stable outlook, but the growth deceleration is real: industry revenue growth is projected at 8-10% for FY2026, down sharply from 14% in FY2024, with operating margins holding at 11-12% and roughly ₹25,000-30,000 crore in planned capex directed at capacity and EV-component localisation. That localisation point is worth dwelling on: only 30-40% of India's EV supply chain is currently localised, with battery cells — 35-40% of a vehicle's total cost — still almost entirely imported, which ICRA's commentary flags as the next major investment gap rather than a solved problem.

Cement and steel both carry Stable-to-improving outlooks that lean on the identical justification: India's roughly ₹12.2 lakh crore government infrastructure capital-expenditure allocation across roads, rail and urban infrastructure. For cement, that demand tailwind is expected to drive 7-8% volume growth in FY2027, even as OPBIDTA per tonne compresses from an estimated ₹900-950 in FY2026 to ₹820-870 in FY2027 on input-cost pressure, with West Asia-linked crude price movements cited as a specific downside risk to watch. For steel, the same government capex allocation (roughly 25% higher for steel-intensive sectors specifically) is expected to push demand growth from 7.6% in FY2026 to 9-10% in FY2027, with operating margins holding roughly flat around 14.8% despite elevated coking coal and iron ore costs, even as sector leverage (total debt to OPBDITA) is projected to rise from 2.6x to about 3.0x. One steel-specific data point worth flagging on its own: capital intensity for new capacity, at roughly $176 per tonne of installed capacity as of March 2026, sits well below the sector's historical peak of around $450 per tonne — ICRA's own reading is that this makes the industry structurally more resilient to a cyclical downturn than it was in past cycles, a specific claim rather than a generic "the sector is healthier now" statement.

Auto component figures (8-10% FY26 revenue growth, 11-12% margins, ₹25,000-30,000 crore capex, 30-40% EV localisation) are from Autocar Professional's coverage of ICRA's auto-component and vehicle-volume forecasts, published in 2025 and 2026 respectively. Cement's FY2027 volume-growth and OPBIDTA/tonne figures are from Business Standard (23 September 2025) and Indian Cement Review, both citing ICRA's own research directly. Steel's demand-growth, margin, leverage and capital-intensity figures are from ICRA's "Steel Industry — Trends & Outlook" note dated May 2026, reached via search index.

Energy and infrastructure: execution bottlenecks more than demand problems

Power and renewable energy carry a Stable outlook with a genuinely large pipeline behind it: more than 150 GW of renewable capacity was under construction as of 30 June 2026, and renewable energy's share of generation (including large hydro) is projected to cross 35% by FY2029-30, up from 22% in FY2024-25. The specific number worth flagging is the execution gap behind that pipeline: 40-45 GW of capacity still had unsigned power purchase agreements as of April 2026, and ICRA's own commentary names land acquisition, transmission infrastructure buildout, PPA-signing delays, equipment-price exposure and the financial health of state electricity distribution utilities (discoms) as the specific risks to watch — a a demand story with an execution problem sitting underneath it, not a demand problem.

Roads carry a Stable outlook specifically for the toll segment, but the execution slowdown here is sharper and already realised rather than a forward risk: road construction execution fell roughly 12% year-on-year in the first seven months of FY2026, moderating to a projected 9,000-9,500 km for the full year against 10,660 km completed in FY2025. Toll collection growth is still projected at a healthy 5-9% for FY2026, moderating to 5-8% in FY2027, and NHAI's asset-monetisation programme (via Toll-Operate-Transfer bundles) raised ₹12,357 crore in the first nine months of FY2026 alone, with full-year proceeds expected to reach roughly ₹1.3 lakh crore. A specific, usable figure for anyone tracking toll-road annuity economics: inflation-linked toll-rate hikes for FY2027 are projected at roughly 3.2% for newer projects and 1.6-2.0% for older ones.

Power/renewable energy figures (150+ GW under construction, 35% generation-share target, 40-45 GW unsigned PPAs) are from ICRA's "Indian Renewable Energy Sector" research note dated June 2026 and a related press release dated 7 May 2026, both reached via search index. Roads figures (execution decline, toll growth, NHAI monetisation, toll-hike projections) are from ICRA's Indian Roads Sector notes dated February and March 2026, cross-checked against Tribune India's coverage of the same data.

Consumer and services: moderating growth, not a slowdown story

Residential real estate carries a Stable outlook, recovering rather than accelerating: new launches across the top seven cities are projected to grow 6-9% in FY2026 to roughly 620-640 million square feet, a real recovery from a 14-17% decline in FY2025, though still below the record 701 million square feet launched in FY2024. The segment mix is shifting away from affordable housing toward mid-market and luxury product, and ICRA's specific forward-looking read is that larger, more reputed developers are positioned to keep gaining market share as the sector consolidates — a claim about which companies benefit, not a claim about the sector's aggregate health.

Hospitality carries a Stable-to-positive outlook with moderating growth: pan-India premium hotel RevPAR is estimated to reach ₹5,900-6,300 in FY2026, rising further to ₹6,200-6,500 in FY2027, with occupancy holding at 72-74% through both years and industry revenue growth slowing from 9-12% (FY2026) to 7-9% (FY2027). Telecom carries a Positive-leaning outlook, though it's worth flagging that this piece could not find an ICRA telecom note dated later than May 2025 — the figures here (ARPU trending toward ₹200-210, an estimated further ₹35-40 of ARPU needed to reach 15% ROCE industry-wide, another 12-15% tariff hike anticipated around mid-2026) are accordingly less current than the rest of this piece and should be treated as a slightly older data point.

Cotton spinning and textiles sit apart from both the Stable group and the Negative group described in Section 6, and deserve their own label: Cautious. Spinner margins are projected to contract 50-100 basis points in FY2026, with a sample of thirteen companies representing 25-30% of industry revenue projected to see a 4-6% year-on-year revenue decline. Unlike aviation, fertilisers and oil marketing, this pressure traces to a genuinely sector-specific cause rather than the shared geopolitical shock: India's cotton output is projected to fall 1.7% year-on-year in calendar year 2026, with sown acreage now roughly 20% below its 2021 peak. ICRA's own offsetting factor to watch is a domestic cotton-yarn demand recovery, aided by a "China plus one" sourcing shift among global apparel buyers and improving global apparel demand generally.

Real estate figures are from ICRA's Residential Real Estate note dated March 2026 and Business Standard's coverage (11 September 2025) of the same underlying data. Hospitality figures are from ICRA's Indian Hospitality Industry note dated March 2026 and Hotelier India's coverage. Telecom figures are from ICRA's Indian Telecom Industry note dated May 2025 (the most recent ICRA-attributed telecom figure found in this piece's research) and Tribune India's coverage; the absence of a 2026-dated ICRA telecom note is reported as a genuine gap in this piece's sourcing, not an assumption that ICRA has not published one. Cotton spinning figures are from ICRA's Indian Cotton Spinning Industry note dated January 2026 and Fibre2Fashion's coverage.

The Negative cluster, in detail: aviation, fertilisers, and oil refining/marketing

Aviation's downgrade, from Stable to Negative in March 2026, carries the starkest numbers in this entire survey: ICRA estimates an industry-wide net loss of ₹32,000-34,000 crore for FY2026, driven by forex losses tied to rupee depreciation, moderating passenger-traffic growth, and higher fuel prices following the West Asia conflict — and projects that loss widening further, to ₹36,000-38,000 crore, in FY2027. Domestic passenger-traffic growth guidance was cut to 3-6% for FY2027, down from an earlier 6-8% expectation. The single most direct statement in ICRA's aviation commentary, worth quoting in substance rather than softening: airlines are explicitly flagged as unlikely to fully pass through higher fuel, lease and operating costs via fare increases — a direct statement that margin pressure here is expected to persist rather than self-correct through pricing.

Fertiliser manufacturing carries the same Negative outlook and the same stated cause, with its own specific transmission mechanism: subsidy levels are described as inadequate relative to elevated raw-material costs (sulphur, ammonia, phosphoric acid), and urea plant operating rates are expected to stay below 80% given curtailed natural gas availability, with FY2027 sales volumes expected to moderate accordingly. The policy variable ICRA flags as the one to watch here is specific and actionable: whether government subsidy levels are adjusted to keep pace with raw-material cost pass-through, since that gap is what's driving the downgrade rather than any demand-side weakness.

Oil refining and marketing splits within itself: integrated players carry the Negative outlook, while standalone refiners are rated Stable — a distinction worth not collapsing into one blanket "oil sector negative" headline. For the integrated players, and specifically the state oil marketing companies, the pressure comes from negative marketing margins (LPG under-recoveries, subdued retail fuel spreads) even as gross refining margins themselves remain healthy; petroleum product consumption is still projected to grow 2-3% year-on-year in FY2026 (petrol 6-7%, diesel 1-2%, aviation turbine fuel 7-8%), meaning this is a margin-structure downgrade layered on top of genuine demand growth, not a demand-side downgrade.

Aviation's loss figures, traffic-growth guidance and the fare-pass-through statement are from ICRA's Indian Aviation Industry research (2026) and corroborating coverage in Outlook Business and Indian Aviation News (July 2026). Fertiliser figures (subsidy inadequacy, sub-80% urea utilisation) are from ICRA's fertiliser-sector research note and a press release dated 29 April 2026. Oil refining/marketing's integrated-versus-standalone split, marketing-margin pressure, and POL consumption growth figures are from ICRA's Refining and Marketing Industry note dated March 2026 and Informist Media's coverage grouping this downgrade with aviation and fertilisers under the same West Asia/tariff rationale. All three notes were reached via search index; icra.in itself was not directly reachable in this research environment for any of them.

A strategic advisory: what actually makes these investments more bankable

What follows in this section is this piece's own reading, offered explicitly as strategic commentary rather than as an ICRA recommendation — ICRA's own notes name sector-specific risks to watch, but do not themselves offer a cross-sector playbook for improving bankability, and this section shouldn't be read as if it does. Read across the fourteen sectors above, though, a consistent pattern emerges in what separates a sector or project ICRA rates as resilient from one flagged as exposed, and it's less about which industry a project sits in than about four structural features a project either has or doesn't.

Revenue certainty beats revenue size. The clearest illustration in this survey is the gap inside renewable energy itself: over 150 GW of capacity is under construction, yet 40-45 GW of it still carried unsigned power purchase agreements as of April 2026. That is capacity with real demand behind it in aggregate, but without the specific, contracted, creditworthy offtake that actually makes a single project bankable to a lender. The practical lesson generalises well beyond power: a long-term, indexed, creditworthy offtake contract, signed before financial close rather than assumed to follow it, is worth more to a project's bankability than a favourable sector-wide demand forecast. NHAI's own toll-monetisation programme — raising roughly ₹12,357 crore in nine months of FY2026 by selling operating rights to already-built, already-cash-flowing toll roads — is the same logic from the other direction: a completed asset with a demonstrated, contracted revenue stream converts into fresh capital far more readily than an asset still carrying construction or offtake risk.

Cost-pass-through mechanics matter more than headline margins. Aviation and fertilisers, the two Negative-outlook sectors covered in most detail above, share a specific structural weakness that a healthy topline number can obscure: neither has a reliable mechanism to pass a sudden input-cost shock through to its own revenue. ICRA states plainly that airlines are unlikely to fully recover higher fuel, lease and operating costs through fares; fertiliser manufacturers depend on a government subsidy that the sector's own downgrade implies isn't adjusting fast enough to match raw-material costs. A project or company with an explicit, contractual cost-pass-through or indexation clause — the kind toll-road operators have, with their 3.2%/1.6-2.0% inflation-linked rate revisions built directly into concession terms — is structurally more resilient to exactly the kind of external shock (the West Asia conflict and tariff developments named repeatedly above) that this survey shows can hit unrelated sectors simultaneously. Evaluating a potential investment's bankability should mean asking specifically whether a cost shock has a contractual route to the customer or the taxpayer, not just whether current margins look adequate.

Shared infrastructure lowers the bar to bankable, one project at a time. Cement and steel both lean on the same roughly ₹12.2 lakh crore government infrastructure allocation as their demand driver, which works precisely because that capital is being spent on shared, multi-user infrastructure rather than single-project assets. That's the same underlying principle behind the "hub-and-cluster" cost-sharing model this blog has covered separately in the context of India's CO2 pipeline and storage buildout for carbon capture: pooling multiple sources or users onto one piece of shared infrastructure spreads its fixed cost across more revenue, which is often what turns a marginal, unbankable single-project economics into a viable one. A strategic investor evaluating any of the execution-bottleneck sectors flagged in this survey — renewable energy's transmission and land-acquisition gaps, roads' financing needs beyond NHAI's own monetisation capacity — should weigh whether a project can plug into shared infrastructure already being built for other reasons, rather than requiring its own dedicated buildout from zero.

Diversify away from a single macro trigger. The starkest finding in this entire piece is that three unrelated sectors were downgraded for the identical reason within the same window. That's a direct argument for diversification at the portfolio level, but it also has a project-level analogue: a project whose economics depend entirely on one commodity price, one exchange rate, or one geopolitical assumption holding steady carries a correlated risk that a lender or investor evaluating it in isolation can easily underprice. Oil marketing companies are the clearest example inside this survey of the alternative: gross refining margins have stayed healthy even as marketing margins compressed, because refining and marketing are different enough businesses, exposed to different enough drivers, that one segment's resilience partly offsets the other's exposure. Bankability, on this reading, is as much a property of how a project's specific revenue and cost drivers are diversified from each other as it is a property of the sector the project happens to sit in.

The specific data points referenced (the 40-45 GW unsigned-PPA gap, NHAI's ₹12,357 crore nine-month TOT monetisation, aviation's fare-pass-through statement, fertiliser's subsidy-adequacy gap, toll roads' 3.2%/1.6-2.0% inflation-linked rate revisions, and refining-versus-marketing margin behaviour) are drawn from the sector sections above and sourced there; this section does not introduce new factual claims beyond what's already cited, only a synthesised reading of what they collectively suggest about bankability. The hub-and-cluster cost-sharing comparison references this blog's own earlier reporting on India's CO2 pipeline and CCUS buildout, cited as this blog's own prior work rather than an external source.

What doesn't follow from any of this

None of this should be read as a claim that India's economy is broadly weakening — eleven of the fourteen sectors surveyed here carry a Stable or Positive ICRA outlook, and several of the strongest tailwinds identified (government infrastructure capex behind cement and steel, a genuinely large renewable-energy construction pipeline, recovering real-estate launch volumes) are real, structural positives independent of the three Negative-rated sectors. It also doesn't follow that aviation, fertilisers and oil marketing are failing for reasons specific to how those industries are run in India; on the evidence gathered here, all three were hit by the same external, geopolitically-driven cost shock within the same window, which is a different and more specific claim than "three unrelated sectors are struggling." And it doesn't follow that the figures in this piece should be treated as final or precise: every one of them traces to a search-indexed summary of an ICRA report page or PDF, or to business-press coverage citing ICRA, because icra.in itself was blocked from direct access throughout this piece's research. A reader relying on any specific figure here — a credit-growth number, a margin projection, a loss estimate — for an actual investment, credit, or policy decision should verify it directly against ICRA's own published report before treating it as precise.
