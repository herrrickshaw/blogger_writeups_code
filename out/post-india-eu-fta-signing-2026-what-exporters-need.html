Top line

  • 16 Dec 2026 is the signing date The Hindu reported from two diplomatic sources; Hindustan Times reported 17 or 18 December. No official date had been announced by 29 October, and entry into force is projected for early to mid-2027.
  • 90.7% of India’s exports to the EU by value enter duty-free at entry into force, the Commerce Ministry says. Another 2.9% reach zero duty over 3 to 5 years and 6% get reduced duty or quotas.
  • €82.32 per tonne of CO2e is the Commission’s CBAM certificate price for July to September 2026. The agreement gives no CBAM exemption, and steel above the quota faces the EU’s 50% out-of-quota duty.

India-EU FTA timeline: signing planned for December 2026, in force expected in 2027

  1. 27 Jan 2026Negotiations conclude at the India-EU summit in New Delhi (Commission press release).
  2. 11 Sep 2026The Commission sends its proposals for signature and conclusion to the Council, on a fast track that completes the legal work on the English text only. The published texts, including the steel quota annex, stay subject to legal revision.
  3. 29 Oct and 2 Nov 2026The Ministry of External Affairs and ANI report that legal scrubbing is still under way and that no signing date has been announced. The EU ambassador had aimed for scrubbing to finish by summer.
  4. Mid-December 2026Signing in Brussels during a planned visit by the Prime Minister, reported for 16 December (The Hindu) or 17 to 18 December (Hindustan Times). The Council has to authorise signature first; this blog found no Council decision by 16 November.
  5. Spring 2027European Parliament consent vote expected, according to the chair of its foreign affairs committee. Council conclusion and India’s ratification follow.
  6. Early to mid-2027Entry into force expected. The EU ambassador said first half of 2027 in May. Tariff cuts then phase in over up to 10 years.

What it means: No preference can be claimed before entry into force, which needs signature, Parliament consent, Council conclusion and India’s ratification, so duties in force on the import date apply until then.

What to do: Exporters should price 2027 orders at current EU duties and treat the entry-into-force notice, which has not been published, as the first date on which a claim is possible.

What it means: Steel above the quota pays the EU’s 50% duty, by a GTRI reading of the text, and the Commission has said the agreement does not change CBAM, so every covered tonne still carries a carbon charge.

What to do: Steel exporters should plan 2027 volumes around the Annex 2-B quota, not around 2025 shipments, until the Commission explains the gap of about 300,000 tonnes that Indian industry has flagged.

What it means: Preference needs a statement on origin that meets the product-specific rule, EU product and food-safety rules continue to apply, and verified actual emissions replace marked-up default values for steel, aluminium and other CBAM goods.

What to do: Exporters should build a bill-of-materials origin file for each product, check EU product rules such as REACH and labelling, and contract an accredited CBAM verifier in 2026 for the declaration due on 30 September 2027.

The prerequisites in detail
  • Origin proof is a statement, not an agency certificate. The Commission’s summary says origin rests on self-certification: a statement on origin, uploaded to a portal so the importing customs authority can verify it.
  • Origin rules are product by product. Product-specific rules sit in Annex 3-B and the authentication procedure in Annex 3-D. The statement’s form and the portal were not confirmed in the sources read.
  • Product standards are not waived. The EU keeps its sanitary and phytosanitary rules with no exception. The technical barriers chapter sets up a working group on conformity assessment that can take up India’s quality control orders, but it does not remove EU rules such as REACH or labelling.
  • Carbon verification is the importer’s legal duty and the exporter’s data task. Only an authorised CBAM declarant may import covered goods above 50 tonnes a year. Actual emissions must be verified by an accredited verifier, which can be a third-country firm accredited by an EU national accreditation body.
  • Verifiers for Indian plants are few so far. Commerce Ministry officials were reported on 17 September as saying six foreign institutions had applied to verify for Indian exporters. Recognition of Indian verifiers is a subject of the annex’s technical dialogue, not an EU rule.
  • The CBAM annex gives process, not an exemption. It extends to India any CBAM flexibility the EU gives another country and opens a dialogue on verifiers, default values and India’s own carbon price. India has no traded carbon price to deduct yet, so the deduction is zero for 2026 goods, according to Climate Decode on 20 October.

How the EUR 119 billion estimate was built
  • The Confederation of Swedish Enterprise published it in May 2026 in cooperation with the Confederation of Indian Industry; the modelling is by Copenhagen Economics, using a gravity model.
  • It assumes all tariffs are removed and non-tariff barriers and services restrictions fall by 63%. The report calls the results estimates, not forecasts, and says full details of the agreement were not yet public.
  • It assumes the agreement has been in place for 10 years by 2035. With entry into force expected in 2027, the period in force would be shorter; this is this blog’s reading.
  • Sector differences are not modelled, and some firms and sectors may lose from the competition.

What would fix this

  1. The European Commission (DG Trade) should publish a dated reconciliation of the Annex 2-B steel quotas with its June 2026 country allocations before signature.
  2. India’s Department of Commerce should publish the statement-on-origin procedure, the portal and the authentication rules of Annex 3-D before entry into force.
  3. The Commerce Ministry and the Commission, through the CBAM technical dialogue, should publish the list of verifiers that can serve Indian plants and a timetable for default values.
  4. The Bureau of Energy Efficiency should finalise the iron and steel targets under the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme, so that a domestic carbon price exists to be recognised.
  5. The Department of Commerce and DGCI&S should publish preferential-claim and utilisation data by sector each quarter from entry into force.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Method
  • Dates come from the Commission’s press releases and trade pages, Indian government statements and press reports dated 27 January to 13 November 2026. Reported dates are labelled as reported.
  • Sector values are the Commerce Ministry’s own table for calendar year 2024, which sums to USD 33,497.6 million duty-free at entry into force and USD 1,307.8 million phased. This blog added the shares.
  • The table covers 12 sectors that pay EU duty today, not all trade: India’s merchandise exports to the EU were USD 75.85 billion in 2024-25 (PIB), and EU goods imports from India were EUR 71 billion in 2024 (Commission).
  • Steel shares divide the Annex 2-B quota by the rounded 2.4 Mt export figure and match the shares Reuters reported (39.4% and 68.4%).
What this piece could not establish
  • Whether legal scrubbing is finished. The last statements found, on 29 October and 2 November, say it is ongoing. No Council decision authorising signature and no official signing date were found by 16 November.
  • The signed text. The Commission says the texts are final only on signature; every tariff schedule and quota here is as published on 11 September 2026.
  • Line-by-line staging. The tariff schedules were not opened, so no single product’s duty path is given.
  • EU-side trade data. The sector values are India’s figures; Eurostat and DG Trade data were not used to check them.
  • The CBAM annex text. It is described from a Rajya Sabha answer, the Commission’s position as reported, and trade-press and think-tank summaries.
  • FTA utilisation. No claim data exists, because the agreement is not in force.
