India's Ethanol Programme Has a Capacity Surplus and a Maize Deficit — at the Same Time

Two things are true about India's ethanol programme at the same time, and they point in opposite directions. The country built roughly twice the distillation capacity it currently needs. And the single feedstock that carried the programme to its 20% blending target — maize — is now having a real, dated, currently unfolding supply squeeze. Capacity oversupply and feedstock tightness are not the same problem, and conflating them misses both.

Two established facts, restated together

This blog has already reported that India's ethanol distillation capacity runs at roughly half its potential — about 2,000 crore litres built against 1,100–1,200 crore litres of actual E20-era demand, per CareEdge. Separately, this blog has already reported that grain, not sugarcane, got India to 20% blending: maize alone supplied 45.7% of allocated ethanol in ESY 2025-26, up from 6.2% three years earlier, with surplus FCI rice supplying more fuel than sugarcane juice does. Read separately, these look like the same story — "India overbuilt." Read together, they say something sharper: the capacity glut is real, but it is not evenly distributed across feedstocks, and the one feedstock the programme leaned on hardest has grown from almost nothing to the largest single source in three years.

The warning this blog flagged in August

A post on this blog from 21 August 2026 caught the divergence before it had a price attached to it: maize had become India's most expensive government-priced ethanol feedstock at ₹71.86 a litre, ahead of sugarcane juice, rice and every grade of molasses, with maize-ethanol volumes rising from zero to nearly 500 crore litres in three years — even as that season's actual maize acreage fell 9.7%. The demand-side price signal and the on-the-ground planting decision were pointing in opposite directions a month before the supply picture actually turned.

By September, the divergence had a price attached

Maize wholesale prices rose roughly 30% since May–June 2026, to around ₹27 a kilogram, per trade-data reporting from BusinessToday (16 September 2026); separate trade-press figures put maize around ₹26,500 a tonne against roughly ₹14,000 three years ago. A below-average 2026 monsoon, reported 18 September 2026, is threatening the standing crop directly. The US Department of Agriculture's Foreign Agricultural Service is forecasting a 10% fall in India's 2026-27 maize crop.

Sourcing on the price figures. The specific rupee-per-kilogram and rupee-per-tonne numbers above come from trade press (BusinessToday, ChiniMandi, Outlook Business) reporting market and industry data, not a government price series audited for this piece. Treat them as directionally reliable, not as an official benchmark.

Ethanol distilleries are now competing with chicken and eggs

Poultry feed is roughly 55–60% maize by weight and has traditionally been the largest single claimant on India's maize crop. Rising ethanol demand is now a second, fast-growing claimant on the same crop, and trade press (BusinessToday, Outlook Business, ChiniMandi, all September 2026) reports the competition pushing egg and chicken prices upward. India also became a net maize importer in 2024, a fact this blog has reported via a Lok Sabha reply — though no source found while researching this piece confirms the government has imported maize specifically to feed ethanol distilleries, as opposed to feed or food demand generally; that specific causal claim is not established here.

The Economic Survey gave the tension an official name

The Economic Survey 2025-26, tabled by the Finance Ministry around January 2026, explicitly flags "early warning signals" of tension between energy self-sufficiency (Aatmanirbharta) and food self-sufficiency. It documents maize acreage growing at 6.68% a year and output at an 8.77% compound annual growth rate between FY22 and FY25, even as pulses acreage and output declined over the same period, and separately flags edible-oil import dependence and food-price-volatility risk. This is a genuinely official, central-government document naming the exact tension this piece is describing — not an industry estimate or a press inference.

Why this source matters here. Every other post on this blog that has touched this topic predates the Economic Survey 2025-26. This is the first genuinely newer, more authoritative source available on the food-vs-fuel question specifically for India's ethanol programme.

What the industry says, and why that's not neutral

The Indian Sugar & Bio-energy Manufacturers Association (ISMA) has been vocal since October 2025 that sugar-based ethanol received only 289 crore litres, roughly 28% of ESY 2025-26 allocation, well short of NITI Aayog's own roadmap projection of 55% from sugar. ISMA warns of roughly ₹40,000 crore in stranded cane-distillery investment and sub-50% capacity utilisation, and is lobbying for a guaranteed 50% sugar-feedstock allocation floor.

Read ISMA's numbers as advocacy, not neutral data. ISMA represents the sugar sector specifically, which loses ground every time the ethanol programme leans further on maize and rice. Its allocation-share and stranded-investment figures are directionally consistent with the grain/cane split this blog has already reported from OMC allocation data, but its policy ask — a guaranteed cane floor — is a lobbying position, not a finding.

What would fix this

The programme already tracks a rice-versus-PDS balance for one feedstock. It has no public equivalent for the feedstock now carrying the largest share of the load.

  • Publish a rolling maize-allocation balance, the way FCI's rice release to distilleries can in principle be checked against public-distribution norms. Right now there is no published, regularly updated accounting of maize moving to ethanol versus poultry feed versus food and starch uses.
  • Resolve ISMA's sugar-floor lobbying with a public, data-backed decision, either way. Both the allocation data and the industry's counter-argument already exist; no public document currently reconciles what OMCs are actually deciding once ISMA's ask is weighed against the maize price and supply signal.
  • Publish the ethanol programme's contingency plan before a bad monsoon forces an ad hoc one. If the 2026-27 maize crop falls anywhere near USDA's forecast 10%, the programme needs a public answer on whether the marginal litre reverts to FCI rice, to cane, or whether the blending target itself flexes — decided in advance, not improvised mid-season.
  • Give the Economic Survey's own "early warning signal" language an owner. A central document naming a real tension, with no ministry publicly assigned to resolve it, is a warning with no address.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
