Every litre of petrol sold in India carries ₹5 of Road and Infrastructure Cess, and every litre of diesel ₹2 — the part of the Centre's fuel tax earmarked by law for roads and infrastructure. When the Centre cut excise by ₹10 a litre in March 2026, it left that cess untouched, so it is now 42% of what the Centre takes on a litre of petrol. Electric vehicles pay none of it, and none of the rest of the fuel tax either. Today that costs the road fund less than one per cent of its income. The question is what replaces it before it costs much more.

What the Centre takes on a litre

Central excise on petrol and diesel is four separate levies stacked together. The Petroleum Planning and Analysis Cell's Ready Reckoner lays them out as of 1 October 2025: a small basic excise duty, a special additional excise duty that does most of the revenue work, the Road and Infrastructure Cess, and an Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess. On 27 March 2026, with crude prices spiking during the Strait of Hormuz crisis, the Centre cut the special additional duty by ₹10 a litre — from ₹13 to ₹3 on petrol and from ₹10 to nil on diesel — to hold pump prices down. Nothing else moved.

Unblended petrol carries a further ₹2 basic duty. State VAT comes on top: in Delhi, about ₹15.40 a litre on petrol and ₹12.83 on diesel at October 2025 prices.

The road cess has been cut hard before. It began as ₹1 a litre in 1998–99, was formalised by the Central Road Fund Act of 2000, became the Road and Infrastructure Cess at ₹8 a litre in the 2018 Budget, and peaked at ₹18 a litre in May 2020, when the Centre used it to capture the windfall from collapsing crude prices. The excise cuts of November 2021 and May 2022 came out of it, bringing it to ₹5 on petrol and ₹2 on diesel, where it has stayed. The March 2026 cut came from somewhere else, which is why the cess is now a much larger share of a much smaller excise bill.

Where the road cess goes

By law the cess feeds the Central Road and Infrastructure Fund. Under the original Act, half of the diesel cess went to rural roads, and the rest of the diesel cess plus all of the petrol cess was split between national highways, state roads and railway bridges and safety works; a 2018 amendment widened the fund to "infrastructure" generally, including railways, ports and energy. The cess was budgeted to raise ₹45,780 crore in 2025–26 and ₹46,930 crore in 2026–27. That sounds large, but the road transport ministry's budget for 2026–27 is ₹3,09,875 crore, 60% of it for the National Highways Authority of India, so the cess covers only about 15% of central road spending. The rest comes from general tax revenue and from monetising highways already built.

Even that 15% does not reliably reach the fund. The Comptroller and Auditor General has flagged every year since 2010–11 that cess money is not fully transferred to the funds it is levied for; of ₹1,24,099 crore in cesses not transferred by March 2018, ₹72,726 crore was road cess collected since 1998–99. The road cess is earmarked in law and only loosely in practice — which matters for what happens when its base starts to shrink.

The road cess pays for about a sixth of what the Centre spends on roads. The rest already comes from general taxes — including the much larger fuel excise that EVs also do not pay.

What an electric vehicle doesn't pay

An electric vehicle draws on the same roads and pays no fuel tax at all: no road cess, no other central excise, no state VAT on fuel. The table below estimates what a typical petrol two-wheeler, petrol car and diesel bus pays each year, which is what their electric replacements avoid.

Applied to the electric vehicles sold in 2025–26 — 14,01,663 two-wheelers, 1,99,952 cars and 5,039 buses, according to Vahan registration data — that comes to about ₹235 crore a year of road cess and about ₹585 crore of central excise not collected. Electric three-wheelers are left out because most e-rickshaws replace cycle rickshaws, not fuel-burning vehicles. Against a ₹45,780 crore cess, the new cohort costs the road fund about half of one per cent a year. Each year's sales add another slice, and the electric fleet on Vahan is already around 7–8 million vehicles, many of them e-rickshaws.

Two things make the gap bigger than the road cess alone. First, the cess is the small piece. The Centre collected ₹2,71,529 crore of excise from the petroleum sector in 2024–25 and the states ₹3,02,059 crore in sales tax and VAT, according to PPAC. A November 2022 CEEW study estimated that fully electrifying cars and two-wheelers would cut central fuel excise by 28% and state fuel VAT by 20%, and that electricity duty on charging would recover only about 1% of the loss. States are the more exposed: fuel VAT is about a fifth of their own tax revenue, as this blog has shown separately. Second, EVs also escape most other vehicle taxes. They pay 5% GST against 18% or 40% for petrol and diesel cars after September 2025's GST changes, and most large states waive road tax and registration fees for them — Delhi until March 2030 for cars up to ₹30 lakh, Maharashtra until March 2030, Tamil Nadu until December 2027, Telangana until December 2026, Uttar Pradesh until October 2027. Karnataka went the other way in April 2026, reinstating lifetime road tax of 5–10% on electric cars while keeping two-wheelers exempt, the first large state to reverse.

The March 2026 cut had a side effect worth noticing: by slashing central excise, it nearly halved what a petrol two-wheeler or car pays the Centre each year — ₹2,380 now against ₹4,380 before for a two-wheeler — and so nearly halved the central tax an EV avoids. The road cess, untouched, is now the most durable part of what an EV is not paying.

How other countries are charging EVs for roads

Countries further along the transition have already moved, and their choices show the options.

The Australian case is a warning about who is allowed to charge. In India, taxes on vehicles suitable for use on roads are a state subject under Entry 57 of the State List, so states can charge EVs directly through road tax, as Karnataka has begun to; a per-kilometre charge for road use on national highways sits more naturally with the Centre, which already collects tolls. India also has the tool a distance charge needs. The National Highways Fee Rules were amended in September 2024 to allow satellite-based tolling, charging per kilometre beyond the first 20 km a day, and a pilot is running in Gujarat. No official proposal yet links that system to an EV road charge.

What would fix this

  • The Finance Ministry should publish road-cess collections and actual transfers to the Central Road and Infrastructure Fund side by side every year, and transfer the full amount — the step CAG has asked for since 2010–11 — so the cess either funds roads in full or is honestly treated as general revenue.
  • The Budget's statement of revenue foregone should include an annual estimate of fuel excise and road cess not collected from electric vehicles, by segment, so the size of the gap is measured by the government before it becomes large, rather than by think tanks after.
  • The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways should use satellite tolling to pilot a per-kilometre road charge for heavy electric vehicles — buses and trucks — on national highways, set at roughly what a diesel vehicle pays in road cess per kilometre, while light EVs stay exempt during the adoption phase.
  • States should replace open-ended EV road-tax waivers with published phase-downs tied to EV share of new registrations, so owners and manufacturers know when concessions end, rather than facing an abrupt reversal like Karnataka's.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
