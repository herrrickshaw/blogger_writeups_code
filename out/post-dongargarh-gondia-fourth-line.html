On 27 August 2026, South East Central Railway notified its intention to acquire land in Goregaon Tehsil, Gondia District, Maharashtra, for the Dongargarh–Gondia Fourth Railway Line — an 84.10 km, ₹2,223 crore project designed to add 52 million tonnes a year of freight capacity on one of Indian Railways' busiest corridors. The notification itself is a legal instrument under the Railways Act, not a press release: it runs to a schedule of named landholders, survey numbers and land areas in a village called Tumkheda Bk., some of the acquisitions measured in fractions of a hectare. That gap — a headline project worth thousands of crores, executed via a document that itemises individual farm plots by name — is what land acquisition for infrastructure actually looks like at the point of contact.

Why this corridor, and why a fourth line

The Dongargarh–Gondia section sits on one of Indian Railways' heaviest-used freight corridors, carrying coal, steel and cement traffic from the coal-rich belt of central and eastern India westward and northward. A third line already exists; this fourth line is capacity augmentation on an artery that is, by the project's own justification, already running close to its limit. The stated addition — 52 million tonnes per annum — would carry coal, steel, iron ore, cement, ballast and stone chips, fly ash, fertiliser, limestone, manganese, dolomite, foodgrains and petroleum products, essentially the full bulk-freight basket that moves out of this belt.

What Section 20A actually authorises

The notification is issued under Section 20A(1) of the Railways Act, 1989 (as amended in 2008), the provision that lets the Central Government declare land acquisition for a "Special Railway Project" — a faster-track acquisition route than the general land acquisition law, built specifically for railway construction. Anyone with an interest in the listed land has 30 days from publication to file a written objection under Section 20D(1) with the Competent Authority and Sub-Divisional Officer (Revenue) at Tirora, Gondia District; the Competent Authority must hear the objector in person or through a legal representative before ruling, and that ruling is final.

The smallest line in the schedule. One listed acquisition, from survey number 396, is 0.0093 hectares — roughly 93 square metres, a strip smaller than a small house plot. It sits in the same legal document as a ₹2,223 crore, 84-kilometre project figure. Both numbers describe the same railway line.

The net read

National infrastructure numbers — crores of rupees, kilometres of track, million tonnes of annual capacity — are the register these projects get discussed in. The instrument that actually delivers them is a village-by-village, survey-number-by-survey-number legal notice, with a 30-day clock and a named revenue officer to write to. Both descriptions are accurate; neither is the whole picture without the other.
