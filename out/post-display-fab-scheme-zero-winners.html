In December 2021, India offered up to ₹12,000 crore to whichever of at most two companies built a large-panel display factory. Two real bidders showed up, proposing a combined $6.7 billion — and neither was ever approved. Four years, one scheme reopening and one Foxconn withdrawal later, the government's own tally of chip-and-display-fab wins now includes a "display fab" — but it is a ₹3,200 crore gallium-nitride micro-LED project approved under a completely different, much smaller category than the one Vedanta and Elest actually applied for.

India's Display Fab Scheme Got Two Billion-Dollar Bids and Approved Neither. Then It Redefined "Display Fab" Small Enough to Claim One Anyway.

What the 2021 scheme actually promised

The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology notified the "Scheme for Setting up of Display Fabs in India" on 21 December 2021. Its terms were specific and large: a company, consortium or joint venture proposing to build a Generation-8-or-above TFT-LCD fab, or a Generation-6-or-above AMOLED fab, in India — with capacity of at least 60,000 panels a month for TFT-LCD or 30,000 for AMOLED — could receive fiscal support of up to 50 per cent of project cost, capped at ₹12,000 crore, against a minimum capital investment of ₹10,000 crore and a minimum revenue threshold of ₹7,500 crore in any of the three years before applying. The scheme allowed a maximum of two beneficiaries, ran for a notional six-year tenure, was implemented through the newly created India Semiconductor Mission as nodal agency, and required Union Cabinet approval of any selected applicant. Applications opened 1 January 2022 for an initial 45-day window.

Two real bidders, zero approvals

This was never a scheme nobody tried. By the February 2022 deadline, two genuine applications had come in, together worth roughly $6.7 billion in proposed investment and seeking around $2.7 billion in combined incentives (spanning both the semiconductor and display categories): a Vedanta-led bid, initially structured as a joint venture with Foxconn, proposing a Generation-8.6 TFT-LCD fab plus AMOLED panel capacity at Dholera, Gujarat; and a bid from Elest, backed by Rajesh Exports, proposing a Generation-6 AMOLED fab publicly announced for Telangana in June 2022 at a stated ₹24,000 crore. Neither was ever approved. A Deccan Herald analysis of the scheme's first phase put it plainly: "None of the three companies that applied for silicon fabs or the two that applied for display fabs before the first deadline of February 15, 2022 qualified for it."

The two applications didn't fail cleanly, either — they simply stalled. Neither was formally rejected in any public order this piece could find; neither cleared the India Semiconductor Mission's technical and financial evaluation and negotiation stage. The Vedanta side of the story is the better documented one: Foxconn formally withdrew from its roughly $19.5 billion joint venture with Vedanta on 10-11 July 2023, ending the JV structure that had underpinned the original display-fab bid, with Vedanta stating it would continue alone. Elest's application is the harder one to trace — this piece found no confirmation, positive or negative, of its status after mid-2023 coverage of the scheme's reopening. It may still be technically pending, may have been quietly dropped, or may have lapsed when the scheme's terms changed; the public record simply goes quiet.

Reopened once, then swallowed by a much bigger scheme

The scheme did not sit static after its first 45-day window closed without a winner. MeitY reopened both the semiconductor and display fab schemes under a "Modified Semicon India Programme" from 1 June 2023, with revised guidelines for the display fab scheme specifically dated 29 May 2023; existing applicants, Vedanta included, were told they might need to update or resubmit their proposals under the new terms. That reopening still did not produce an approved display fab.

The bigger structural change came later. The Union Cabinet approved Semicon 2.0 on 15 July 2026, an outlay of ₹1,27,500 crore (roughly $13.25 billion) restructuring fiscal support across every category of India's chip and display ambitions, with detailed guidelines notified by MeitY on 31 August 2026. Under the new tiers, support for silicon-based semiconductor fabs was cut from 50 per cent to 40 per cent of project cost; support for compound semiconductor fabs and display fabs together was cut to up to 35 per cent — down from the uniform 50 per cent the original 2021 scheme offered; advanced packaging also sits at up to 35 per cent, conventional packaging at up to 25 per cent. The display-fab-specific thresholds moved too: a Generation-6-or-above OLED fab now needs a minimum ₹5,000 crore in revenue rather than the original ₹7,500 crore (capital investment threshold unchanged at ₹10,000 crore), while an entirely new, much smaller Micro-LED sub-category was created, with a minimum investment of just ₹1,500 crore and revenue of ₹600 crore.

What this piece could not directly verify. The exact clause wording and percentages for Semicon 2.0's 31 August 2026 notification are corroborated across five or more independent trade-press and legal-explainer sources, but this piece was not able to fetch and quote the primary PIB or MeitY document directly. Treat the specific support percentages above as well-sourced secondary reporting, not a verbatim reading of the gazette text.

The "display fab" the government now counts

By its own July 2026 tally, the India Semiconductor Mission has approved 12 projects with cumulative investment over ₹1.64 lakh crore — and PIB's own categorisation of that list includes, alongside one full silicon fab and one compound-semiconductor fab, one project labelled a "Gallium Nitride Micro LED Display Fab." That project is Crystal Matrix Ltd, Cabinet-approved in early May 2026 for a facility at Dholera, Gujarat, producing GaN micro-LED and mini-LED wafers and panels, at a stated investment of roughly ₹3,200 crore.

Crystal Matrix is a real approval, but it is not a match for what the 2021 scheme was actually built to attract. It was approved under the Modified Scheme for Compound Semiconductors/Silicon Photonics/Sensors Fabs, using the new, much smaller Micro-LED thresholds — ₹1,500 crore minimum investment, ₹600 crore minimum revenue — not the Generation-6-or-above AMOLED or Generation-8-or-above TFT-LCD large-panel category, with its ₹10,000 crore investment floor, that Vedanta and Elest were actually chasing. Coverage of the approval also flagged reason for caution about delivery: Angel One's reporting on the deal noted Crystal Matrix's own balance sheet, as of FY25, showed office equipment rather than fab or cleanroom infrastructure — a small company approved for a small, differently defined category, not evidence that India's original large-panel display fab ambition has been met.

The one-line version. Zero large-panel Generation-6-or-above AMOLED or Generation-8-or-above TFT-LCD display fabs — the actual target of the December 2021 scheme — have ever been Cabinet-approved. The one "display fab" now in the government's own count is a much smaller project, approved under a different rulebook created years later, for a technology category the original scheme never mentioned.

Where India Semiconductor Mission's real traction actually is

None of this means the India Semiconductor Mission has failed across the board — it means the failure is concentrated specifically in large-panel displays. Of its 12 approved projects as of September 2026, nine are packaging, assembly and testing (OSAT/ATMP) facilities, several already in commercial production: Micron Technology's unit at Sanand, Gujarat (Cabinet-approved June 2023, now producing commercially); Tata Semiconductor Assembly and Test at Morigaon, Assam (roughly ₹27,000 crore, approved 29 February 2024); CG Power's facility at Sanand with Japan's Renesas Electronics and Thailand's Stars Microelectronics (roughly ₹7,600 crore, approved the same day, commercial production beginning July 2026); Kaynes Semicon at Sanand, also in commercial production; plus Suchi Semicon (Surat), Continental Device India (Mohali), Advanced System in Package Technologies with South Korea's APACT (Andhra Pradesh), 3D Glass Solutions (Odisha) and an HCL-Foxconn facility at Jewar, Uttar Pradesh. One full silicon fab — Tata Electronics with Taiwan's PSMC, at Dholera — and one silicon-carbide compound-semiconductor fab, SiCSem in Odisha with the UK's Clas-SiC Wafer Fab, round out the twelve. This blog's own count of India's chip-fab scheme found a similar pattern of packaging and assembly moving faster than fabrication itself, on the semiconductor side specifically; the display-fab category shows the same lag, more starkly.

The one credible name still in the display-fab pipeline is Dixon Technologies, which received Indian government clearance in March 2026 — under Press Note 3 / FEMA security-screening rules for Chinese investment, not an ISM fiscal-incentive award — for a joint venture with China's HKC Overseas Ltd (Dixon holding 74 per cent, HKC 26 per cent) to manufacture LCD and TFT-LCD display modules in India, reportedly eyeing a roughly $3 billion capex display fab with an eye toward future Semicon 2.0 support. As of this piece's research, that JV has cleared a security-screening hurdle, not a Cabinet approval; it is a pipeline candidate, not a fourth data point alongside Vedanta, Elest and Crystal Matrix.

What would fix this

  • MeitY and PIB should report the India Semiconductor Mission's project tally with explicit sub-category labels, so a ₹3,200 crore Micro-LED project and a ₹10,000 crore-plus large-panel fab don't both simply read as "1 display fab" in a headline count.
  • The government should publish a current status for Elest's application — pending, withdrawn, or lapsed — rather than leaving a scheme applicant's fate untraceable in the public record three years on.
  • MeitY should explain, in its own words, why display-fab support fell from 50 per cent to 35 per cent while the revenue threshold also fell — whether that reflects a genuine reassessment of what's achievable at India's current stage, or simply an attempt to broaden eligibility after four years without a large-panel winner.
  • Any future evaluation of the Dixon-HKC joint venture, if it becomes a formal ISM applicant, should be conducted against clear, published criteria given the scheme's history of applicants stalling indefinitely in negotiation rather than being approved or publicly declined.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
