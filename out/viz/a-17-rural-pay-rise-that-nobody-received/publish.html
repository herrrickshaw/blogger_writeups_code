India’s reported rural wage growth jumped from a steady 6 per cent to 17 per cent, and the average daily wage crossed ₹500. No comparable change happened in any field. What changed was the sample: the Labour Bureau swapped in a new set of villages and never announced it. The same sample sets the index that decides what ten crore rural workers are legally paid.

Rural wages in India: a sample revision made growth look 3 times faster

Top line

  • 18.12% reported rural wage growth to March 2026 on the new sample, against a break-adjusted estimate of around 4.3%.
  • 11% of sample villages now come from regions that hold 1.15% of rural population.
Updated 27 October 2026. Two corrections. First, the March 2025 MGNREGA notification did not set “a national average of ₹370 a day”: in 2025-26 only Haryana (₹400), Goa (₹378) and Karnataka (₹370) paid that much or more, and the simple average across 17 major states was about ₹307. So the government’s ₹298.8 average is consistent with the state rates, and there is no second official figure to reconcile; the paragraph, proposal and caveat that treated ₹370 as one have been rewritten. Second, the June 2025 inflation figures were the wrong way round: year-on-year inflation was 1.73% for agricultural labourers (CPI-AL) and 1.42% for rural labourers (CPI-RL). The argument is unchanged, since either figure justifies almost no wage revision. The follow-up piece, Rural Job-Guarantee Pay Rose 9.6% in July, sets out the state rates.
Updated 26 October 2026. MoSPI’s data service now has September 2026 figures for the rural price indices discussed below: year-on-year inflation was 6.30% for CPI-AL (agricultural labourers) and 5.98% for CPI-RL (rural labourers), up from 5.18% and 4.83% in August. The preliminary May 2026 comparison between the newly added regions and the national average has not been updated, because no regional breakdown has been released since.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Reported rural wage growth jumped to 18.12% when the sample changed

What it means: Reported all-occupation wage growth rose after the Bureau changed its sample, while the break-adjusted estimate is far lower.

What to do: Publish an official back-series on the old sample so wage growth can be compared like for like.

Newly added regions supply 11% of sample villages but hold 1.15% of rural population

What it means: A national average built on this sample rises whether or not anyone is paid more.

What to do: Publish an official back-series that weights regions by population so the average is not driven by sample design.

Agricultural labourers' measured inflation rose from 1.73% to 6.30% by September 2026

What it means: The first CPI-AL print of 1.73% was the input a wage formula would have used, yet inflation later reached 6.30%.

What to do: Flag the sample break in the CPI-AL publication, which the post says still does not do so.

The replacement scheme's average wage is ₹327.4 a day, up from ₹298.8

What it means: The average wage under the replacement scheme is ₹28.6 a day higher, but states now pay most of the wage bill.

What to do: Resolve the 60:40 funding dispute, since Punjab and Telangana have asked for more central support.

What would fix this

  • The Labour Bureau should publish a back-cast of the rural wage series on the new 787-village, 34-state sampling frame, so the July 2025 break can be measured officially rather than by an outside analyst’s splice of roughly 4.3 per cent.
  • The Labour Bureau should print a dated break note on every wage-rate and CPI-AL/CPI-RL table issued since July 2025, stating that the sample changed from 600 to 787 villages, so the 17–18 per cent headline is not read as a pay rise.
  • The Labour Bureau should publish, for a full overlap year, CPI-AL computed with both arithmetic and geometric means, so the Ministry of Rural Development and states can see how much of the 1.73 per cent June 2025 CPI-AL inflation print comes from the averaging change alone.
  • The Ministry of Rural Development should state, in each wage notification under VB-G RAM G, the index series, base and weighting used to set the rate, and say whether its national average is simple or weighted, so figures such as ₹298.8 and ₹327.4 can be reproduced from the state rates.
  • The Labour Bureau should publish state-wise wage and price series with each state’s sample-village weight alongside its Census rural-population share, so users can re-weight the national average themselves when newly added regions hold about 11 per cent of villages but 1.15 per cent of rural population.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

The break-adjusted growth figure of about 4.3 per cent is a splice by an outside analyst, not an official statistic. The Labour Bureau has not published a back-cast series on the new sampling frame, which is what would settle the question properly; until it does, any estimate of the underlying trend is a reconstruction. The direction is well supported — the sample change is documented and its composition effect is arithmetic — but the precise magnitude is not.

Whether the government’s ₹298.8 national average is a simple or a weighted average of state rates is not published. The 62 per cent single-month jump in fishing wages and the 29 per cent in beedi-making are reported from the Bureau’s own tables; no occupation-level explanation has been offered for them.

Finally, VB-G RAM G is nine weeks old at the time of writing. Person-day figures in its first months are not yet a reliable guide to how it will run: reports of a sharp year-on-year fall sit alongside a month-on-month rise from 30 crore person-days approved in April 2026 to 43 crore in May, and the two are not necessarily in conflict. Judgement on the scheme should wait for a full season.

Sources

The CPI-AL and CPI-RL base revision from 1986-87 to 2019 = 100 — the expansion from 20 states and 600 villages to 34 states and union territories and 787 villages, the move from arithmetic to geometric mean, the COICOP-2018 classification, the increase from 65–106 to 150–200 priced items, and the shift to the NSS 68th Round (2011-12) consumption pattern — is per the Ministry of Labour and Employment and Labour Bureau announcements of the revised series.

That CPI-AL is used to determine wages under the central rural employment guarantee, and as a reference for minimum wages for agricultural and rural labour, is per the same source, which also notes their use for dearness allowance in government, public sector and private establishments.

The switch from arithmetic to geometric mean, the statement that inflation under the new base has “been moderate as compared to the old series”, and the June 2025 prints of 134 points with year-on-year inflation of 1.73 per cent (CPI-AL) and 1.42 per cent (CPI-RL), as given by MoSPI’s data service, are from the Ministry of Labour and Employment release of 18 July 2025 (PIB Release ID 2145905).

That the arithmetic mean of a set of unequal positive numbers always exceeds their geometric mean is the AM-GM inequality, not a source-dependent claim. The contrary expectation — that a modernised basket would produce more realistic and potentially higher MGNREGA wages — is as argued in policy commentary published in April 2025, before the first figures appeared.

The wage-series discontinuity and its decomposition — the ₹454 to ₹511 jump between June and July 2025, reported growth of 6.23 / 6.79 / 5.75 per cent to June 2025 against 18.12 / 20.16 / 16.40 per cent to March 2026, the ₹522 average, the 62 per cent and 29 per cent occupational moves, the roughly 11-percentage-point weight shift toward the north-eastern states, NCR and Goa, the 1.15 per cent of rural population against 11 per cent of sample villages, the 50–55 per cent wage differential, the 2.9 against 3.9 per cent inflation gap, and the break-adjusted estimate of about 4.3 per cent — are from Dhananjay Sinha’s AlphaEcon analysis “Rural India’s Wage ‘Boom’ That Wasn’t” (22 June 2026), which reports that the Bureau confirmed the sampling change to him directly and published no notice of it; the underlying base revision has been independently verified here against the Labour Bureau’s own published description.

The finding that MGNREGA’s 2006–08 rollout significantly raised real farm wages, with the effect growing over time, and that the Covid lockdown had no significant lasting effect, is from “Agricultural wages in India: trends and structural changes”, Agricultural Economics Research Review vol. 37 no. 1 (2024), using ARIMA-intervention modelling on gender-disaggregated wage data for harvesting, sowing and ploughing, 1995–2022.

VB-G RAM G details — presidential assent 21 December 2025, commencement 1 July 2026, 125 guaranteed days, the 305-day window with up to 60 reserved no-work days, the 40:60 and 10:90 cost-sharing, the ₹298.8 to ₹327.4 average wage and the ₹300 floor, payment within a week with interest on delay, projected state outlay rising from about ₹8,690 crore to ₹35,300 crore, and the ₹95,692 crore allocation against MGNREGA’s reduced ₹30,000 crore — are from the Act, PIB material on it and Budget 2026-27 reporting.

The March 2025 MGNREGA state wage rates for 2025-26 (Haryana ₹400, Goa ₹378, Karnataka ₹370, Nagaland and Arunachal Pradesh ₹241) are per the Ministry of Rural Development notification as reported; the 17-state simple average of about ₹307 is this blog’s arithmetic. PM-KISAN figures — ₹6,000 a year in three instalments, ₹4.47 lakh crore over 23 instalments, ₹18,680.63 crore to 9.34 crore farmers in the 21st instalment of November 2025, and the Cabinet extension to 2030-31 with a ₹3.15 lakh crore outlay — are from PM-KISAN and Cabinet announcements.

DBT aggregates — 314 schemes across 53 ministries, coverage up from 11 crore to 176 crore beneficiaries, ₹3.48 lakh crore of claimed savings and the fall in subsidy spending from 16 to 9 per cent of expenditure — are per Government of India DBT reporting; the savings figure is a government estimate of avoided leakage and is not independently audited here.
