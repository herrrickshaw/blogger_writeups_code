India’s third-phase fuel-efficiency rules for cars, notified on 30 September 2026, borrow almost every tool used in Europe, the US, China, Japan and Brazil: weight-based targets, credits for efficiency technologies, trading between carmakers and a price for falling short. Where they differ is generosity. Each battery EV counts as three cars, a bigger multiplier than any other major market has used, with no step-down reported; every petrol car running on E20 gets an 8% discount; and flex-fuel cars get the kind of ethanol credit the US dropped because owners rarely bought the fuel. On a like-for-like test cycle the FY2032 target is about 97 g/km, close to Europe’s current level — but with 10% EV sales, the credits would let the fleet’s actual tailpipe emissions stay near today’s.

India's car efficiency rules: each battery EV counts as 3 cars, above other markets

Top line

  • 3× EV multiplier, the largest in any major market: at a 5% EV share it cuts a fleet's counted average by 9.5 g/km.
  • 95.5 → 127.4 g/km allowed for petrol cars as the EV share goes from 0% to 10%, so each EV loosens the petrol limit beside it.
  • 8% discount on declared CO2 for petrol cars on E20 or higher, so a car tested at 103 g/km counts as 94.8.

What would fix this

  • The Bureau of Energy Efficiency should publish each manufacturer’s annual CAFE result: its fleet average with and without super-credits, Carbon Neutrality Factors and technology credits, plus credits traded and bought out and at what price, so the public can see how much of compliance is real efficiency.
  • The Ministry of Power should cap the total benefit any manufacturer can take from super-credits, as the EU did at 7.5 g/km, and step the multipliers down between the first and second compliance blocks, as China did.
  • The Ministry of Power and the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should tie the 22.3% flex-fuel factor to the volume of high-ethanol fuel actually sold, through oil-marketing-company sales data, so that the credit does not repeat the US flex-fuel experience.
  • BEE should publish the gap between MIDC and WLTP results for each model from the April 2027 WLTP reporting, and start collecting real-world fuel-consumption data from on-board meters as the EU does, so that CAFE-IV targets can be set on the WLTP cycle used by the EU, Japan and China and checked against how cars are actually driven.
  • The Ministry of Power should publish the outcome of the CAFE-II FY2023 penalty proceedings against the eight manufacturers, so that the credibility of CAFE-III’s buy-out rests on a known record.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Sources and caveats

CAFE-III details are from press reports of the notification: The Hindu, “Centre notifies new CAFE norms, tightens fuel-efficiency targets for passenger vehicles” (30 September 2026); The Economic Times, “CAFE III fuel efficiency norms notified for cars: 3x credit for battery EVs, no special benefit for small cars”; and Autocar Professional, “Government notifies CAFE III norms”. This blog has not read the gazette notification itself; whether super-credits are capped, how the weight line is defined and whether the buy-out is charged per vehicle should be checked against it. The g/km and L/100 km figures convert with the notified petrol factor, 0.04217 × CO2. EU rules: Regulation (EU) 2019/631 and amendments as summarised by the European Commission and the ICCT; the 2025–27 averaging amendment (Council, 27 May 2025); the December 2025 Automotive Package proposals. US: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (July 2025) as summarised by Sidley Austin; EPA’s rescission of the endangerment finding (February 2026); NHTSA’s SAFE III final rule for MY2022–31 (28 September 2026; Federal Register, 30 September 2026); the ICCT’s summary of the EPA MY2023–26 rule for multipliers; NHTSA’s report to Congress on the Alternative Motor Fuels Act. China: DieselNet and ICCT summaries of the CAFC standards and the dual-credit policy. Japan: ICCT, “Japan 2030 fuel economy standards” (2019). Korea: press reports of the 2026 amendment. Brazil: ICCT policy update on MOVER (2025). CAFE-II penalty figures are from press reports of November–December 2024 and were disputed by the companies. The fleet example (95 petrol cars at 110 g/km plus 5 EVs) and the exchange-rate comparison are this blog’s illustrations. Several sources were read through search extracts rather than full text. The cycle conversions use ICCT’s MIDC-to-WLTP factor of 1.226 (as applied in its 2024 working paper on Indian light commercial vehicles, citing Suarez and co-authors, 2023) and ICCT’s 2014 white paper on test-cycle conversion factors; China’s 2025 WLTC figure and Korea’s earlier trajectory are from ICCT’s 2023 overview of global standards. India’s FY2021 fleet average of 121.3 g/km is from ICCT’s December 2021 working paper on new passenger cars. Real-world gaps are from the European Commission’s first report on on-board fuel-consumption monitoring (March 2024) and ICCT’s “From laboratory to road” series. All WLTP-equivalent figures and the flexibility table are this blog’s approximations; they are meant to show orders of magnitude, not exact equivalence.
