- India leads the world in coconut count (PIB puts its share at 31.24% of global production), but Indonesia and the Philippines out-produce it by weight — Indian nuts run smaller, so the two rankings tell different stories and get conflated constantly.
- The real opportunity isn't new land, it's the yield gap: Kerala has the most area under coconut of any state but the lowest productivity of the big four (~9,664 nuts/ha), while Tamil Nadu, with less land, out-yields it by roughly 40% (~13,775 nuts/ha).

## Largest producer by count, not by weight — and that distinction matters

India is routinely described as the world's largest coconut producer, and PIB backs that up with a specific figure: a 31.24% share of global production, against a smaller 17.90% share of global area. But that ranking is by nut count. By tonnage, Indonesia (roughly 17–19 million tonnes) and the Philippines (roughly 15 million tonnes) both out-produce India, because the Indian coconut is on average smaller than the Indonesian or Philippine nut. Both statements are true at once, and conflating them is the single most common error in coverage of this sector — India is the volume leader in the unit that actually matters to a farmer (nuts sold), not the leader in raw biomass.

## The yield gap: where the real opportunity sits

The area-versus-productivity split across the big four producing states is the clearest evidence that expansion, not just new planting, is where the gains are.

Tamil Nadu farms less coconut land than Kerala and produces almost as much fruit as Karnataka, the national leader. That gap between the best and worst performing major state is the opportunity, not any unfarmed acreage.

The Coconut Development Board has effectively confirmed this reading with its wallet: on World Coconut Day 2025 it raised its national area-expansion grant from ₹6,500 to ₹56,000 per hectare — an eightfold jump, and the most direct evidence available that the Board has concluded the productivity problem needs money, not just extension advice.

This article's own analysis of the DGCI&S 8-digit trade series used elsewhere on this blog, FY2025-26. Net surplus roughly $100mn across the eleven coconut-related HS codes traced.

The overall number is a genuine net surplus — India sells more coconut, copra, coconut oil and shell products abroad than it buys. But two lines run backwards and both point at the same underlying gap: India imports $14.67mn of raw copra and $56.83mn of coconut/copra oil-cake and meal combined, despite being the world's largest coconut producer by nut count. Coconut oil-cake is the residue left after oil extraction, used as livestock feed and fertiliser — importing it in volume while exporting $85.6mn of the crude-and-refined oil it comes from is consistent with a processing chain that extracts oil efficiently but doesn't fully capture or price the downstream residue market domestically. Coconut-shell charcoal runs backwards for a different reason: India imports $25.9mn of it, more than double its $11.6mn of exports, because shell charcoal is the raw material for coconut-shell activated carbon. India exports that in far larger amounts — reportedly ₹4,689 crore in 2025-26 — and it falls outside the eleven codes traced here.

## What the Coconut Development Board is actually funding

CDB runs several distinct programmes, not one undifferentiated "coconut scheme" — each targets a different point of failure in the value chain.


[T3 "What the Coconut Development Board is actually funding" 7 rows; use rows by number in a chart "table" spec]
  cols: c0 Scheme | c1 What it targets | c2 Mechanism
  r0: New Coconut Promotion Scheme (Union Budget 2026-27) | Replanting, seedling scale-up, integrated processing capacity | Part of a ₹350 crore allocation shared with cashew and cocoa; area-expansion subsidy raised roughly eightfold (from ~$73.63 to ~$634/ha equivalent) and seedling subsidy raised nearly sixfold (~$0.091 to ~$0.51/seedling); explicit ambition to make India a global coconut and coir export leader by the 2030s
  r1: Replanting & Rejuvenation Scheme | Senile, disease-affected and unproductive old palms — a widely cited structural drag on national yield | ₹54,000/hectare assistance over two years, plus ₹13,000/palm compensation for removing old palms; covered 25,517 hectares at ₹130.46 crore over five years; 2025-26 target 1,672 hectares
  r2: Coconut Palm Insurance Scheme (CPIS) | Crop-loss risk on individual palms | Premium split roughly 50% CDB / 25% state / 25% farmer (or 10% farmer if the state opts out); sum insured ₹600 per palm (age 4–15) rising to ₹1,150 per palm (age 16–60); farmer premium ₹2.25–3.50/palm/year
  r3: Productivity Improvement through Coconut-based Cropping System (PICCS) | Under-monetised inter-row land in existing coconut gardens | ₹42,000/hectare to establish multi-species intercropping within standing coconut plantations
  r4: Producer Society → Federation → Company structure | Fragmented small holdings with no collective bargaining power | Coconut Producer Societies (40–100 farmers, 4,000–6,000 palms) aggregate into Coconut Producer Federations (8–10 societies, ~100,000 palms), which can incorporate as companies — CDB's own stated structure for building scale out of smallholder plots
  r5: Neera (coconut sap) promotion | A high-value product India under-monetises relative to its raw-nut sales | State-level tapping-licence and excise reform, not a CDB subsidy directly — Kerala runs 204 tapping licences under its "Kalparasa" branding against Tamil Nadu's 13; Karnataka is separately moving to amend its Excise Act to declassify Neera as an unfermented product
  r6: Cocomitra / Friends of Coconut Tree (FoCT) | The palm-climbing labour shortage that is a repeatedly cited driver of neglected, unharvested and eventually senile palms | Trained-climber and mechanical-climbing-device programmes

## What to watch

- Whether CDB's Statistics.aspx dataset becomes directly fetchable — this article's own attempt hit a search interface rather than the underlying state-wise table, and a full primary-sourced state breakdown (including Andhra Pradesh's actual area and the smaller producing states' area figures) would meaningfully sharpen the yield-gap analysis above.
- Whether the new eightfold jump in the area-expansion subsidy actually moves new planting, or whether ageing-palm replacement remains the binding constraint regardless of the subsidy on offer for genuinely new area.
- Whether the coconut oil-cake and copra import lines shrink as any new processing capacity comes online under the 2026-27 scheme — that is the most directly falsifiable claim in this piece, checkable against next year's trade data on this blog's own dataset.
- Whether Karnataka's Neera excise reform passes, and whether it measurably changes tapping-licence counts the way Kerala's earlier reform did.
