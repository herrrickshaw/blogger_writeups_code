Brazil has sold ethanol-blended petrol at every pump in the country, without exception, since 1938. Since 1 August 2026 that blend has been 32%, raised from 30% for an initial six months and the highest mandatory rate anywhere, and a separate pump at the same station sells pure ethanol for the 82% of the light-vehicle fleet that can run on it. India crossed 20% nationwide in April 2026 and has already published the standards for 22-30%. Brazil's fuel policy gets most of the attention; the less-told half of the story is what had to change inside the dispenser itself — the seals, the metering, the certification — before any of those blend numbers could reach a tank safely.

Top line

  • 32% is Brazil's mandatory ethanol share in petrol since 1 August 2026, the highest mandatory rate anywhere, and a temporary one.
  • 82% of Brazil's light-vehicle fleet is flex-fuel by stock, so drivers can pick pure ethanol at the same station.
  • 20% is India's nationwide ethanol share since April 2026, with standards already published up to 30%.
Updated 7 November 2026. Brazil raised its blend from 30% to a temporary 32% (E32) on 1 August 2026, for an initial 180 days, with E35 under test and a legal challenge pending; the text and table now say so, while the headline still refers to E30. For India, the post adds the reported May 2026 instruction to prepare pumps for E22–E30 and the excise waiver on those blends; no pump rollout date has been set.

Brazil's ethanol share has ranged from 18% to 32%, and 32% is temporary

What it means: Brazil has mandated ethanol in petrol since 1938, and the 2024 law let the regulator move the blend within 22-35%.

What to do: The E32 decision should wait for the E35 technical tests the post says are still pending.

Dispensers needed certification at each step, not just a blend decision

  1. 1938Ethanol blending in petrol made mandatory
  2. 1975Proálcool decree 76.593 responds to the 1973 oil shock
  3. 2003VW Gol 1.6 Total Flex launches as the first mass-market flex-fuel car
  4. 2022Portaria INMETRO 227/2022 updates dispenser rules last set in 1985
  5. 2026E20 becomes the only petrol sold nationwide in India

What it means: Ethanol attacks seals and elastomers more than metal, so dispensers need metrology and UL certification before a blend is safe to sell.

What to do: Publish the outlet-level list of ethanol-compatible seal retrofits, as the post proposes for India's oil marketing companies.

India's E20 is behind Brazil's 32%, but its standards already reach E30

What it means: India's dispensers are at an earlier stage than Brazil's, which built its certification regime over five decades.

What to do: BIS and Legal Metrology should publish a materials test standard and a searchable meter-approval register, as the post proposes.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should publish the aggregate cost of the E20 dispenser retrofit by oil marketing company, with the number of outlets completed, so that the reconciled cost figure this post could not find exists before the same exercise is repeated for E22 to E30.
  • The oil marketing companies should publish a station-level list showing which retail outlets have completed ethanol-compatible seal and component retrofits, and which blends each outlet is certified to dispense, so that the pump labels required by IS 19850:2026 can be checked against the equipment behind them.
  • The Bureau of Indian Standards, with the Petroleum and Explosives Safety Organisation, should notify a materials-compatibility test standard for fuel dispensers handling E22 to E30, covering elastomers, seals, meters and filters, so that India has the equipment-side equivalent of the fuel specification it has already notified.
  • The Department of Consumer Affairs' Legal Metrology division should publish its list of dispenser meter models approved for each ethanol blend in a searchable public register, so that a buyer or inspector can verify a pump's approval directly, as this post advises readers to do with Inmetro's registry in Brazil.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
