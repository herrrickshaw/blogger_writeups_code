An open cooling tower uses about two litres of water per kWh of computing; a closed dry loop uses almost none but costs more power. Most of India’s big new campuses sit on the coast near subsea cables, where seawater changes the answer.

Top line

  • 88% less water per kWh of IT load for dry cooling in the worst case, counting the coal plant behind the grid.
  • 0.135 kWh extra electricity per kWh of IT load, the price of dry cooling when compressors run every hour.
  • 29 km from a cable landing for four of the campuses; the other three are 478 km or more away.
Updated 7 November 2026. Maharashtra’s committee under Anil Kakodkar has been given three months to report on data centres’ water, power, heat and noise impacts (Telangana Today), so its findings are due around January 2027. The water question has sharpened since: the state has declared drought in 265 of its 358 talukas, and opposition parties have asked that new data-centre water allocations, including Amazon’s Thane site, wait for the committee (ThePrint). What it means: Counting the power plant's water, dry cooling uses about 0.27 litres per kWh against about 2.2 for an evaporative tower.

What to do: Use dry cooling where water is scarce, and set its design temperature high enough to avoid compressors on most hours.

The price of dry cooling is electricity, and the worst case is avoidable

What it means: Dry cooling with compressors on every hour adds 0.135 kWh per kWh of IT load, which is the worst case the post costs out.

What to do: Keep closed-loop air-cooled chillers in existing halls and raise supply temperatures rather than converting to towers.

What it means: Coastal sites can cool a closed inner loop through seawater, which the post calls the efficient answer on the coast.

What to do: Build coastal campuses with closed inner loops, and never cool with municipal drinking water.

What would fix this

  • States hosting data centres should require annual reporting of Water Usage Effectiveness under IS/ISO/IEC 30134-9, split by source (potable, groundwater, treated sewage, seawater, desalinated), together with PUE, for every site above 5 MW.
  • “Closed loop” and “zero water” claims in project approvals should state how heat leaves the site, not only how it leaves the rack.
  • Coastal states should set out a clearance route for seawater intake and outfall for data centres, with a discharge temperature limit and monitoring, so that seawater cooling is a practical alternative to municipal water.
  • Maharashtra’s Kakodkar committee, due to report within three months of its October order, should publish the water allocation for each data-centre hub and the source it comes from.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

No Indian operator publishes site-level water or cooling energy, so the comparison rests on physics and code-minimum efficiencies rather than measured plants; real towers and chillers can do better than the code minimums. Hourly weather data for Indian cities could not be obtained for this piece, so it does not estimate how many hours a year dry coolers could run without compressors in each city. The Rs 30–49 desalination and Rs 45 treated-sewage costs are older Chennai estimates, and the Rs 7 electricity tariff is an assumption. Maharashtra’s 4,040 MW Navi Mumbai figure and the water demand attached to it come from a syndicated report of a state assessment that could not be opened. Several sources, including government releases, could not be opened directly and were read through search-engine text and news reports.
