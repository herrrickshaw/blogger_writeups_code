The armed forces’ quantum framework, released in January 2026, covers communication, computing, sensing and materials. The Army has bought quantum key distribution and is rolling out post-quantum encryption, but computing is a 6-qubit lab processor, sensing is prototype contracts, and the first Indian ultra-cold refrigerator for quantum computers is due only in late 2027.

Top line

  • 12% of the ₹6,003.65 crore mission outlay had reached institutions by February 2026, as reported.
  • 1 of the 4 pillars has equipment bought: secure communication, through a 2024 Army contract.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Only about 12% of the mission outlay had reached institutions by February 2026

What it means: Releases of ₹699.50 crore against ₹2,330.68 crore sanctioned show that about 12% of the ₹6,003.65 crore outlay had reached institutions by February 2026.

What to do: Publish a year-by-year table of releases and spending from the Department of Science and Technology.

Secure communication is the one pillar the forces have bought

  1. Aug 2022Ministry of Defence says it will adopt QNu Labs’ 150 km QKD system
  2. 30 Sep 2024Army signs a procurement contract for QNu Labs’ QKD system
  3. Jun 2025Army says post-quantum cryptography is being integrated across its networks
  4. Jul 2026DRDO completes military field trials of a fibre QKD system with Taqbit Labs

What it means: Secure communication is the only pillar with equipment on contract and software being rolled out, while computing, sensing and materials remain at laboratory, prototype or import stage.

What to do: Use QKD for fixed links between headquarters and rely on post-quantum cryptography for wider networks, as the post argues.

What would fix this

  • Headquarters Integrated Defence Staff should publish an unclassified summary of the Military Quantum Mission Policy Framework: the milestones by pillar and year, and which service or agency owns each, so progress can be followed without disclosing capabilities.
  • The Department of Science and Technology should publish National Quantum Mission allocations, releases and spending by year and by hub, in one table each year, so the gap between the ₹6,003.65 crore outlay and actual spending is not pieced together from press reports.
  • The National Security Council Secretariat and CERT-In should set a dated plan for moving government and critical-infrastructure networks to post-quantum cryptography, as the Army has begun to do for its own networks.
  • DRDO should report the milestones of its quantum Technology Development Fund projects (the dilution refrigerator, the gyroscope and the RF sensor) against their contracted dates, so the move from imported to Indian hardware can be tracked.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
