India's lysine imports: $106 million a year, unit price down 18.7% in FY26

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • $102 million of India's $106 million lysine imports in FY26 was a trade deficit, and no Indian lysine plant was found.
  • -18.7% fall in lysine's unit import price in FY26, while volume fell only 4%.
Updated 12 October 2026. Two newer points. First, there is now one indicative price: an aggregator quotes lysine at $870 a tonne FOB China in July 2026, down from $930 in June, against $1,827 in Germany. Second, the EU’s duties on Chinese lysine, in force since 12 July 2025, are under a re-opened inquiry, and on 29 July 2026 the Commission began registering Chinese imports so that duties could be applied retroactively. Inferences from these are marked as this blog’s own in the text below.
Updated 7 November 2026. The United States has now shut its market to most Chinese lysine as well. On 21 July 2026 the US Department of Commerce issued final determinations on feed-grade L-lysine from China, with dumping margins of 73.55% to 139.83%; its countervailing-duty order, published on 16 September 2026, adds subsidy rates of 48.21% for most exporters and up to 82.11%, with cash deposits from 8 September. In the EU, the inquiry mentioned above is an absorption reinvestigation opened on 30 April 2026 at Eurolysine’s request, on top of duties of 47.7% to 58.3%; no outcome has been published. With both large Western markets closing, more Chinese lysine is likely to look for buyers in Asia, including India. That is this blog’s inference, and it fits the falling import price below; it also makes an Indian plant’s price problem harder, not easier.

What to do: Assume any Indian entrant would compete with plants of much larger scale, as the post notes.

What would fix this

  • The Department of Commerce should publish amino-acid imports by origin, tonnage and unit price at the eight-digit line, including threonine, tryptophan and valine, so India’s dependence on Chinese supply is measured and not inferred from a vendor report.
  • The Department of Animal Husbandry and Dairying should publish feed-additive amino-acid use by acid, so demand for lysine is a measured number and not an estimate built from imports.
  • The Department of Biotechnology should fund an independent techno-economic study of a molasses-based lysine plant in India, with yield, oxygen-transfer and downstream costs stated, and publish it with the strain-licence question answered.
  • The Department of Food and Public Distribution should publish molasses output and allocation by state after ethanol diversion, so a feedstock claim for any amino-acid plant can be checked against what is actually left.
  • The Directorate General of Trade Remedies should publish landed unit prices of lysine imports by origin, so a domestic investor can see whether import prices cover the cost of the plants that supply them.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Sources and caveats

India trade: this blog’s chemical import-substitution registry (HS-8 lines 29304000, 29224100, 29224220, 29224910, 29224290, 29309014, 29225015, 29224210; FY26 imports, deficit, value and price change from DGCI&S data; 827 qualifying lines). Processes: a study extract on the microbial production of seven amino acids (C. glutamicum for lysine, molasses, starch hydrolysate or sucrose as carbon source, 40–50 g lysine HCl per 100 g, 50% liquid and 98–99% crystal grades, enzymatic aspartate from fumarate, chemical routes for glycine and DL-amino acids); Ajinomoto’s page on how amino acids are made (four routes, molasses in the medium, the 1960s shift of glutamate to fermentation); a vendor blog from a Dubai equipment trader (scale-up issues; its unsourced figures were not relied on, and the claim of up to 50% yield loss from contamination is reported as a vendor claim); a US organic-standards review of L-cysteine (keratin hydrolysis route). Market: an industry report on feed amino acids (China 76% of lysine and 93% of threonine production in 2023), a second report (China operating capacity 4.31 million tonnes, over 78.9%), reports on Meihua (1 million tonnes, 26%); an India lysine market report (89% Chinese share of import value; vendor product, method not stated); a 2026 Asia feed-ingredients market update; Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/1330 of 10 July 2025 as summarised in trade press (the rates differ slightly between summaries); an MLex report of 29 July 2026 on registration of Chinese lysine imports; a procurement-data aggregator’s July 2026 price page (indicative, unverified) and a 2026 China amino-acids report (Fufeng’s Kazakhstan base). Plant scale: an IMARC plant-setup estimate (10,000–50,000 t a year; ₹50–1,500+ crore; prices at historic lows in 2025) and a 2017 Universitas Gadjah Mada thesis design (25,000 t a year of lysine HCl from molasses). The 50,000–62,500 t sugar figure and the 0.2% share are this blog’s arithmetic from the 40–50 g per 100 g yield and 28 million tonnes of sugar eaten; molasses carbon and white sugar are not the same thing, so the share is an order of magnitude. Most sources were abstracts, vendor pages or search summaries, not full texts. No Indian lysine plant or announcement was found, which is a limit of the search. No process or investment advice is given.
