A parliamentary committee has restated the 15% target. Gas’s share of primary energy slipped from 7.5% to 7.0% over the decade to 2023-24, and reaching 15% would take almost three times today’s gas, nearly all of it imported LNG.

Gas in India's energy mix: share down from 7.5% to 7.0%, against a 15% target

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 190 bcm of gas a 15% share would need in 2029-30, 2.8 times what India used in 2025-26.
  • 7.0% gas share of primary energy in 2023-24, down from 7.5% in 2014-15.
  • 10.5% average load of India's gas power plants in 2025-26.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Gas's share of India's energy has slipped, not risen, since 2014-15

What it means: Gas use grew more slowly than total energy supply, so its share slipped over the decade.

What to do: Publish the yearly path the target implies, so progress against 15% can be measured.

A 15% share by 2029-30 would need 2.8 times the gas India used in 2025-26

What it means: Almost all of the extra gas would have to be imported as LNG, at about 112 million tonnes a year.

What to do: Test the target against an import plan that India can actually finance.

Idle gas power plants are a small lever, running at about a tenth of capacity

What it means: Taking the fleet back to its 2017-18 load would add only about 1.0 percentage points to gas's share.

What to do: Use a flexible allocation framework so idle plants get gas at prices they can burn.

Terminals are not the bottleneck; pipelines and gas prices are

What it means: The constraint is pipeline capacity to move gas inland and the price of the gas, not the tanks.

What to do: Prefer more pipeline out of existing terminals over new terminals.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should restate the 15% target with a published path: consumption, domestic output and LNG imports for each year to 2030, on the same basis as MoSPI’s energy balance, as the committee’s yearly action plan implies.
  • Gas policy should be judged against PNGRB’s own 2030 demand range, which implies about 7–9% of primary energy, rather than a 15% figure no forecaster expects.
  • The flexible allocation for gas power should be tied to peak hours, where higher prices can be paid, and its effect reported as gas burned and hours run each month.
  • Pipeline capacity out of the under-used terminals should take priority over approving new ones.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

The committee’s report was read through PRS Legislative Research’s summary, not the report itself. The energy shares use MoSPI’s energy balance, which ends in 2023-24; the 2025-26 share is an estimate that converts PPAC’s volume into energy using the 2023-24 ratio of 38.4 PJ per bcm. The same ratio for 2015-16 is 43.2, so the two sources do not convert consistently, and the estimate could be off by several tenths of a point. Primary energy growth to 2029-30 is assumed, not forecast. The 2024-25 consumption figure is derived from PPAC’s reported fall in 2025-26. Gas-power capacity is 25 GW in industry statements but about 20 GW in the CEA’s monitored-station reports, and the extra gas needed to raise the fleet’s load is scaled from PPAC’s power-sector consumption rather than modelled plant by plant. Full-year LNG import figures for 2025-26 were not found.
