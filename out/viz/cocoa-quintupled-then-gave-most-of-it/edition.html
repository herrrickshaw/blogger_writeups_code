Cocoa went from about US$2,400 a tonne in 2022 to a record near US$12,900 in December 2024, then gave most of it back. Vanilla's average export price fell roughly 90% between 2019 and late 2025. Both crops grow in small patches of the tropics, and both are routinely described as climate-change stories. The evidence supports that for cocoa's spike, only partly. For vanilla's crash it barely applies at all — and India, which grows a little of each, has already lived through one of these cycles and is planting into the next.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Cocoa's New York record was 5.5 times the 2022 average, then prices unwound

What it means: High prices cut demand and the 2024/25 crop recovered, so the 2023/24 shortage of 494,000 tonnes had already turned into a small surplus.

What to do: Treat the 2026 price rebound as unconfirmed, since ICCO has temporarily withheld 2025/26 production and grindings data.

Madagascar's vanilla export price fell about 90% between 2019 and 2025

What it means: Exports were held back by a minimum price that was scrapped in May 2023, and the unsold backlog then flooded the market.

What to do: Use the central bank series for current prices, since trade quotes for cured Grade A beans run several times higher than its US$38 average.

India's cocoa-bean import bill tripled in 2024 while tonnes rose 39%

What it means: Because the price is set in London and New York, an Indian chocolate maker pays the West African shortage price whatever the origin of its beans.

What to do: Plan any expansion of Indian cocoa around today's lower prices, since farm-gate prices have already fallen by about half.

What would fix this

  • The Directorate of Cashewnut and Cocoa Development should publish state-wise cocoa area, production and a monthly farmgate price series on its own website, updated each season. Today the state table surfaces mainly through parliamentary answers and farm prices only through press reports, which makes it impossible for a grower to judge whether new planting makes sense.
  • Andhra Pradesh's Horticulture Department should tie its annual cocoa area-expansion target to contracted offtake volumes and publish both, so that new planting tracks demand buyers have committed to (such as the Mondelez purchase arrangement) rather than the 2024 price peak — the mistake India made with vanilla in 2003–05.
  • The Spices Board should restore vanilla to its published area, production and export tables and update its vanilla crop page, which still describes an area of about 1,000 hectares, so farmers can see that the crop is now a 44-tonne net import rather than the export opportunity it was sold as two decades ago.
  • ICAR's Central Plantation Crops Research Institute and Kerala Agricultural University should publish multi-season yield and bean-quality results for the cocoa varieties they distribute, measured under heat and drought stress, since heat and disease drove West Africa's shortfall and India's expansion is aimed at the same buyers.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
