India's National Green Hydrogen Mission targets a production cost of $1.50 per kilogram by 2030. The first competitively bid supply contracts under its own SIGHT scheme — to Indian Oil and to Bharat Petroleum/Hindustan Petroleum's refineries — came in at $4.11–4.21 per kilogram, roughly two-and-a-half to three times the target. The standard methodology for calculating the levelised cost of hydrogen explains exactly which levers have to move, and by how much, to close a gap that size — and why the number quoted in any headline rarely tells you whether it's the cost of producing hydrogen or the cost of actually getting it to a buyer.

Top line

  • $4.11–4.21/kg is what India's first competitively bid green hydrogen contracts (SIGHT) cost to produce, against a $1.50/kg target for 2030.
  • $0.50/kg is roughly how much a 10% cut in the electricity price feeding an electrolyser lowers its hydrogen cost, per one industry overview.
Updated 22 October 2026. This post compares SIGHT bids of $4.21/kg (₹397) for IOCL and $4.11/kg (₹387) for BPCL/HPCL with the $1.50/kg 2030 target. Those rupee figures appear to include 18% GST. Excluding tax, the IOCL Panipat award was reported at ₹336/kg and the HPCL Visakhapatnam award at ₹328/kg. A later refinery tender, for Numaligarh, closed in February 2026, before this post, at a reported record low of ₹279/kg (about $3.08/kg) excluding taxes. A BPCL–Sembcorp joint venture won it, according to SolarQuarter. That is about twice the target, not two-and-a-half to three times. The gap is narrower but still large. What it means: Both bids for India's first green hydrogen supply contracts are above $4 per kg, roughly 2.7 times the mission's own 2030 cost target.

What to do: Read every headline India hydrogen price against the $1.50/kg target and state whether it is a plant-gate or a delivered figure.

Electrolysers are projected to learn far more slowly than solar PV

What it means: Electrolyser costs are expected to fall much more slowly per doubling of deployment than solar panel costs did, so closing the gap will take longer.

What to do: Set electrolyser cost targets on a 16 to 21% learning rate, not solar's 36%.

Waiving open-access charges is the largest policy lever at $0.48/kg

What it means: Waiving open-access charges is the largest single lever in the financiers' policy-plus-market stack, ahead of electrolyser and renewable cost reductions.

What to do: Prioritise open-access and power-cost measures in policy, since they move the cost most in the report's model.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of New and Renewable Energy should publish the assumptions behind the $1.50/kg 2030 target — electrolyser CAPEX, electricity price, utilisation, discount rate, and whether it is a plant-gate or delivered figure with or without incentives — so that progress is measured against a defined number, as the Agora/Umlaut methodology recommends.
  • SECI, as SIGHT implementing agency, should publish for each awarded supply contract the electrolyser technology, assumed utilisation, renewable power source and electricity tariff behind the bid, so that the $4.11–4.21/kg prices can be decomposed into the levers this post could not separate.
  • The National Green Hydrogen Mission Secretariat should publish annually the hydrogen actually produced by SIGHT-supported plants and the electrolyser utilisation they achieved, so that the lever this post identifies as most important is tracked from operating data rather than modelled.
  • The National Green Hydrogen Mission Secretariat should report each year how much of the estimated ₹5.05 lakh crore in state and central support has actually been claimed, by state and by component, so that the incentive layer beneath the Mission's ₹19,744 crore budget is measured as realised support, not a policy-duration ceiling.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. This piece supplies the standard LCOH methodology and the two verified India data points (the $1.50/kg 2030 target and the $4.11–4.21/kg current SIGHT bid prices) rather than reconstructing a specific Indian project's LCOH bottom-up; it does not have India-specific electrolyser CAPEX, actual achieved utilisation rates, or delivered electricity tariffs for the projects behind those SIGHT bids, so it cannot say precisely how much of the 2.7–2.8x gap is utilisation, how much is electricity price, and how much is electrolyser capital cost. The storage, pipeline and shipping cost figures are drawn from a World Bank educational presentation explicitly marked draft and illustrative, built mainly around intercontinental and Gulf-to-Europe case studies; they are not India-specific delivered-cost estimates and should be treated as illustrating the general shape of the production-vs-delivered distinction, not as numbers applicable to any particular Indian hydrogen hub. The two CEEW reports cited in Section 5 model a single state (Gujarat) as their base case for the LCOH cost build-up and policy-impact waterfall; the ₹5.05 lakh crore state-and-central support figure is an unannualised, policy-duration aggregate, not a committed spend, and CEEW's own methodology notes it does not project what share of that potential support will actually be claimed.
