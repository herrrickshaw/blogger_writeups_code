Between March and August 2025, Parliament repealed and replaced four shipping laws in one sitting after another — the oldest of them 169 years old — in one of the most complete legal overhauls of any Indian sector in decades. None of it changes the number that actually matters: Indian-flagged ships still carry only about 5% of the country's own overseas trade.

Top line

  • ~5% of India's overseas trade is carried on Indian-flagged ships, while about $75bn a year is paid to foreign lines.
  • 16–20% extra cost to operate a ship under the Indian flag, a gap the four new Acts do not close.
Updated 7 November 2026. Corrected India’s share of world tonnage: 14.02 million GT is under 1% of the world fleet, not about 2% (India Shipping News). The fleet passed 14.2 million GT after 92 vessels were added in 2025-26, and the ministry plans 62 more in 2026-27 (Indian Infrastructure). A fifth law belongs with the four below: the Indian Ports Act, 2025, which replaced the Indian Ports Act, 1908, passed Parliament on 18 August 2025.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Four Acts replaced laws aged 67 to 169 years in five months

What it means: Four Acts were passed between March and August 2025, retiring laws that were up to 169 years old.

What to do: Check each new Act's commencement and rules against the four-Act list in the table before relying on it.

Foreign-flagged ships carry about 95% of India's own overseas trade

What it means: The new Acts leave this split in place, with about 5% of cargo on Indian-flagged ships.

What to do: Track the fleet and freight figures over the coming years to see whether Indian ships gain cargo share.

Flying the Indian flag costs 16–20% more, which the new Acts do not change

What it means: The post estimates the flag cost gap at 16–20%, the main reason shipowners avoid the Indian flag.

What to do: Measure whether the $20 billion roadmap and the 300-vessel re-flagging target narrow that gap.

What would fix this

  • DG Shipping should publish each year the share of India's overseas cargo carried by Indian-flagged ships, broken down by cargo such as crude oil, natural gas, coal and urea, so the 4–5% figure can be read from a primary table rather than from trade-press reports of it.
  • The Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways should publish an annual vessel-by-vessel tally against its targets of re-flagging 300 foreign-owned vessels by 2030 and adding about 100 ships in five years, so the economic side of the reform is tracked as precisely as the legal side.
  • The Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways should publish a component-wise breakdown of the 16–20% extra cost of operating under the Indian flag, and state which measure addresses each component, so it is clear what the new Acts and the investment roadmap do and do not close.
  • The RBI should report sea-freight payments to foreign shipping lines as a separate line in its balance-of-payments services data, so the estimated $75 billion annual freight bill can be checked and followed as the fleet changes.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
