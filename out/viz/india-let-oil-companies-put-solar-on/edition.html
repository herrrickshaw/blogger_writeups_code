An amendment to the Oilfields (Regulation and Development) Act, in force since April 2025, expanded the Centre's power to let solar and wind projects sit on the same land as an oil or gas lease. It's the reason India's biggest oilfield operator, ONGC, now has a green subsidiary. Sixteen months on, that subsidiary's own rooftop solar — the renewable capacity actually sitting on ONGC's own petroleum-lease land — adds up to under a megawatt. Its 4-gigawatt renewable portfolio came from buying somebody else's company instead.

Top line

  • 696 kW of ONGC's own rooftop solar, against 2.85 GW of combined renewables.
  • ₹6,248.5 crore paid for Ayana's equity, not the ₹19,500 crore headline figure.
  • 3,362 MW operating at Ayana by 30 September 2026, up from 2,911 MW at purchase.
Updated 9 November 2026. Ayana Renewable Power, the company ONGC and NTPC Green bought, had 3,362 MW in operation by 30 September 2026. At the purchase it had 2,911 MW operating and 1,652 MW contracted or awarded, according to NTPC Green Energy’s results presentation for the quarter to September. The new capacity, at projects in Rajasthan and Gujarat, comes from Ayana’s own pipeline, not ONGC’s petroleum leases. No newer figure for ONGC’s own rooftop or on-lease solar was found.
Updated 28 October 2026. The Petroleum and Natural Gas Rules, 2025 have now been read in the Gazette of India’s own text via the SansadSaar index of the archive.org Gazette of India collection: G.S.R. 888(E), dated 9 December 2025 (CG-DL-E-11122025-268409), in force from publication, replacing the Petroleum Concession Rules, 1949 and the Petroleum and Natural Gas Rules, 1959. One correction: the chapter on “Environment Protection, Decarbonisation, Monitoring of Greenhouse Gas Emissions and Flared Gas” is Chapter VII, not Chapter VIII; Chapter VIII covers sharing of infrastructure. Its content is as described: environmental protection, flared-gas reporting each quarter, and site restoration backed by a site restoration fund. The Rules also list among a lessee’s rights “to explore, plan, develop, and establish comprehensive energy projects in the oilfield” and to use the oilfield for decarbonisation, with a separate surface lease needed for land actually used. The Oilfields (Regulation and Development) Amendment Act, 2025 is confirmed as Act No. 6 of 2025, assented to on 28 March 2025 (CG-DL-E-28032025-262080).
Updated 18 October 2026. On 30 September 2026 the Union Cabinet approved Green Energy Corridor Phase III in a form very different from the one described below. As reported (Renewable Watch, Indian Infrastructure), it has an outlay of ₹1,86,405 crore, ₹1,36,378 crore for intra-state transmission and ₹50,000 crore for 50 GWh of battery storage, with central support of ₹54,082 crore (about 29%, not 40%), to evacuate up to 135 GW by 2032-33 rather than 2029-30. It is implemented by state transmission utilities for lines within states, not as an inter-state HVDC backbone. The ₹56,000 crore, 40% and 2029-30 figures below are superseded; see this blog’s piece on GEC Phase III. The original text below is unchanged.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

ONGC's gigawatt-scale renewables came from buying Ayana, not building

What it means: The renewable capacity ONGC can point to was acquired, not built on its own petroleum leases.

What to do: Confirm when the 1,652 MW contracted or awarded at purchase is commissioned, since the post gives no date.

The ₹19,500 crore headline is enterprise value, not the equity cash paid

What it means: The headline figure includes Ayana's assumed debt, which the equity payment does not.

What to do: Quote equity and enterprise value side by side for any deal, so the headline is not read as cash paid.

ONGC's own rooftop solar totals just 696 kW, far below its portfolio

What it means: This is the capacity sitting on land ONGC already holds, and it is small against its renewable ambitions.

What to do: Report ONGC's on-lease solar separately from acquired capacity, since no newer figure was found.

What would fix this

  • DGH and the petroleum ministry should require ONGC to file at least one Development Plan for renewable generation on its own oilfield acreage, with a public site and timeline. ORDA 2025 created the legal pathway; nothing currently forces ONGC to actually use it rather than substitute an acquisition for it.
  • ONGC Green's annual disclosures should separately report organically developed capacity against acquired capacity. A single "2.85 GW, heading to 10 GW" headline currently lets an M&A deal and a from-scratch oilfield-solar project read identically, when they represent completely different progress against the law's actual purpose.
  • The Ministry of Power should prioritise GEC Phase 3's Gujarat and Rajasthan legs specifically, since that is where both ONGC's largest acreage and its Ayana-derived generation cluster already sit; a 2029-30 commissioning date leaves a multi-year window in which any oilfield-sited solar would have nowhere to send surplus power.
  • Any future oilfield-sited RE project selling into a deficit state should carry a tripartite Centre-state payment backstop as standard, on the model already used for NTPC's supply into states like Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, rather than leaving each project to negotiate its own offtake security from scratch.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
