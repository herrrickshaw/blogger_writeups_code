Ayushman Bharat Health Account (ABHA) is a free, voluntary, 14-digit digital health identity that has quietly become one of the largest identity systems India has built since Aadhaar — over 93 crore accounts as of mid-2026, more than 104 crore health records linked to them, and a growth curve that's roughly sextupled in five years. It is not the same thing as Ayushman Bharat's PM-JAY insurance scheme, though the two names get conflated constantly; ABHA is the identity and records layer underneath India's whole digital-health push (ABDM), not a health-cover product. This piece explains what ABHA actually is, what the real adoption numbers say about who has one and where, what's genuinely working (a QR-scan queue-skip feature already used over 23 crore times), and what the honest caveat is on the consent and privacy side of a system this large.

Top line

  • 97.6 crore ABHA accounts as of 17 September 2026, up from 14.7 crore at end-2021.
  • 23.21 crore Scan-and-Share digital OPD tokens generated by 18 June 2026, each at a real hospital visit.
Updated 7 November 2026. Corrected. Section 4 described the data-protection framework as the DPDP Act and “its 2025 draft rules”. The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 were in fact notified on 13–14 November 2025, with obligations phased in over about 18 months to May 2027; reporting of the rollout places registration of consent managers, the role ABDM’s consent layer plays, from November 2026. How the rules apply to ABDM specifically is still not set out in a public note, so that open question stands. Also from the Health Ministry’s 10 July 2026 update: about 24 crore Scan & Register tokens (the queue-skip count this piece puts at 23.21 crore on 18 June) and 9.85 lakh healthcare professionals on the national registries. No ABHA total newer than the 17 September figure (97.6 crore) was found.

What would fix this

  • The National Health Authority should publish monthly counts of consent requests raised, granted, denied and revoked under ABDM, by state, alongside accounts and linked records, so it becomes measurable whether the consent-gated design is actually used rather than only built.
  • The National Health Authority should publish the more than ₹107 crore in digital-adoption incentives broken down by recipient category, state and facility type, so the public can see which hospitals and stakeholders were paid and set that against Scan and Share usage.
  • The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology and the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare should issue a joint note on how the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 applies to ABDM, naming which ABDM participants act as data fiduciaries and consent managers, so this open question is settled in writing.
  • The National Health Authority should commission and publish an independent survey of ABHA holders' understanding of what they consent to, split by state and rural and urban areas, so claims that consent is informed, or merely a formality, rest on evidence rather than commentary.
  • The National Health Authority should release ABHA accounts, linked records and Scan and Share tokens as one downloadable month-end series by state, so figures from different fact sheets and release dates no longer have to be footnoted as not directly comparable.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
