PNGRB commissioned its own expert committee, with Deloitte's help, to find out why India moves so much LPG and fuel by road when pipelines are cheaper. The committee's July 2026 report, read here in full, names the pipelines running near-empty and the ones running past 150% of their rated capacity, and states plainly that only 45% of India's LPG moves by pipeline. This piece is a straight read of what that report actually says — the one piece in this blog's pipeline series built on a document read cover to cover rather than reconstructed from search results.

PNGRB expert committee: some pipelines at 165% of capacity, others far below

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 45% of India's LPG moved by pipeline in FY2023-24; the rest went by road and rail.
  • 29% of petroleum products moved by rail, while road carried only about 1%.
  • 7.9%–165.3% is the range of FY26 pipeline utilisation across the committee's register.
Updated 13 October 2026. On 21 August 2026 PNGRB authorised GAIL to build the Jhansi–Sitarganj, Shikrapur–Hubballi/Goa and Cherlapally–Nagpur LPG pipelines (about 1,800 km, about ₹7,000 crore), per the regulator’s press note as reported by IANS and Outlook Business; the “bid out” stage described below is past for those three. One report describes the Paradip–Raipur link as still pending, which conflicts with this article’s statement that it was awarded to Dilip Buildcon; check the PNGRB notice. What it means: The committee's own tally puts LPG pipeline share at 45% nationally, with the North-East having no LPG pipelines at all.

What to do: Connect nearby bottling plants to existing common-carrier pipelines rather than trucking to them, as the committee recommends.

Petroleum products depend on rail, with road close to negligible

What it means: Pipelines carry most petroleum products, and the committee says rail remains essential because pipelines cannot replace it entirely.

What to do: Keep rail capacity in the plan, and add rail-unloading facilities where they cut specific road movement, as the committee proposes.

What it means: The committee's register shows wide spread between pipelines, from Panipat–Delhi at 7.9% to Haldia–Mourigram–Rajbandh at 165.3%.

What to do: Read utilisation figures against each line's history, since over-100% lines may be debottlenecked and low-use lines may be newly built for future demand.

What would fix this

  • PNGRB should publish a corrigendum to the July 2026 committee report stating the correct capacity of the Numaligarh–Siliguri pipeline, since the report gives 5.5 million tonnes a year in its summary table and 1.7 million tonnes in its utilisation table.
  • PNGRB should publish the pipeline-by-pipeline utilisation register every year, showing both nameplate and current effective capacity after any debottlenecking, so readings like 165.3% on Haldia–Mourigram–Rajbandh or 2% on Krishnapatnam–Hyderabad can be interpreted without guessing which capacity they are measured against.
  • PNGRB should publish an action-taken report with dates on the committee's recommendations, such as linking the two named Andhra Pradesh bottling plants to the Hassan–Cherlapally line and building the Madurai rail-unloading facility, so delivery can be checked against the report.
  • PNGRB should publish the region-wise LPG modal split — pipeline, road and rail — every year, so the 45% national pipeline share for FY2023–24 becomes a baseline that the pipelines now under construction can be measured against.
  • PNGRB should publish an assessment of LPG pipeline connectivity options for the North-East, the one region the committee found with no LPG pipelines at all and where one location's supply is trucked nearly 1,000 km from Haldia port.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Sources and caveats

This piece is based on a direct, complete reading of "Committee Report on Optimization of LPG and Petroleum Product Movements" (Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board, dated July 2026, prepared with Deloitte, under the chairmanship of Shri Arvind Kumar), supplied to this piece as a file rather than found via search. Figures are quoted or closely paraphrased from the report's own tables and text; page and table references are given inline where a claim could be attributed to a specific table. The report itself carries at least one internal inconsistency this piece could not resolve: the Numaligarh–Siliguri pipeline's capacity is given as 5.5 million tonnes a year in the report's summary table and 1.7 million tonnes a year in its pipeline-by-pipeline utilisation table, and this piece reports both rather than picking one. The LPG modal-share figures (FY2023-24) and the petroleum-product figures (which mix FY2025 demand data with FY26 pipeline-utilisation data) are not all from the same year, which this piece has tried to flag at each figure's first use rather than implying a single reference year for the whole piece. This piece's other pieces on India's pipeline networks cited a "PNGRB-commissioned zonal study" or "Deloitte study" from search-engine snippets without being able to open the underlying document; this report appears to be that same document or a closely related one, and those pieces have been corrected separately to cite the confirmed figures here instead of the earlier, lower-confidence search-snippet versions.
