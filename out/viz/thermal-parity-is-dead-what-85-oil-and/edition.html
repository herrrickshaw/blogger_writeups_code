One MMBtu of gas and one-sixth of a barrel of crude carry the same heat. That equivalence — thermal parity — is the textbook anchor for oil-gas pricing, and academic literature has spent fifteen years documenting how far real markets have drifted from it. India’s own domestic gas price is a live experiment in the opposite direction: a formula that re-pegs gas to crude by government notification, every month, regardless of what the physical energy-equivalence math says it should cost.

  • +0.72 to −0.17 oil-gas price correlation before and after the 2008 break, NYMEX 2006–2014.
  • $7.86 notified domestic gas price for August 2026, against $2.64 at Henry Hub.
Updated 7 November 2026. Two corrections to the gas side. The ceiling that actually binds on nomination-field gas from ONGC and Oil India is $7.00/MMBtu from April 2026, raised from $6.75 (Free Press Journal); PPAC’s monthly “domestic natural gas price”, $7.86 for August, is the formula price, not that ceiling (PPAC notified $10.76 for April, above the cap). Against the $7.00 ceiling, August’s ratio is about 12.2:1 (2.2× thermal parity), not 10.8:1. And crude has moved: the Indian basket averaged about $116/bbl in September 2026 (PPAC data, via ThePrint), which against the $7.00 ceiling is about 16.6:1, three times thermal parity. Exhibit 1 and the chart are August-dated.

What it means: The 2015 cointegration study found no long-run oil-gas relationship after the 2008 break, which it attributes to North American shale supply.

What to do: Until a liquid domestic gas hub exists, the ceiling is the only working decoupling lever.

India's notified gas price sits well above Henry Hub's unregulated spot price

What it means: The notified $7.86 is well above the $2.64 Henry Hub spot price, a policy outcome rather than a market one.

What to do: Any reform should name its replacement benchmark, since each of the three routes carries a real cost.

What would fix this

  • PPAC should publish with each monthly domestic gas price notification the full working — average crude basket price, raw 10 per cent output, applicable floor and ceiling, and final price — so the gap between a raw $8.4–8.5 and the notified $7.86 per MMBtu is explained on the notice itself.
  • PPAC should publish the nomination-field gas ceiling as a standalone figure with its schedule of step-ups, separate from the $8.90 ceiling for deepwater, ultra-deepwater and high-pressure fields, so users need not infer which cap is binding.
  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should publish each year an estimate of the revenue ONGC and Oil India forgo because the ceiling binds, and which consumer segments receive the gas, so the cost of shielding city-gas and fertiliser users is stated openly.
  • PNGRB should publish monthly volumes traded on the Indian Gas Exchange as a share of domestic gas consumption, so progress toward a hub liquid enough to serve as a domestic pricing reference can be measured rather than described as a fraction.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish
What this article does not establish. Whether India’s policymakers are actively considering a move away from crude-basket indexation — nothing in the sources used here indicates an active proposal to do so; this is a structural description of the current formula and the options a decoupling would face, not a report on live policy deliberation. The exact current Asian LNG spot (JKM) price — a figure was found in initial research but could not be independently corroborated against a second source, so it is omitted rather than published on single-source confidence. Whether the 2009 US decoupling identified by Mittal (2016) has itself begun re-coupling in the years since 2014, which the paper’s own data does not cover — Exhibit 1’s August 2026 Henry Hub comparison suggests it has not, but that is one snapshot, not a re-run of the cointegration test.
