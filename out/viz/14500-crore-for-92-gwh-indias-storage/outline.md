On 4 November the Union Cabinet approved ₹14,500 crore of viability gap funding (VGF), a capital subsidy that covers part of a project’s cost so that it can sell its service at a price buyers will pay, for 92 GWh of grid-scale energy storage that has not yet been commissioned. The money runs from 2026-27 to 2030-31. This blog’s [piece on India’s battery storage boom](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/09/indias-battery-storage-boom-is.html) found tariffs for new battery projects climbing back above the viability benchmark in 2026 and a domestic cell industry far behind the pack assemblers. The new scheme is the third round of storage VGF in three years, and the first to include pumped hydro.

This piece sets the three schemes side by side, works out what each pays per kWh, checks how far they get toward the storage the Cabinet itself says the grid needs, and asks whether the 40% domestic-content rule for batteries can be met with the cells India makes today.

## What the Cabinet approved

The release splits the 92 GWh into 60 GWh of pumped storage (PSP), 30 GWh of battery energy storage (BESS) and 2 GWh of other technologies. It expects ₹97,600 crore of investment, so the subsidy is 14.9% of the total. Projects will be awarded through tariff-based competitive bidding. Public and private distribution companies can get VGF for batteries and pumped storage, and public or private entities for the other technologies. Battery projects must have at least 40% domestic content.

The release puts the need at 411 GWh of storage by 2031-32, and says the 92 GWh is 170% of present grid-scale capacity. It does not say how the ₹14,500 crore is split between pumped storage and batteries, or what rate per MWh each will get.

The 2023 scheme, approved on 6 September 2023, set aside ₹3,760 crore of budget support for 4 GWh of batteries, up to 40% of capital cost. As approved, that was ₹9,400 per kWh against an assumed cost of ₹23,500 per kWh. Battery prices then fell, and later rounds capped support at 30% of capital cost or ₹27 lakh per MWh (₹2,700 per kWh). The same money was stretched to about 13.8 GWh.

The second scheme, a Ministry of Power order of 9 June 2025, used ₹5,400 crore from the Power System Development Fund (PSDF), a fund built from grid congestion and deviation charges, for 30 GWh: 25 GWh across 15 states and 5 GWh for NTPC. It paid a flat ₹18 lakh per MWh, ₹1,800 per kWh, about 16% of the ₹33,000 crore of investment reported at the time.

The new scheme averages ₹1,576 per kWh across all three technologies. The Central Transmission Utility’s September 2026 benchmark for battery capital cost, as reported, is ₹0.80 crore per MWh, or ₹8,000 per kWh. Against that, the 2025 rate covers 22.5% of a battery’s cost and the new average 19.7%. If the 30 GWh of batteries were paid at the 2025 rate, they would take ₹5,400 crore, leaving ₹9,100 crore, or ₹1,468 per kWh, for pumped storage and the rest. That is an illustration, not the scheme’s split.

Lower support per kWh is partly good news, since it tracks cheaper hardware. But the battery piece showed 2026 tariffs rising, with Maharashtra’s distribution company awarding 2-hour storage at ₹2.38 lakh per MW per month in September, above the viability benchmark of ₹2.3 lakh. Pumped storage costs are rising too: IEEFA–JMK report that a SECI 8-hour pumped-storage award in 2026 cleared at ₹108–110 lakh per MW per year, about 40–43% above a 2025 Uttar Pradesh award. A smaller subsidy per kWh is arriving as the price developers bid is going up.

The 2023 scheme was approved more than three years ago. By July 2026, according to a Rajya Sabha answer, battery energy storage purchase agreements had been signed for 12.7 GWh, 6.54 GWh had reached financial closure, 0.5 GWh was commissioned and ₹168.04 crore had been paid, 4.5% of the budget. Under the 2025 scheme, letters of award covered 22.43 GWh and agreements 19.65 GWh, 5 GWh had reached financial closure, and ₹180 crore had been sanctioned. Projects under the 2025 order are due within 18 months of their agreements.

VGF is paid in stages, much of it only after a project starts running, so low payments do not mean the schemes have failed. But they do mean that almost none of the 43.8 GWh supported under the first two schemes was yet supplying the grid when the third was approved.

## Can batteries meet 40% domestic content?

The release sets the 40% floor but does not define it: whether it is measured on the whole project cost or on equipment, and whether imported cells assembled in India count. That matters because cells are a large share of the cost. IEEFA–JMK put Chinese cell prices at about $53.57 per kWh in September 2026, roughly ₹5,114 per kWh. Against the ₹8,000 per kWh capital-cost benchmark, cells would be about 64% of a battery project. Against vendor turnkey prices of ₹1.25–1.8 crore per MWh, they would be 28–41%. At the low end of cost, a project with imported cells has little room to reach 40% from containers, inverters, cabling and civil work alone.

Indian cells are scarce. The battery piece found India’s lithium-ion cell capacity around 1 GWh at the end of 2025, against about 60 GWh of pack assembly, with estimates of China’s share of India’s cell imports at 63–85% or higher. The ACC PLI scheme for cell factories had awarded 40 GWh and commissioned about 1.4 GWh. A 10 GWh tranche reserved for grid storage closed for bids on 13 October 2026. IEEFA–JMK also report that cells and containers landed in India cost 14–23% more than Chinese prices after tax.

The government-backed battery pipeline now covers about 124 GWh: about 13.8 GWh under the 2023 scheme, 30 GWh under PSDF, 30 GWh under the new scheme and 50 GWh under Green Energy Corridor III. If the 40% rule needs Indian cells, it will bind on a market where domestic supply is a fraction of one year’s demand, and the 14–23% landed premium will be priced into bids. The VGF would then partly pay for that premium rather than for more storage.

## How far this gets toward 411 GWh

The IEEFA–JMK tracker put installed storage at about 44.3 GWh in June 2026: 36 GWh of pumped hydro and 8.3 GWh of batteries. The Cabinet release’s “170%” implies present capacity of about 54 GWh. The difference may come from what each counts, and this piece uses the tracker’s figure. Mercom’s separate tracker puts batteries alone at about 9.3 GWh at the same date. Adding the undelivered capacity under the two earlier schemes and the new 92 GWh gives about 180 GWh, 44% of 411 GWh, leaving a gap of about 231 GWh. Green Energy Corridor III’s 50 GWh of batteries, due a year after the 2031-32 date, would take it to 56%.

The totals assume every supported project is built. Some may overlap with capacity already counted, and pumped storage projects often take longer than five years to build. The new scheme needs about 18 GWh a year to be bid, built and commissioned through 2030-31, about twice the battery capacity installed by mid-2026 (8.3–9.3 GWh, depending on the tracker), every year.
