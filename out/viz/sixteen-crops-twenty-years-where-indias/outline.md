An [earlier piece on this site](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/10/indias-farm-map-in-seven-charts-maize.html) mapped India’s crops as a whole. This one goes crop by crop through sixteen of them, the main cereals, pulses, oilseeds, cotton and sugarcane. For each it asks five questions. How fast has output grown? Did that growth come from more land or from more output per hectare? Which states and districts grow it? How wide is the yield gap between the best and worst major producers? And how much does the national harvest swing from one year to the next?

How much to trust it. All figures are from the Department of Agriculture’s Unified Portal for Agricultural Statistics (UPAG): all-India area, production and yield from 1966-67, state figures from 1997-98 and district figures from 2015-16, with 2025-26 at the third advance estimate. To keep one drought or bumper year from driving the comparisons, “2003-06” means the average of 2003-04 to 2005-06 and “2023-26” the average of 2023-24 to 2025-26. Growth is split into area and yield on a log scale, so the two parts add up exactly. Cotton production is in bales of 170 kg of lint; UPAG’s download labels it lakh tonnes, but the figures only reconcile with its published yield as bales. State yield gaps compare only states producing at least 3% of a crop. Telangana appears from 2014, when it was carved out of Andhra Pradesh, so its earlier output sits in Andhra’s figures. The data and code are in the open repository [india-mandi-arrivals](https://github.com/herrrickshaw/india-mandi-arrivals).

Moong and maize grew fastest, about 6% a year for twenty years, roughly half from new land and half from better yields. Nine crops grew 2.6–3.7% a year, and for rice, wheat, gram, lentil and urad yield did more of the work than area. Three crops break the pattern. Soybean grew only by expanding: its area rose from 73 to 129 lakh hectares while its yield stayed at about a tonne a hectare. Groundnut and bajra went the other way, losing area but raising yields enough to grow anyway. Jowar and ragi are the only crops to shrink: jowar’s area fell from 90 to 39 lakh hectares, and better yields could not make up for it.

The gaps are widest in rainfed coarse grains and pulses, where a few irrigated pockets coexist with large dryland areas. Andhra Pradesh’s jowar, much of it grown in the rabi season, yields 2.9 times the national average while Rajasthan’s yields about two-thirds of it. The gaps are narrowest in lentil, sugarcane and soybean. For wheat and rice, Punjab’s yields are 41–46% above the national average.

Rice is grown almost everywhere: the three largest producers supply only 35%, down from 41%. Soybean is the opposite, with Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh alone producing 84%. ## What it adds up to

Three patterns run through the sixteen crops. The fastest growers, maize and moong, are the crops where both land and yield expanded, pulled by new demand from ethanol and poultry feed in maize’s case. The crops with the most room to grow output without more land are those with the widest gaps between their best and worst states: jowar, maize, moong, urad and bajra. And the crops that matter most for price stability, the irrigated cereals, are also the steadiest harvests, while the rain-fed oilseeds and pulses swing by a fifth or more in a typical year.
