India's cotton yield peaked at 566 kilograms of lint a hectare in 2013-14 and has since fallen back under 450, even as the Cotton Association of India keeps revising this season's own output estimate upward and imports head for a record. Kaala cotton — the low-input indigenous variety now being revived — is native to Kutch in Gujarat, not to the Bt-hybrid cotton belts of Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu and Madhya Pradesh where the yield problem and the soil strain are actually concentrated. This piece keeps that mismatch in view rather than papering over it, and looks at what is genuinely available to those three states: soil-specific fixes, and a farmer-income line sitting mostly unclaimed in the cottonseed and stalk every cotton plant already produces.

Top line

  • 566 kg of lint a hectare was India's 2013-14 peak; the 2024-25 estimate is roughly 436 to under 450.
  • ~60 lakh bales of cotton imports projected by CAI for 2025-26, up from 47 lakh.
  • 29.74 million tonnes of surplus cotton-stalk residue, mostly unused as biogas feedstock.
Updated 27 October 2026. China’s 2024-25 cotton yield was given as about 1,993 kg/ha. USDA puts it at a record of about 2,384 kg/ha, and China’s official figure is 2,171.6 kg/ha; the text and table now say so. India’s 2024-25 range (about 436–450 kg/ha, 437 provisional) is confirmed on official data; USDA’s estimate is about 461.

Cotton stalk is 29.74 million tonnes of surplus residue, mostly unused

What it means: Stalk is mostly burnt in the field or discarded, and a small CBG plant costs Rs 300-500 lakh, beyond most single farmers.

What to do: Build cluster-level or FPO-backed biogas capacity rather than expecting individual farmers to convert stalk.

Zinc is deficient in 60% or more of the soils in Madhya Pradesh's two cotton tracts

What it means: Cotton fibre quality is unusually sensitive to potassium, which these soils can under-supply even when they look rich.

What to do: Test soils for zinc and potassium before dosing nitrogen alone.

What would fix this

  • ICAR-CICR (Nagpur) should publish district-level soil-nutrient data — especially zinc and potassium — for continuous-cotton tracts in Vidarbha, the way Madhya Pradesh's own agro-climatic zone surveys already do for Malwa and Nimar. A state-average dose cannot fix a deficiency this specific to a district's cropping history.
  • ICAR-CICR should run and publish a breeding trial evaluating G. herbaceum genetic material for drought and pest resilience, framed explicitly as breeding stock rather than a "grow Kaala cotton here" recommendation the geography does not support.
  • State cooperative dairy federations and FPOs should expand village-level cottonseed-crushing capacity in cotton belts, so cake and oil value is captured near the farm rather than the raw seed leaving the district at a lower price.
  • MNRE/GOBARdhan should extend viability-gap funding specifically to cotton-stalk-fed CBG clusters in Vidarbha, Nimar and Tamil Nadu's cotton tracts, given a 29.74-million-tonne national stalk surplus that is mostly burnt rather than converted today.
  • CAI and the Ministry of Textiles should publish a single, reconciled lint-yield series by state, in kg/ha, on a fixed schedule. This piece had to convert a seed-cotton figure against a separately reported lint-yield series just to compare Vidarbha to the national number; a reader should not have to do that.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Sources and caveats

India's 2013-14 peak lint yield (566 kg/ha) and 2024-25 estimate (roughly 436–450 kg/ha), the world/China/Brazil comparison, and the "lowest yield among top-5 producers" finding are from secondary coverage (Drishti IAS, Shankar IAS Parliament, IAS Express) summarising USDA and industry data; this piece could not trace every figure to a single primary USDA table and treats them as reliable-by-repetition. CAI's 2025-26 crop estimate revisions (317 lakh bales in February 2026, 320.5 in March, 324 in April, 337 in July) and the import estimate (record ~60 lakh bales, up from 47 lakh, with roughly 51 lakh bales landed by end-June) are from Fibre2Fashion and Apparel Resources trade-press coverage of CAI's own releases; this piece worked from that coverage rather than the primary release PDF on caionline.in, which was not independently re-fetched for this piece. CITI's 348-lakh-bale consumption estimate and CAI's reduced 15-lakh-bale export estimate are from the same coverage. Pink bollworm resistance to Cry1Ac and Cry2Ab, Gujarat's 2015 loss of 1.41 million bales (~$817 million), the national ~13.2-lakh-bale/₹3,900-crore annual loss estimate since 2017-18, and the secondary-pest (whitefly, jassid, mealybug) mechanism are from peer-reviewed and trade literature (ScienceDirect, PMC, journals.sagepub.com) on Bt cotton sustainability in India. The $9-billion-to-$800-million cotton export-earnings figure is from a single review paper's framing (journal.hep.com.cn) and was not independently reconciled against DGCI&S or Ministry of Commerce trade data for this piece; treat it as a claim needing primary confirmation, not a verified figure. Kaala/Wagad cotton's botanical identity (Gossypium herbaceum), Kutch origin, Indus Valley-era archaeological evidence, agronomic profile, colonial-era decline and 2010-11 rebranding are from Homegrown India, Mongabay India, Asia InCH and green-tailor.com; this piece did not find and does not claim any ICAR field trial introducing this genetic material into Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu or Madhya Pradesh. Vidarbha's current seed-cotton yield (7–8 quintals/ha) and the "untenable" hybrid-economics framing are from IndiaSpend; the separate 2001-2025 Maharashtra lint-yield series (average 285 kg/ha, low 100 in 2001, high 475 in 2017) is from a CEIC Data indicator page. The lint-to-seed-cotton conversion used to compare these two figures (approximately 35% ginning out-turn) is this piece's own approximation, not an official figure for the current season. Tamil Nadu's cotton-area contraction is described directionally from ResearchGate- and MASU Journal-indexed studies on Tamil Nadu cotton cultivation and TNAU's own "Problem Soils and Their Management" reference; the specific area figures found in different secondary summaries were internally inconsistent on units (lakh versus million hectares) and are therefore not restated as a precise number here. Madhya Pradesh's Vertisol nutrient profile and the 62.6%/60.0% zinc-deficiency figures for the Malwa Plateau and Nimar Valley are from a published agro-climatic zone micronutrient survey (plantarchives.org/ResearchGate); the potassium-fibre-quality mechanism is from a PMC-indexed study on soil-applied potassium in cotton; the 18% area-decline figure for Madhya Pradesh is a single secondary estimate, flagged as such rather than confirmed against state agriculture department data. The 89.5 kg N/ha figure for cotton is drawn from this blog's own earlier reporting, linked above, itself sourced to an FAO country study using 2003-04 data. Cottonseed's approximate 60–65% share of ginned kapas weight is a general ginning-industry ratio, not a current-season figure. The 1.4-million-tonne additional edible-oil potential and 9.7-percentage-point import-gap reduction from cottonseed are from NITI Aayog/government edible-oil self-sufficiency strategy coverage (PIB, RIS). The ₹2,900/quintal cottonseed-cake price and its NCDEX trading are from Feedipedia and Dairy Knowledge Portal reference pages, current as of when accessed and subject to normal commodity-price movement. The 80%-or-more cotton-stalk mass share and the 29.74-million-tonne national surplus-stalk estimate (against 234 MMT total surplus agricultural residue) are from UNCTAD reporting on cotton by-products and a ScienceDirect techno-enviro-economic overview of compressed biogas from agricultural residues. CBG yield (50–55 kg per tonne of feedstock), market price (₹50–75/kg) and plant capex (₹300–500 lakh for a small/medium plant) are from industry explainer sources (EAI, PelletRates, various CBG-plant-setup guides); the ₹3,000–3,500-per-tonne revenue figure is this piece's own arithmetic from those published unit rates, not an official cost-benefit study, and excludes capital and operating cost entirely. Berar/Vidarbha's 1860s cotton-monoculture history, the American Civil War-driven shift from roughly 12% to about 90% of Britain's raw cotton imports, and Berar matching Egypt's export volume by 1867, are from the University of Manchester's Cottonopolis research project, Undisciplined Environments, and The News Dirt's historical coverage, cross-referenced against a peer-reviewed comparative study of colonial-era Indian cotton cultivation (Rönnbäck, The Economic History Review, 2022). The Khadakwasla Dam (1879) and Wilson Dam/Pravara (1910–1926) construction history, and the framing of the latter as built partly to promote cotton cultivation in Ahmednagar district, are from India Water Portal's colonial-irrigation history coverage. Sugarcane's role as the largest revenue contributor to the Bombay Irrigation Department, and 1870s-era colonial recognition of salinity from canal irrigation on black soil, are from the same source and from academic coverage of salinity in colonial Indian and Australian irrigation (Australian Geographer). The present-day 1.5%-of-total-geographical-area saline/sodic soil figure across the Godavari, Ghod, Purna, Manar, Mula, Pravara, Nira and Krishna basin command areas, and its attribution to over-irrigation and sugarcane-industry establishment, is from a published Maharashtra soil-resource survey indexed via ResearchGate/academic aggregators; this piece did not independently re-fetch the primary survey document. This piece treats the colonial-history section as historical synthesis explaining a documented precedent for mechanisms (monoculture-driven nutrient depletion, canal-irrigation salinity) already discussed on their present-day merits earlier in the piece, not as a claim of unbroken causal continuity in specific soil parcels across 160 years. Nothing in this piece is agricultural, investment or seed-variety advice; a farmer or agency should confirm any of the above against a current primary source, particularly CAI's own release and the relevant state agriculture department, before acting on it.
