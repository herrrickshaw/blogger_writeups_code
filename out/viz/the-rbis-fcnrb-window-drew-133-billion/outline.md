Between 8 June and 31 August, banks took about $133 billion of three-to-five-year dollar deposits from non-resident Indians and swapped them with the Reserve Bank of India. That is nearly five times the FCNR(B) money raised in the 2013 scheme that was its model. The rupee held, reserves hit a record of $785.7 billion on 4 September, and the RBI closed the window a month early.

Four weeks later reserves stood at $734.6 billion, $51 billion lower, and the RBI’s net short position in dollar forwards had passed $200 billion. The two facts belong together. The swap brings dollars in now and records a promise to deliver them back in 2029 to 2031 at today’s exchange rate. What the window bought is time, and the price is a forward liability that the RBI does not itemise.

The point of the design is that a depositor earns the dollar interest rate, which was 5.5% to 7.4% in reported offers, with no exchange-rate risk, because the RBI has taken the risk. The RBI receives the dollars at once and sells them forward to the same banks at the same rate. The banks hold rupees, the RBI holds dollars, and nobody has to find a counterparty for a three-to-five-year hedge, which is not easy to buy.

Reserves rose from $666.9 billion in late June to $785.7 billion on 4 September, including a record weekly rise of $44.9 billion in the week after the window closed. Then they fell for four weeks: $4.9 billion, $14.9 billion, $18.3 billion and $13.0 billion. In the week to 2 October foreign currency assets fell $10.7 billion and gold $2.3 billion. Reserves are now just above the previous record of $728.5 billion set on 27 February, and still $67.7 billion above the June low.

The RBI does not split the weekly change between dollar sales and revaluation. One report says the RBI sold a net $22.4 billion between 11 and 25 September to defend the rupee; one analyst estimate puts the month’s net sales near $30 billion and valuation losses near $21 billion. Both are estimates from outside the RBI. The RBI Governor has said reserves remain adequate, at about 11 months of imports.

None of this means the window failed. The rupee traded between 94.3 and 96.6 to the dollar through August, against a record low of 96.96 in May, and the dollars bought that stability. But reserves counted in dollars include money that is owed back.

The RBI buys the dollars from the bank today and sells them to it later, so each swap adds to its short position in dollar forwards. That book was already at a record $106.7 billion at the end of May, from the RBI’s earlier defence of the rupee. It rose to $136.8 billion at the end of July and to $200.1 billion at the end of August, with the part maturing in more than a year almost doubling from $91.5 billion to $176.6 billion. The short-dated part is smaller than the headline: the RBI also holds long positions, and its net short in contracts under a year was only about $23.5 billion.

At the end of August the book was equal to about 27% of reserves ($200.1 billion against $740.8 billion on 28 August), a comparison that mixes two dates and is only a rough guide. The RBI’s own 2016 note on the 2013 scheme said the forward purchases largely front-run the swaps, so reserves would “witness significant accretions initially to be followed by depletions around maturity”. In 2013, banks raised about $34 billion, of which about $27 billion came through FCNR(B) deposits, mostly for three years, and the RBI said in 2016 it was fully prepared for maturity. The 2026 FCNR(B) money is nearly five times that and runs longer.

The Governor has said nearly half of the money is five-year. That pushes about $65 billion to mid-2031, with the rest from mid-2029 to mid-2030. Banks may offer to renew deposits at maturity, and renewals are eligible for the swap, so the RBI may not have to pay all of it out. Whether it will try to roll the money over, and on what terms, has not been said.

## What it costs

The cost does not appear in a budget. The RBI sold dollars forward at today’s rate, so if the rupee is weaker at maturity the RBI will have given up the gain on those dollars, and if it is stronger the RBI gains. Each 1% fall in the rupee against the rate at inception is worth about $1.3 billion on $133 billion. The hedging cost the RBI is reported to absorb, 2.8% to 3.0% a year on the principal, would come to about $3.7 billion to $4.0 billion a year if applied to the whole sum. That is this blog’s arithmetic on the reported rate. One economist columnist puts the pressure on the external account at about $3.5 billion a year, or about $15 billion over the life of the deposits.

The swaps were struck near the rupee’s weakest levels of the year, and the RBI has called the rupee undervalued, as reported. If that is right, the position could end in a gain. If the oil-led pressure that opened the window persists, it will not. The accounts that would show which is happening are not published in any detail.
