India's own vehicle-testing data only measures ethanol's mileage penalty at two blend levels, E10 and E20. Run the actual combustion chemistry on the full E0–E30 ladder and the widely repeated "E20 cuts CO2 by 30%" claim does not survive contact with the arithmetic — the real tailpipe number, once the extra fuel burned is counted, is close to zero in an uncalibrated vehicle and around 5% in a calibrated one.

E20 and tailpipe CO2: near-zero cut in uncalibrated engines, about 5% in calibrated ones

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 6.8% is the most a litre of E20 can cut tailpipe CO2 against petrol, far from the 30% claimed.
  • ~0% net per-km CO2 cut at E20 in an uncalibrated engine.
Updated 27 October 2026. Two details from Parliament are added. The 30 July 2026 Lok Sabha reply cited here for the 2–6% efficiency loss also claims that E20 cuts CO2 by about 30% compared with E10, so the 30% figure this piece tests appears in an official answer, not only in press coverage; the reports do not say whether it is a tailpipe, per-kilometre or lifecycle figure, and this piece’s chemistry still finds a per-kilometre tailpipe cut of about zero to 5% at E20. A reply on 23 July 2026 put the efficiency loss for vehicles built for E10 at 3–5%, within the range above; it has been added to the text and the table.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

What it means: Ethanol carries about 66% of petrol's carbon per litre, so each 5-point blend step trims CO2 by about 1.7%.

What to do: Ask for tested CO2-per-kilometre figures at each blend level before accepting any per-litre or headline cut.

Net CO2 per kilometre falls only where the engine is calibrated for E20

What it means: An uncalibrated engine loses about as much mileage as the fuel's energy gap, so its CO2 cut disappears.

What to do: Tie any E20 CO2 claim to the calibration status of the vehicles it is measured on.

What it means: No measured E20 mileage loss in India exceeds the energy-content ceiling of 6.8%.

What to do: Publish tested mileage at E25 and E30 before any blend above E20 is planned.

What would fix this

  • ARAI should publish its E25 test results on E10- and E20-compliant vehicles, giving measured mileage and tailpipe CO2 per kilometre by vehicle category, so the blend ladder beyond E20 rests on tested figures rather than energy-content ceilings calculated from physical constants.
  • NITI Aayog should publish a single E20 lifecycle-GHG figure for each feedstock — sugarcane, maize and surplus FCI rice — with its method attached, so the untraceable round “40%” claim can be either reconciled with the roughly 65% and 50% pure-ethanol figures or withdrawn.
  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should report tailpipe CO2, tailpipe CO and HC, and lifecycle GHG as three separate lines whenever it states the benefits of blending, so CO and HC reductions of around 30–50% are not read as a CO2 cut that the chemistry puts nearer 6.8% per litre.
  • The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways should require manufacturers to declare, in each new model’s type-approval fuel-consumption figure, the blend the engine is calibrated for and its tested mileage on E20, since calibration decides whether a car gets close to zero or roughly 5% CO2 benefit per kilometre.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
