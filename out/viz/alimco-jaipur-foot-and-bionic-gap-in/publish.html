Between a state-owned manufacturer, a Jaipur-based charity that fits limbs for free, and an 80-year-old military rehabilitation hospital, India runs artificial-limb programmes at a scale few countries its income level can match. What that system has not produced is a mass-market domestic alternative to the sensing, motorised hands and knees the global bionics industry has been selling since the mid-2010s — and on at least one point of policy fine print, the reporting hasn't kept up with the scheme either.

Top line

  • 50% rise in both ADIP income limits, effective 26 September 2024: the free-device line went from about ₹15,000 to ₹22,500 a month.
  • 2.25 million beneficiaries claimed by the Jaipur Foot charity in April 2024, while its other pages cite 2 to 2.5 million.
  • 1944 is when the Pune Artificial Limb Centre was established; the LUKE Arm, a sensing device, was FDA-cleared in May 2014.
Updated 22 October 2026. This post gives the medical-devices PLI scheme's March 2025 position: 21 approved projects making 54 device types. Newer figures are out. A July 2026 Lok Sabha reply, as reported by Indian Pharma Post, put the scheme at 27 approved projects, 23 of them commissioned, with about ₹1,153 crore invested. A September 2026 government release, as reported by Angel One and MedPath, said production had started for 57 devices, including MRI machines, CT scanners, cath labs and linear accelerators. Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Both ADIP income limits rose exactly 50% in September 2024

What it means: Households earning ₹22,501 to ₹30,000 a month now pay half cost, and a 1 February 2025 budget report still quoted the old limits.

What to do: Check the current ADIP guidelines before quoting any threshold, since secondary reports lag the revision.

The Jaipur Foot charity's own beneficiary count ranges from 2 to 2.5 million

What it means: BMVSS fits every limb free of cost, but its cumulative beneficiary count is not stable across its own pages.

What to do: Quote BMVSS counts only with their dates, because its pages disagree.

India's limb institutions predate the sensing-limb wave by decades

  1. 1944Artificial Limb Centre, Pune, established
  2. 1968Jaipur Foot designed by Ram Chandra Sharma and P. K. Sethi
  3. 1972ALIMCO incorporated, 30 November
  4. 1981ADIP funding scheme begins
  5. May 2014LUKE Arm FDA-cleared, the first sensing device in the table

What it means: ALIMCO, BMVSS and ALC Pune all fit the passive, mechanical design at scale, while the sensing devices came from elsewhere.

What to do: Confirm the Phoenix Foot and KADAM dates directly with the source organisations before publishing either.

What would fix this

  • The Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities should keep a dated changelog of ADIP eligibility revisions on the scheme page and publish yearly fitments by device type and state, so that changes like the 26 September 2024 threshold rise are not misreported months later, and limbs can be separated from other aids.
  • ALIMCO should publish its annual reports on its own website, with limb-specific output and revenue, and correct its public communications to a single founding date, so that its revenue no longer traces only to a data aggregator and the 1972–1976 discrepancy is settled by ALIMCO itself.
  • The Department of Pharmaceuticals should state in the PLI Scheme for Medical Devices product list whether externally worn prosthetic limbs and orthotics are eligible, so that manufacturers and readers do not have to guess whether “implants including implantable electronic devices” covers socket-fitted limbs.
  • ECHS should publish a prosthetic-specific rate list and reimbursement ceiling, including for microprocessor and powered devices, so that veterans fitted at ALC Pune or elsewhere know in advance what is covered, instead of searching a 2018 procedure and 2022 compendium that do not state it.
  • The Directorate General of Armed Forces Medical Services should issue a PIB release on the Phoenix Foot Mk-I and the Saksham clinic, with specifications and intended fitment numbers, so that India’s own prosthetic development is documented in a primary source rather than a single specialist-outlet report.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. ALIMCO's reported FY2024-25 revenue (a figure around ₹726 crore appears in at least one third-party company database) is excluded above because it traces only to a data aggregator, not to ALIMCO's own annual report or the Department of Public Enterprises' Public Enterprises Survey, neither of which this piece's research could reach. The exact founding date of BMVSS is not stated here because it could not be independently confirmed within this piece's sourcing. ALC Pune's "Phoenix Foot Mk-I" and "Saksham" clinic rest on a single specialist-outlet report, not a located PIB release; treat both as reported, not confirmed, until verified directly. BionicM's exact founding/incorporation year is inconsistent across the sources this piece checked (roughly 2015–2018) and is presented here as approximate. Hanger Inc.'s clinic-network size is given as a range because the company's own materials cite different counts at different, mostly unstated dates. This piece did not attempt to verify Aether Biomedical's "200 patients" active-user figure's currency, or confirm whether BMVSS's overseas-camp figures (which range from "44 countries, 111 camps, 1 million-plus beneficiaries" to "46 countries, 45,000-plus beneficiaries" across different coverage) describe the same programme measured differently or two genuinely different totals. Nothing in this piece should be read as guidance on how to apply for ADIP, ECHS, or any other scheme discussed; anyone seeking a device or reimbursement should confirm current rules directly with DEPwD, their State Level Steering Committee, ALIMCO, BMVSS or ECHS, as applicable.
