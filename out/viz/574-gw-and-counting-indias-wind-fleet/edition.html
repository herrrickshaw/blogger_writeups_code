India's wind fleet crossed 57,443 MW in June 2026, with Gujarat, which overtook Tamil Nadu as the largest state fleet in 2023, now adding almost half of all new capacity (49% in FY2025-26). But measured against NIWE's own resource atlas — 695.5 GW of onshore potential at 120m hub height — the entire national fleet still uses barely 8 per cent of what the wind alone could support, and offshore wind, with 70 GW of technical potential off the Gujarat and Tamil Nadu coasts, has yet to commission a single megawatt.

What it means: No offshore turbine has been commissioned, and GWEC expects only around 2 GW by 2035 against about 70 GW of potential.

What to do: Re-tender the first offshore capacity on the revised schedule MNRE has said it is reconsidering.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of New and Renewable Energy should publish a monthly state-wise wind table giving cumulative capacity, fiscal-year additions and generation for every state, so a league table like this one no longer depends on press coverage and blank cells for Maharashtra and Rajasthan.
  • The National Institute of Wind Energy should keep its wind-atlas documents, with state-wise installable potential at 100m, 120m and 150m hub heights, permanently downloadable from its own website, so the 302, 695.5 and 1,163.9 GW figures can be checked against the primary source rather than secondary compilations.
  • MNRE should report each year installed wind capacity as a share of NIWE's 120m potential, state by state, so the roughly 8 per cent national utilisation figure — and which states are furthest behind their own resource — is an official number rather than a blog calculation.
  • MNRE should publish capacity repowered under the December 2023 National Repowering & Life Extension Policy, by state and year, separately from new-site additions, so it is possible to see how much of FY2025–26's acceleration came from replacing old turbines rather than new land.
  • NIWE should publish one official offshore wind potential estimate by coast, with its methodology, so the 70 GW and 91 GW figures in circulation are reconciled before the revised offshore tender is judged against them.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. State-wise installed-capacity and generation figures are drawn from press coverage of MNRE/renewable-energy-tracking data as of June 2026, not a direct MNRE dashboard pull, and some state figures (Maharashtra, Rajasthan) lack a directly sourced FY2025-26 addition or generation breakout in the reporting gathered here, hence the blank cells in the table rather than an invented number. The 302/695.5/1,163.9 GW NIWE potential figures are the same figures this blog's earlier Tamil Nadu piece cites and flags as drawn from secondary compilations of NIWE's published assessment, since niwe.res.in was not directly reachable during either piece's research; the underlying NIWE atlas documents were not independently re-verified for this piece. The 70 GW offshore potential figure and the 91 GW figure cited in this blog's earlier Tamil Nadu piece are different numbers from different sourcing and years — both are reported as stated rather than reconciled into one figure, consistent with how the Tamil Nadu piece itself flagged this same inconsistency. GWEC's 6.3 GW-in-2025 figure (calendar year) and MNRE's ~6 GW-in-FY2025-26 figure (fiscal year, April-March) describe overlapping but not identical periods and should not be read as confirming each other to the decimal.
