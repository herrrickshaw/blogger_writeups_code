NIWE's own wind atlas says Tamil Nadu has installed only about a third of its assessed onshore potential, and almost none of its offshore potential, despite being India's most mature wind state. This piece works through NIWE's numbers, what's actually been built, and why the gap between potential and installed capacity has less to do with wind resource and more to do with an ageing turbine fleet and a transmission grid built for a smaller sector.

Top line

  • 35% of NIWE's 100m onshore potential in Tamil Nadu is built: about 11,938 MW of 33,799 MW.
  • 10–15% capacity utilisation for the ageing fleet, against 25–30% for modern taller turbines on the same sites.

Updated 22 October 2026. Two updates. First, capacity: this piece puts Tamil Nadu's installed wind capacity at about 11,938 MW (31 October 2025), behind Gujarat's 14,493 MW. MNRE figures for 30 June 2026, as reported in July 2026, put Tamil Nadu at about 12,274 MW, still second to Gujarat at about 16,087 MW. That is about 36% of NIWE's 33,799 MW onshore potential (our arithmetic), so “about a third” still holds. Second, transmission: the table below says Green Energy Corridor Phase II is targeted for 2025-26. That period ended on 31 March 2026, and this blog found no report that Tamil Nadu's share was completed or extended. Separately, the Cabinet approved Green Energy Corridor Phase III on 30 September 2026 (₹1,86,405 crore, 135 GW by 2032-33); its Tamil Nadu share was not checked. What it means: Tamil Nadu has built roughly a third of the onshore wind that NIWE assesses at 100m hub height, so most of the resource is still unused.

What to do: Repower the oldest sites with taller turbines and close the grid and evacuation gaps that hold back new capacity.

National potential rises from 302 GW to 1,163.9 GW as hub height reaches 150m

What it means: Taller towers reach faster, steadier wind, so the same corridors become usable at a much larger scale.

What to do: Plan repowering and new-build sites against 120m and 150m hub heights rather than the 100m assessment.

Ageing turbines run at 10–15% capacity factor against 25–30% for modern ones

What it means: Most of the gain from repowering comes from taller, larger-rotor turbines on the same land, not from more sites.

What to do: Fast-track the state's repowering policy by settling banking-charge, grid-connectivity and evacuation disputes first.

What would fix this

  • The National Institute of Wind Energy should publish state-wise installable wind potential at 120m and 150m hub heights, including Tamil Nadu, so that the state's planning no longer rests on a 100m figure of 33,799 MW that understates the resource at the heights modern turbines use.
  • The National Institute of Wind Energy should publish its offshore wind assessment for Tamil Nadu as a primary atlas document on its own website, so that the roughly 35 GW estimate behind the revised offshore tender can be cited to NIWE rather than to varying secondary reports.
  • The Tamil Nadu Electricity Regulatory Commission should issue an order fixing the banking charges and grid-access terms that apply to wind sites during repowering, so that the two disputes developers cite as slowing the 2024 repowering policy are settled before the policy's 31 March 2030 expiry.
  • TANTRANSCO and the state distribution utility should publish a dated, circuit-km schedule for upgrading the 11kV lines that serve Tamil Nadu's wind corridor, so that evacuation capacity for repowered turbines can be tracked against the repowering programme.
  • The Tamil Nadu Energy Development Agency should report annually the wind capacity decommissioned and repowered under the 2024 policy, in MW by site, so that progress on replacing turbines running at 10–15 per cent CUF can be measured.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. This piece works from NIWE's published 100m-hub-height atlas figure for Tamil Nadu (33,799 MW); a state-specific figure at the taller 120m/150m hub heights NIWE has separately assessed nationally was not found in the sourcing gathered here, so the true installable potential at the hub heights modern turbines actually use is very likely higher than the headline number this piece uses throughout. The ~35 GW offshore figure and the ~91 GW national offshore estimate it's compared against both come from recent secondary coverage rather than a primary NIWE offshore atlas document, and offshore assessments in India have been revised across multiple studies; treat both as directional rather than final. NIWE's official site (niwe.res.in) was not directly reachable from this piece's research tools at the time of writing.
