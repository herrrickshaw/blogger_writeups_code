## Two laws, two notifications, easy to conflate

"MoPNG issued a pipeline gazette notification" undersells how much is packed into that one sentence, because it could mean either of two structurally different actions under two different laws passed 44 years apart.

The first is authorisation, under the Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board Act, 2006. PNGRB runs a bidding process and then authorises a specific entity — GAIL, a state gas company, a joint venture like Petronet or IHB Limited — to lay, build, operate and expand a named pipeline of a defined length, for a defined fluid, along a defined route. This is the regulatory and commercial approval: it fixes who gets to build the pipeline and, eventually, who can charge what tariff to move fuel through it. PNGRB publishes its own running data-bank of every pipeline it has authorised, by entity, length and status.

The second is land acquisition, under the much older Petroleum and Minerals Pipelines (Acquisition of Right of User in Land) Act, 1962 (sometimes abbreviated ARUL, PMP, or P&MP Act in the notifications themselves). Once a pipeline is authorised, the company still has to actually get onto the land it crosses — and rather than buying every parcel outright, the Act lets the Central Government declare a narrower "right of user": the right to lay, maintain and access a pipeline underground, while ownership and (mostly) surface use stay with the landowner. This runs in two notified stages: a Section 3(1) notice declaring the intention to acquire the right of user over specifically described land (triggering a 21-day objection window), followed, once objections are heard, by a Section 6(1) notice making the acquisition final. For a single national pipeline hundreds of kilometres long, this generates dozens of separate notifications as the acquisition proceeds village by village, district by district, sometimes years apart.

Almost everything in the rest of this piece is the second kind. PNGRB's authorisation records are themselves genuinely useful public data (see §3–4) and this piece draws on them directly; but the "pipeline gazette notifications" that dominate MoPNG's own gazette output, by sheer count, are ARUL Act right-of-user notices, not PNGRB authorisations.

This distinction is drawn from secondary legal-explainer sources (Lexology, Bhatt & Joshi Associates) rather than a primary reading of both Acts side by side; the Acts' own texts are consistent with this framing but this piece did not cross-check every procedural detail (e.g. exact objection-hearing timelines) against the bare statute.

## What all this notification traffic is actually building: the national gas grid

The pipelines generating these notifications are not abstract. PNGRB's own published data-bank puts India's total authorised natural gas pipeline network at 34,803 km as of 31 March 2026: 33,027 km of common-carrier lines, 997 km of tie-in connectivity and 779 km of dedicated lines. Of that, 27,905 km was operational and 8,551 km under construction or partly commissioned, both figures including sub-transmission lines owned by city gas companies. A year earlier the picture was different: snapshots from 2024 and 2025 gave 33,500 to 34,233 km authorised and roughly 25,430 km operational, so about 2,500 km came into operation in nine months. Treat the total as a moving target that grows with each new authorisation. The government's own stated ambition is a ~35,000 km network, part of a broader target to raise natural gas's share of India's energy mix from roughly 6.3% to 15% by 2030.

Alongside natural gas, India's petroleum product pipeline network runs to roughly 18,858 km operational plus about 1,401 km under construction, according to industry-press coverage of PNGRB's own figures. And as of August 2026, PNGRB authorised three new common-carrier LPG pipeline projects — all to be built by GAIL, together worth roughly ₹7,000 crore and covering about 1,800 km — which the regulator says will take the country's authorised LPG pipeline network from about 7,700 km to nearly 9,500 km, a jump of roughly 23.5%. The three new LPG lines: Cherlapally (Telangana) to Nagpur (Maharashtra), 556 km; Jhansi (Uttar Pradesh) to Sitarganj (Uttarakhand), 611 km; and Shikrapur (Maharashtra) to Goa and Hubli (Karnataka), 633 km. PNGRB frames the LPG push explicitly as shifting fuel movement off road tankers and onto pipe, citing lower logistics cost, less road congestion, and reduced emissions.

## What doesn't follow from any of this

None of this shows the ARUL Act process itself is being misused, delayed unfairly, or applied inconsistently — the Act's basic design (a narrower right-of-user rather than outright acquisition, a notified 21-day objection window) is a real, longstanding legal accommodation, and nothing in this piece's research found evidence it isn't being followed as written. The pipeline schedule slippages in §4 are reported by the companies themselves in their own investor and project disclosures, not uncovered by this piece — they are a normal feature of large linear infrastructure, not a specific finding of mismanagement. And the state-by-state table in §5 should not be read as a complete or authoritative register of pipeline right-of-user activity in any of the 21 states/UTs listed: it is a lower bound, built from what open web search happened to surface, and several genuinely active projects (any pipeline whose land-acquisition notices simply weren't written up by a secondary source this piece's search could find) are certainly missing from it entirely.
