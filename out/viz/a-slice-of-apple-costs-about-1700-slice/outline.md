## 1. The barrier this is actually solving

India has roughly 23.15 crore demat accounts as of June 2026, and the pace of new account openings has been accelerating again — over 2.5 million added in June 2026 alone, the highest monthly count in months. That's a real, broad base of retail savers with somewhere to put money. The problem it doesn't solve on its own is price: owning "one share" of a company is an arbitrary unit set by that company's own share count, not a number calibrated to what an ordinary saver can afford.

The National Stock Exchange in Mumbai — the regulatory home of the 23-crore-plus demat accounts this piece says still can't legally buy a fractional slice of an Indian stock like MRF. Berkshire's Class A shares are the same story taken to an extreme: Warren Buffett has kept them unsplit since the 1960s precisely to keep short-term speculators out, which also means keeping ordinary savers out unless they can find a fractional route in.

## 2. How fractional US-stock investing actually works today

This part isn't hypothetical — Indian platforms (Vested, INDmoney, Winvesta and others) already run it, and the mechanism is straightforward:

- The broker holds the whole share; you hold a beneficial slice of it. When an Indian investor places an order for a fraction of a US stock, the broker buys (or already holds) a full share and splits the economic ownership internally. Several platforms use distributed-ledger-style record-keeping to track exactly who owns what fraction of each pooled share.

- Minimums are genuinely small. Platforms advertise starting amounts as low as $5-10 — meaning a saver can put ₹500-1,000 into a fractional slice of Apple, Microsoft or Berkshire the same way they'd start a mutual fund SIP.

- It runs through India's existing outward-remittance rules, not a special carve-out. Money leaves India under the RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS), capped at US$250,000 per resident individual per financial year, cumulative across all banks and linked to PAN. There's no LRS carve-out for "small" fractional purchases — the same ceiling and paperwork apply whether you're buying $10 or $10,000 of stock.

- It isn't free of friction or cost. Remittances above ₹10 lakh in a financial year attract 20% Tax Collected at Source (TCS) — reclaimable against your income tax return, but a real upfront cash-flow cost in the meantime — on top of whatever FX conversion spread and brokerage the platform charges.

None of this requires Indian company law to change, because the shares being fractionalised are foreign-listed. The custody and splitting happens on infrastructure India doesn't control and doesn't need to legislate for.

## 3. Why the same trick doesn't work for Indian stocks — yet

Fractional ownership of an Indian-listed share is a different problem, and it's a legal one, not a plumbing one. The Companies Act, 2013 requires share ownership in whole units, and SEBI's own regulations reinforce that. A broker cannot simply do for MRF what Vested does for Berkshire — split one share into beneficial fractions internally — because Indian company law doesn't currently recognise fractional shareholding as valid.

This has been on the table before and failed: SEBI rejected a similar fractional-ownership proposal in its regulatory sandbox in 2021, specifically over unresolved questions about who legally custodies a fraction of a share. The Company Law Committee recommended in 2022 that the Companies Act be amended to permit issuance and transfer of fractional shares in dematerialised form — that amendment has not been made.

What changed in 2025 is narrower than "fractional shares are now legal," and worth stating precisely rather than rounding up: in July 2025, SEBI admitted a Bengaluru-based startup, Xaults, into its innovation sandbox to test a new custody model — holding the fractional entitlement at the depository level (NSDL/CDSL) rather than at the broker level, so ownership legally sits with the investor or the depository rather than being an internal broker ledger entry.

Xaults co-founder Neeraj Singh described the approach as working "together with the depository and enable it at the depository level... ownership will still sit in the user's account or directly with the user." The company had a three-to-four-month window to demonstrate the model to SEBI and market participants, with a graduation to full regulatory sandbox testing — and a specific depository partner — contingent on SEBI's assessment afterward. As of this writing, this is a live pilot, not a completed rulemaking, and no announcement of the pilot's outcome was found.

A claim worth flagging and setting aside: several crypto-industry news sites (not any mainstream Indian financial outlet, and nothing found on SEBI's own press-release listings) have reported that SEBI has already launched "tokenized" fractional shares of Reliance Industries for retail investors. That claim could not be independently verified against SEBI's official communications or mainstream Indian financial press, and is not treated as fact in this article. The verified, sourced development is the Xaults sandbox admission described above — a real but much earlier-stage and narrower step than a live tokenized-RIL-shares product.

## 4. What this means for financial inclusion, honestly

The inclusion case for fractional investing is real, but it's currently asymmetric. It's already solved for accessing foreign high-priced stocks — an Indian saver with ₹1,000 and a functioning LRS remittance route can genuinely own a sliver of Berkshire Hathaway or Apple today, which was not meaningfully possible for a retail investor a decade ago. That's not a small thing for a market with 23+ crore demat accounts and a growing SIP culture, most of whose holders are working with far less than ₹1 lakh at a time.

What it hasn't solved is the domestic version of the same problem. MRF is the extreme example, but the underlying issue — high per-share prices as an unintentional wealth filter on which Indian companies an ordinary saver can own directly, one whole share at a time — sits entirely on the Indian side of the barrier, and closing it needs the Companies Act amendment the Company Law Committee already recommended in 2022, not just a clever custody workaround.

The Xaults sandbox pilot is the first real regulatory motion toward that outcome since SEBI's 2021 rejection, but "admitted to a sandbox to demonstrate a concept over three to four months" is a meaningfully earlier stage than "fractional Indian shares are now available to retail investors" — and it's worth being precise about which of those two things is actually true right now.

Verdict: Inclusion via fractional investing is real but currently one-directional. Indian savers can already buy fractional slices of expensive US stocks through existing apps, LRS limits, and ordinary FX/TCS costs — a genuine, working reduction in the price barrier to global blue chips. Fractional ownership of expensive Indian stocks is still not legal, is going through its first real regulatory sandbox test only as of mid-2025, and needs a Companies Act amendment that hasn't happened yet. Treat "fractional Indian shares are coming" as a real but early-stage regulatory process, not a launched product — and treat any claim that it has already shipped, including the tokenized-Reliance-shares reports circulating in crypto press, as unverified until SEBI itself confirms it.

### How much market risk does each of these 38 actually carry?

Price alone says nothing about risk, so this article calculated beta for every stock in the table above against the Nifty 50, using the standard method: gather weekly closing prices for the stock and the index, convert both to period returns, then Beta = Covariance(stock returns, market returns) ÷ Variance(market returns) — equivalent to the slope of a straight line fit through a scatter plot of the stock's returns against the market's. Over just these six weeks, Covariance(MRF, Nifty) works out to 0.75 and Variance(Nifty) to 2.20, giving a beta of 0.34 — but six weeks is far too short a window to trust; this article's actual figures below use the full three years (157 weekly observations) the same way, which is what a reliable beta calculation requires. MRF's real three-year beta comes out to 1.06, essentially in line with the market, not the low-volatility 0.34 the six-week snapshot alone would suggest — a direct illustration of why a short window can mislead.

Reading beta: Beta = 1.0 means the stock has historically moved in the same direction and roughly the same magnitude as the market. Beta above 1.0 means it has moved more than the market — a beta of 1.5 has historically meant roughly 15% moves against the market's 10%, in both directions. Beta below 1.0 means calmer, more stable moves than the market; utility and steady-demand consumer businesses often land here. Beta near 0 means little historical relationship to market moves at all. A negative beta, moving opposite to the market, is rare and none of the 38 stocks below have one.

Ninety percent of listed stocks, by count, already trade under ₹1,000 a share — an investor with ₹1,000 can already buy a whole share of the large majority of what's listed, no fractional access required.

But those cheap stocks carry only 42% of the market's total value; the ₹1,000–3,000 band alone, just 6.8% of stocks by count, carries nearly as much combined market cap as the entire sub-₹1,000 universe. The price barrier this article is actually about isn't the whole market — it's concentrated in roughly 530 stocks (under 10% of the exchange by count) that between them hold close to 60% of India's total listed value, MRF and the rest of Section 5's club being the extreme end of that same slice.

That concentration suggests a specific, small-ticket shape fractional access could realistically take, sized to how Indians already move money digitally rather than to a lump-sum "investment" amount. NPCI's own July 2026 UPI statistics show 741 banks live on the network processing ₹29,87,880 crore across 23,658 million transactions that month — an average of about ₹1,263 per transaction, blended across person-to-person and person-to-merchant.

Worldline's India Digital Payments Report narrows that further: the average ticket size specifically for UPI person-to-merchant payments — the kirana-store, food-delivery, mobility and utility-bill spending that maps onto ordinary daily consumption — was ₹589 in the first half of 2025, down from ₹643 a year earlier, even as the number of such payments kept growing. Indians are already routinely moving ₹500–1,300 a transaction through UPI for everyday spending, multiple times a day, without treating it as a meaningful financial decision each time.

Call the idea tiffin investing: a fractional-investing habit sized and timed like a daily tiffin order rather than a monthly SIP — an optional ₹0–1,000-a-day auto-invest, set to whatever a saver would otherwise have spent that day on ordinary digital consumption, redirected into a fractional slice of an index fund or a chosen stock instead.

The appeal isn't a new mechanism — SIPs already prove Indians will automate small recurring investments — it's the ticket size: today's smallest practical SIP tickets sit at ₹100–500 monthly minimums on most platforms, whereas a tiffin-investing product sized to match actual daily UPI P2M spend (₹589 on average) would let someone redirect a single day's discretionary payment into the market on the days they choose to, rather than committing to a fixed monthly debit regardless of that month's cash flow. This is this article's own proposed framing, not a product SEBI, any exchange, or any broker has announced — unlike the Xaults sandbox pilot detailed in Section 3, which is a real, dated regulatory process, tiffin investing as named here is a synthesis of the price-segmentation data above and the UPI spending data cited, offered as one concrete shape fractional Indian-stock access could take if and when the underlying Companies Act barrier is actually cleared. Treat it as an idea to watch for, not a claim that it exists.

Lined up against the median betas from the fully-computed table above, the honest answer is no, not in this window — and the clearest evidence is that two bands share almost the same beta and produced opposite outcomes.

The under-₹1,000 band and the ₹5,000–10,000 band both carried the same median beta (0.93) — identical market risk, by this measure — yet the under-₹1,000 band lost a median 13.7% over the trailing year while the ₹5,000–10,000 band gained a median 12.7%, a 26-point spread between two bands standard theory would expect to behave similarly precisely because their risk profiles match.

Every other band (₹1,000–3,000 at beta 1.05, ₹3,000–5,000 at 1.08, above-₹10,000 at 1.10) carried somewhat higher risk and posted solidly positive median returns (+10.4%, +22.5%, +8.1% respectively) — so it isn't that higher beta failed to pay off across the board, it's specifically that the cheapest band underperformed a same-beta peer by a wide margin.

Standard theory (CAPM) only claims higher beta should be compensated with higher average return over a long run of history, and doesn't promise that two bands with matching beta will perform alike in any given year, especially once other differences between those bands (company size, sector mix, liquidity, the very different 36% vs. 87% data-completion rates behind each figure) are considered.

This does not mean beta is meaningless — but it is a real, current illustration of why matching beta doesn't mean matching outcome, and why a saver drawn to the cheapest stocks in the market wasn't automatically compensated for whatever risk they were actually taking on, beta or otherwise.

The mean-vs-median gap inside the under-₹1,000 band is its own finding: mean 1-year return for that band was +10.3% even as the median was −13.7% — a wide gap that only happens when a small number of large winners pull the average up while most stocks in the band actually lost money. That pattern is exactly what "buying the cheapest stock you can find" as a strategy runs into: the band's average outcome looks fine only because of outliers most individual buyers won't happen to pick.
