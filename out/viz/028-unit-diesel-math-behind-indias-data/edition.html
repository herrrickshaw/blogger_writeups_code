India is building two new diesel-dependent industries at the same time — a gigawatt-scale AI data-centre pipeline and a five-fold expansion of telecom tower sites for 5G — right as the economics of backup power tip decisively against diesel. Diesel generation costs roughly $0.28 a kilowatt-hour against grid power at $0.085 and solar-charged battery storage at $0.076–0.08. Telecom towercos have already proven the alternative works at scale; data centres, still writing their backup-power playbook, have not yet caught up.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Tower operators already run tens of thousands of sites without diesel

What it means: Indus has converted its 50,000th cell site to run diesel-free, and has installed solar at roughly 30,000 sites.

What to do: Treat the tower operators' phase-out, where the grid is robust, as the template for data-centre backup planning.

Gas gensets cost about half as much to fuel as diesel gensets

What it means: A gas genset's fuel cost is about half a diesel genset's, though its upfront cost runs about 50 per cent higher.

What to do: Sites with frequent or long outages inside PNG-served areas should price a gas genset against diesel on running cost.

A diesel rotary UPS has about a third of a battery UPS's operating cost

What it means: On a 1.5 MW system the diesel rotary UPS pays back in nine months, purely on lower operating cost.

What to do: Run a DRUPS-versus-battery payback on each site's own load before fixing the backup architecture.

What would fix this

  • The Department of Telecommunications should require tower companies to report fleet-wide annual diesel consumption and the number of diesel-free sites, so that the sector’s progress is measured by a current figure rather than by milestones from 2016 and earlier, which is all this piece could find.
  • The Central Electricity Authority should publish a levelised-cost comparison of diesel gensets, solar-charged battery storage and gas gensets specific to data-centre and telecom-tower loads, so that the only Indian benchmark is no longer a Tamil Nadu commercial and industrial study that does not reflect near-continuous, high-uptime loads.
  • The Tamil Nadu government should extend the land-saving G+5 stacking allowance in its Data Centre Policy 2021 to battery storage and gas gensets on equal terms, so that the state’s design concession stops favouring diesel alone for the operating life of facilities built in this decade.
  • PNGRB should require city-gas distribution entities to report piped-gas connections serving backup gensets at data centres and telecom sites as a separate category in each authorised geographical area, so that the missing demand signal for gas backup can be tracked once it appears.
  • The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology should require data centres seeking any central incentive to disclose their backup-power architecture and annual diesel use, so that design choices locked in during the 2026–2031 build-out are visible before they become decades-long commitments.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. Data-centre capacity forecasts for 2030 vary by more than 2.5x across sources cited here (6 GW to 17 GW), reflecting genuinely different methodologies and assumptions, not a typo or an error to be resolved into one number — this piece reports the range rather than picking a winner. The Auroville Consulting BESS-vs-diesel cost comparison is a Tamil Nadu-specific commercial/industrial-sector study, not a data-centre- or telecom-tower-specific one, and is used here as the best available Indian LCOE/LCOS comparison rather than a claim that data centres or towers would see identical numbers; their much larger, near-continuous loads and stricter uptime requirements would need their own costing. The DRUPS-vs-SUPS payback comparison is drawn from one vendor/industry source describing a 1.5 MW reference system, not an independently audited multi-project average. Telecom tower diesel-elimination figures (Indus's 50,000th diesel-free site, Bharti Infratel's sub-litre-per-day towers) describe milestones reached years ago (2016 and earlier), not current fleet-wide diesel usage, which this piece did not find a recent, fleet-wide figure for. No claim is made here that any specific data-centre operator has committed to diesel elimination on Indus Towers' timeline or scale — the comparison is offered as a precedent from an adjacent, structurally similar Indian infrastructure sector, not as a description of the data-centre industry's actual current plans. The ₹6.06-vs-₹12.6-per-kVA-hour gas-vs-diesel genset comparison in Section 5 is a general Indian genset cost comparison from a Centre for Science and Environment feasibility study, not one scoped to data centres or telecom towers specifically, and this piece could not independently verify the primary CSE report's own methodology beyond the figures as reported by a secondary industry source citing it. No specific instance of a gas- or CBG-fuelled genset actually deployed at an Indian data centre or telecom tower was found during research for this piece — Section 5 describes a technically available, cost-plausible option built on existing city-gas and CBG-distribution infrastructure, not a documented current practice. The vendor list in Section 6 is drawn from public product pages and industry-press coverage, not price quotes or capacity confirmations obtained directly from each company, and inclusion is not an endorsement of any product; a real buyer would need current pricing, lead times, and site-specific emissions compliance directly from the manufacturer.
