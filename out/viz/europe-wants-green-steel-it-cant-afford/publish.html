At European gas and hydrogen prices, clean steel costs up to twice ordinary steel to make, and buyers pay a premium of only €100–150 a tonne. CBAM and the new quotas protect Europe’s mills by shutting out imports that were about €136 a tonne cheaper, while doing nothing about energy costs. A slower, simpler rollout would serve Europe’s own goals better.

Hydrogen-based EU steel costs about 1.9 times ordinary hot-rolled coil

What it means: Where hydrogen is expensive, green steel costs far more than blast-furnace steel.

What to do: Judge green steel policy by the energy prices that make it expensive, not by the border charge alone.

Gas prices set the cost of direct-reduced iron, and Europe pays the most

What it means: The gas that a direct-reduction plant burns costs more in Europe than in India or the US.

What to do: Lower European gas and power prices would do more for green steel than a higher price on imports.

With default CBAM values, imported coil costs more than EU-made coil

What it means: Imported coil is cheaper than EU coil before carbon costs, and the charge decides which side is dearer.

What to do: Allow verified plant data to count at the border so that a clean tonne is not priced as a dirty one.

A verified Indian tonne pays far less CBAM than one on default values

What it means: For small exporters the cost is mostly paperwork and verifier capacity, not carbon.

What to do: Fund and simplify emissions verification for small exporters before tightening the default values.

What would fix this

  • The European Commission should allow verification by ISO 14065-accredited bodies recognised by a partner’s national accreditation body, such as India’s NABCB, for 2026–2027 imports, while EU accreditation of third-country verifiers catches up. The India–EU technical dialogue under the trade agreement is the place to agree it, on terms open to all countries that meet the same conditions.
  • The Commission should hold the default-value mark-up at 10% for 2027, instead of raising it to 20%, for installations that have submitted unverified monitoring data, and publish route-specific defaults (blast furnace, gas DRI, coal DRI, scrap EAF) so that an Indian scrap or gas-based plant is not charged as a blast furnace by default.
  • For downstream steel products made by small firms, the Commission should accept the steel mill’s verified precursor emissions plus a standard processing allowance, so that a fastener or forging maker does not need its own installation-level verification to avoid the default.
  • The extension of CBAM to about 180 downstream products from 2028 should start with a reporting-only period of at least two years, as the original goods had from 2023 to 2025.
  • The EU should tie CBAM’s pace to progress on its own energy prices: the EUROFER target of €50 a megawatt-hour for total industrial energy costs by 2030 is a better test of whether European green steel is viable than any border charge.
  • India should notify the scheme to pay certification costs for small exporters, notify the green public-procurement mandate, and agree with the EU how a carbon price paid in India would be deducted, so that Indian revenue stays in India; the options are set out in this blog’s CBAM piece.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted, except where a body is cited.
