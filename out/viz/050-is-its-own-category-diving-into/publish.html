This blog's first price-band piece found no clean link between a stock's price and its risk, but its cheapest bucket, "under ₹1,000", held almost 4,900 stocks. This piece splits that bucket twice. The first split finds the ₹0–250 slice is the worst-performing, highest-risk quarter of the market, and that it alone holds 79% of every stock priced under ₹1,000. The second splits ₹0–250 itself into four narrower bands, to see whether the bottom of that range is uniformly bad or whether the real basement is even smaller.

NSE/BSE stocks under ₹1,000: 4,862 in 4 sub-bands, with ₹0–50 a category of its own

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • −17.7% median one-year return for stocks priced under ₹250, the only one of the four sub-bands below zero.
  • 79.2% of stocks priced under ₹1,000 sit under ₹250, where the median market cap is ₹80 crore.
  • 0.20 Sharpe ratio for stocks priced ₹0–50, the lowest of the eight price sub-bands in this piece.

Under ₹250, the median stock lost 17.7% over the past year

What it means: The median stock priced under ₹250 lost money while every higher sub-band posted a positive return.

What to do: Read the under-₹1,000 label as four separate populations rather than one bucket.

The ₹0–50 band has the weakest risk-adjusted return of the eight bands tested

What it means: The lowest-priced band combines the highest volatility and beta with the weakest return, giving the poorest Sharpe ratio.

What to do: Judge the cheapest stocks on market capitalisation and liquidity before judging them on price.

Every ₹50 band under ₹250 posted a negative median one-year return

What it means: Returns improve at each step up in price, but all four bands under ₹250 remain below zero.

What to do: Treat the gradient inside the bottom quarter of the market as a size effect before reading it as a price effect.

Over half of stocks priced under ₹250 sit below ₹50

What it means: The 2,086 stocks priced under ₹50 make up 54.2% of the under-₹250 group.

What to do: Report the price-band split alongside the count, so the bottom band is not read as typical.

What would fix this

  • NSE and BSE should publish a quarterly table of listed stocks by price band and by market-capitalisation band, with count, median market cap, median traded value, median return, volatility and beta, and the share paying any dividend, so that the ₹0–250 band’s ₹80 crore median company and the ₹0–50 band’s zero median dividend yield are visible from exchange data, not only from private screeners and one-off studies.
  • NSE and BSE should show each stock’s market capitalisation and average daily traded value next to its share price on their public quote pages and in broker data feeds, so a low share price is not read as a measure of size.
  • NSE and BSE should publish free, corporate-action-adjusted daily price histories for every listed security, including BSE-only scrips, with the days each was suspended or untraded, so gaps like the 181 of 1,267 ₹0–250 stocks without enough continuous history for a beta can be explained rather than silently dropped.
  • SEBI should require brokers’ order screens to show market capitalisation band, average daily traded value and trailing dividend next to the price, using one standard definition across brokers, at least for stocks quoted under ₹250, so that a low price tag is not read as a proxy for value when it tracks small size and high beta instead.
  • SEBI should direct the exchanges to publish an annual report on retail participation by price band, showing unique investors and retail share of turnover, so that it is measurable how many savers sit in the segment with the worst Sharpe ratio (0.20) and median 1-year return (−22.7%) found here.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

This analysis is based on historical price and fundamentals data cited above. It is provided for informational and research purposes only and does not constitute investment advice. Local price-history coverage was partial in every band (roughly 80–91% of priced stocks returned a computable beta, itself typically a minority of each band's full count once stocks lacking any NSE/BSE ticker are included) — a limitation shared with, and consistent in scale to, the earlier pieces in this series. In section 1, 1,086 of the 1,267 priced ₹0–250 stocks had enough continuous weekly history for a reliable beta, a real but partial sample of the full 3,850-stock band.

Past risk-adjusted returns are backward-looking and specific to the window measured, not a prediction. Thinly traded microcap stocks, which this price range is disproportionately made up of, can show large price swings on very low volume; individual-stock figures within these bands should be treated with particular caution beyond the aggregate patterns discussed here.
