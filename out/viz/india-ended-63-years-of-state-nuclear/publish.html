On 21 December 2025, a new law was enacted to end six decades of exclusive state control over who can build a nuclear power plant in India. Private Indian companies will be able to apply for a licence once the Centre brings it into force. Foreign-incorporated ones still can't — and that exclusion is doing more work than the headline "India opens nuclear to private investment" suggests.

Nuclear power in India: law ending the 63-year state monopoly is not yet in force

Top line

  • 100 GW is the 2047 target, an eleven-fold rise from 9 GW today; the 20+ GW of private projects are announced, not built.
  • Not in force SHANTI is enacted, but its licensing rules are still in draft and no private licence has been reported.
Updated 9 November 2026. The Act is enacted but, on the public record, not yet in force. Section 1(2) leaves its start to a notification by the Centre. A legal-tracking site that read the Department of Atomic Energy’s own Acts and Rules page on 28 October 2026 found no commencement notification. The draft SHANTI Rules and Regulations of 14 August 2026 (comments closed 4 September) have not been made final. On 23 July 2026 the government told the Rajya Sabha that private licence applications would be considered only after the rules are notified. No private licence has been reported. So private companies cannot yet apply; the wording below that said they “can now apply” and that the law had “taken effect” has been corrected. Sources: Rajya Sabha unstarred question 487 (23 July 2026, DAE), Juris Eagle (28 October 2026), DAE draft rules. The headline and standfirst, which said the law had ended the monopoly, have been changed: SHANTI has been enacted but not yet brought into force.
Updated 28 October 2026. Checked against the Act in the Gazette of India (Act No. 39 of 2025, assented 20 December 2025, published 21 December, CG-DL-E-21122025-268697). Confirmed: the licensing of “any other company” and joint ventures, with foreign-incorporated companies excluded by definition; AERB’s statutory footing (section 7); the fuel-cycle threshold (section 3(2)(b)); recourse only by written contract or intentional act (section 6); and the repeal of the 1962 and 2010 Acts (section 91). Corrected: the Act does not hold back unspecified “activities of a sensitive nature”; section 3(5) lists the activities reserved to the government. Not confirmed: the Act comes into force on dates the Centre notifies (section 1(2)); this blog did not find that notification in the Gazette index up to February 2026, so the “21 December 2025” start date used below is as reported, not as notified. The draft SHANTI Rules were not found in the Gazette index either.
Updated 22 October 2026. This piece says uranium conversion, refining and enrichment are open to private firms up to a threshold the government has yet to set. The Department of Atomic Energy published draft SHANTI Rules on 14 August 2026, with comments invited until 4 September. Summaries of the draft describe a single licence to build, own, operate and decommission a plant, and would let private firms and joint ventures make nuclear fuel. One summary reports that the draft keeps uranium and thorium enrichment, isotope separation and spent-fuel management with the government. If so, the fuel-cycle opening described below is narrower in practice. No threshold value was found, and the final rules have not been seen. Sources: Vision IAS, Civilsdaily, Shankar IAS. The original text below is unchanged.
Updated 18 October 2026. This piece describes the supplier-liability fix as a separate reform still working its way through government. Legal commentary on the SHANTI Act (law.asia, JSA) says the Act itself does not carry over the operator’s statutory right of recourse against suppliers under section 17(b) of the 2010 law, leaving recourse to written contracts and to intentional acts; summaries differ on exactly how far the change goes. A petition filed in the Supreme Court in February 2026 challenges it (SC Observer). The Department of Atomic Energy also released draft SHANTI Rules on 14 August 2026, setting operator liability caps from ₹3,000 crore for the largest reactors down to ₹100 crore for the smallest, with the government covering damage above the cap. Whether this brings foreign vendors back to Jaitapur and Kovvada is still open, but the legal change is not pending in the way described below. The Act’s gazetted text has since been read (28 October 2026): section 6 gives the operator a right of recourse only where a written contract provides it or the incident results from an act or omission intended to cause nuclear damage. The original text below is unchanged.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Reaching 100 GW by 2047 means an eleven-fold rise from 9 GW today

What it means: The split gives NPCIL the largest share, with NTPC and private developers adding the rest, so the target depends on all three.

What to do: Track each developer's commissioned capacity against its announced share so the 2047 target can be checked.

SHANTI cleared Parliament in under a week, yet its licensing rules are still in draft

  1. 15 December 2025Bill introduced in the Lok Sabha
  2. 17 December 2025Passed by the Lok Sabha
  3. 18 December 2025Passed by the Rajya Sabha
  4. 20 December 2025Presidential assent
  5. 21 December 2025Published in the Gazette of India as Act No. 39 of 2025
  6. 23 July 2026Government tells the Rajya Sabha private applications wait for the rules
  7. 14 August 2026Draft SHANTI Rules published; comments closed 4 September
  8. 28 October 2026No commencement notification found on the DAE Acts page

What it means: The Act was passed quickly, but the public record shows no commencement notification and no final rules, so private companies cannot yet apply.

What to do: Publish the commencement notification and final SHANTI Rules, and state the date from which private licence applications are accepted.

Foreign reactor projects stay stalled on supplier liability, not on the ownership ban

What it means: Both planned foreign projects have sat stalled for over a decade because vendors will not accept open-ended supplier liability, which SHANTI does not fix.

What to do: Pass the separate supplier-liability reform so that foreign vendors can bid for Jaitapur and Kovvada.

What would fix this

  • The Department of Atomic Energy should notify the threshold value up to which private entities may undertake uranium conversion, refining and enrichment under the SHANTI Act, so that the fuel-cycle opening the Act announces becomes an operable rule rather than a provision awaiting a number.
  • The Department of Atomic Energy should publish the list of “activities of a sensitive nature” that remain under exclusive government control, so that private and joint-venture applicants know the boundary of what they may be licensed for before committing to projects.
  • The Atomic Energy Regulatory Board should publish a register of licence applications from private companies and joint ventures, with capacity, site and status, so that the 20+ GW of announced private projects can be tracked against what has actually been applied for and licensed.
  • The Department of Atomic Energy should release its draft supplier-liability reform for public consultation with a stated timeline, so that the separate fix that could unlock Jaitapur and Kovvada is visible as a document with dates rather than conflated with the ownership reform already in law.
  • NPCIL and NTPC should each publish an annual project-by-project pipeline towards their 50 GW and 30 GW shares of the 100 GW target, by stage from site approval to commissioning, so that progress from today’s 9 GW is measured in capacity being built, not announced.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
