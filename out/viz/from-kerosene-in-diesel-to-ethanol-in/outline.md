Fuel theft in India has changed shape with each change in what was cheap and where it could be hidden. For three decades the main fraud was subsidised kerosene, poured into diesel. Then pipelines became targets for gangs with drills and tunnels, pumps for fraudsters with chips in their dispensers, and diesel tanks for “biodiesel” that was mostly industrial solvent. In November 2026 a new argument began, over whether petrol in Karnataka holds more ethanol than the law allows. This piece sets out that history, the record of what was proved and what was only alleged, and where the Karnataka dispute stands.

## Kerosene: the subsidy that fed adulteration

For decades the government sold kerosene through ration shops at a fraction of the price of diesel. The two fuels are similar enough that kerosene could be mixed into diesel with little effect a customer would notice, and the price gap made it profitable. A 2005 study for the oil ministry by the National Council of Applied Economic Research (NCAER) estimated that about 38% of public distribution system (PDS) kerosene was diverted; the Rangarajan committee on fuel pricing cited the figure in 2006, and the IMF put it at “almost 40%”. In 2010 the Kirit Parikh expert group said 35% or more was diverted, partly into diesel, and warned that adulteration could undo more than Rs 20,000 crore of refinery investment in cleaner Euro III and IV diesel. A study of Maharashtra for 2004-05 estimated that 50 of the 118 million litres allocated each month went to other markets, and noted that distribution there was often controlled by organised crime.

The government tried to make diverted kerosene detectable. It was dyed blue, which could be removed. A furfural marker proved unstable. In January 2007 the oil ministry ordered all kerosene doped with a chemical marker at 5 parts per million, enforced through the Motor Spirit and High Speed Diesel Control Order of 2005. In September 2008 the Indian Oil Corporation’s vigilance wing found a brown powder that removed the marker, and doping was stopped from 1 January 2009, according to the ministry’s affidavit quoted by the Supreme Court in 2018. An IISD study put the imported marker’s cost at about $33 million a year.

Two killings made the trade national news. On 19 November 2005 S. Manjunath, a 27-year-old Indian Oil sales officer and IIM Lucknow graduate, was shot dead at a pump in Gola, Lakhimpur Kheri, whose sales he had suspended in September for selling adulterated fuel. A trial court sentenced the pump owner’s son, Pawan Kumar “Monu” Mittal, to death in 2007 and seven others to life; the Allahabad High Court commuted the death sentence and acquitted two, and in March 2015 the Supreme Court upheld life terms for six. In January 2011 Yashwant Sonawane, Additional Collector of Malegaon, was set on fire near Manmad while filming kerosene being taken from a tanker. Police said about 300 tankers were filled daily at the Manmad depots and that gangs opened them with duplicate keys. Maharashtra raided depots across the state in the following weeks. The main accused died of burns he suffered in the attack, and a Malegaon court sentenced three men to life imprisonment in September 2022, Indian Mandarins reported.

The kerosene channel has largely closed. As cooking gas spread and states gave up their PDS kerosene quotas, volumes fell, though this blog’s [diesel piece](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/08/diesel-doesnt-move-cpi-it-moves-40.html) notes that PDS kerosene was still 73% of the kerosene India consumed in May 2026.

## Pipelines: drills, valves and tunnels

Pipelines carry crude and products across thousands of kilometres of farmland, and gangs tap them by digging down, fitting a valve and running a hose to a tanker or a storage pit. In 2017 about 1 lakh litres were stolen from the Mathura–Jalandhar line near Mathura through a tunnel, ET Energy reported, and police described a racket of about Rs 100 crore, a figure that was not independently verified. Indian Oil said in 2021 that it had recorded 34 pilferage attempts and 53 arrests in 2020-21, and that it now watches pipelines with SCADA systems, an intrusion-detection system along the line and drones on a 120 km stretch. State oil-security committees chaired by each director general of police meet every quarter. Parliament stiffened penalties for damaging or tapping pipelines in an amendment to the Petroleum and Minerals Pipelines Act passed in 2011; Indian Oil refers to it as the 2012 amendment. A draft Petroleum (Amendment) Bill, 2026, released by the oil ministry for comment on 30 September 2026, would add a separate crime of damaging petroleum facilities or pilfering from them, reported as up to five years or a fine of up to Rs 15 crore for a first offence, and up to ten years for damage to notified “critical petroleum infrastructure”. Comments closed on 30 October; the draft has not been introduced in Parliament ([this blog’s piece on the draft](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/10/indias-petroleum-act-still-fines.html)).

The cases have not stopped. In 2024 the CBI registered a case against two Hindustan Petroleum assistant managers at the Mathura terminal over alleged pilferage; this is an allegation, not a conviction. In January 2025 Rajasthan police said a gang had leased land near Kotputli-Behror, built a wall and dug a 100 m tunnel to an Indian Oil crude line, which it tapped for four months. In December 2025 Delhi Police arrested two men over a tap on a Hindustan Petroleum diesel line near Jaipur, and said they had run such thefts for about 30 years across four states. In 2026, 12 people were arrested over a 40-foot illegal pipe on an Indian Oil line at Pachewar in Tonk in July, and 8 over a tap on a crude pipeline near Balotra in August, where police said about 20 tonnes of crude was taken.

## Diesel pilferage: trucks, trains and generator sets

Most diesel theft is smaller and closer to the user than a pipeline tap, and it rarely reaches national statistics. Its commonest form is siphoning from the tanks of parked trucks. In March 2024 Nalgonda police in Telangana arrested six men who, they said, broke the locks on the tanks of trucks parked on highways at night while drivers slept, and took over 2,400 litres in 17 thefts in a month, carrying it in cans to Andhra Pradesh for sale (Deccan Chronicle). In 2021 Prakasam police in Andhra Pradesh arrested a gang from Madhya Pradesh for similar thefts on the Chennai–Kolkata highway (The Hindu), and in November 2026 police in Bemetara, Chhattisgarh, said they had broken an inter-district gang stealing diesel from parked heavy vehicles (ETV Bharat, Hindi; search snippet only). Highway eateries have long been part of the trade: the dhaba where Yashwant Sonawane’s team seized 4,000 litres of kerosene and 3,000 litres of petrol in 2010 belonged to the man later accused of his killing.

Tankers are the second target, because each carries thousands of litres. In January 2024 Mumbai police registered a case against 11 people accused of drawing diesel from Indian Oil tankers and selling it to a tug operator at Mumbai port at Rs 65 a litre; police said they took 150–200 litres a day and seized about 12,000 litres (Free Press Journal). In Goa, Mormugao police seized five tankers and 12 barrels in May 2025 over alleged siphoning (Times of India). One of the two men arrested in December 2025 over pipeline taps, police said, began in the early 1990s as a tanker driver drawing diesel from his own load (The Economic Times).

How much fleets lose is not measured by any public body. Companies that sell fuel sensors for trucks commonly put losses in unmonitored Indian fleets at 5–15% of fuel spend, and two vendors attribute an estimate of about 8% of diesel filled to the trade magazine MotorIndia, which could not be checked. These are marketing figures, and the same vendors say much of the loss is waste from idling and poor maintenance rather than theft.

Railways lose diesel from locomotives and from the generator cars that power coaches. The Railway Protection Force (RPF) books these cases under the Railway Property (Unlawful Possession) Act. Reported cases are small: in Guwahati, four people were arrested in April 2025 over diesel taken from a train’s generator van, four more in June 2026 after a video of siphoning from a power car spread on social media, and seven in September 2026 over about 150 litres, valued at Rs 14,250, taken from a power car on a sick line (The Sentinel; Pratidin Time). In July 2026 RPF at Jhansi arrested four people and the buyer over about 300 litres (Amar Ujala, Hindi; search snippet only). In May 2026 Talcher police in Odisha arrested nine people and seized 1,120 litres taken from a coal train standing inside a mine for loading (OdishaTV).

Mobile-tower sites with diesel generators are a third target. In May 2025 police in Ri Bhoi, Meghalaya, arrested three men with 220–230 litres in jerrycans; police said they had confessed to siphoning it from three Reliance Jio towers (The Sentinel). Most reported tower thefts, though, are of radio equipment rather than fuel. Mines are a fourth: answering a Lok Sabha question in March 2021, the coal ministry said NLC India’s vehicle tracking helps it manage diesel pilferage, and that Singareni Collieries had put CCTV cameras at its opencast diesel bunkers.

The incentive has grown in 2026. With crude above $100, oil companies charge bulk buyers more than Rs 40 a litre above the retail pump price, as this blog’s [diesel piece](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/08/diesel-doesnt-move-cpi-it-moves-40.html) sets out. Bulk buyers moved to pumps, and private retailers rationed sales to them. A gap that size rewards anyone who can move retail-priced diesel into bulk tanks. In Bengaluru in 2025 the city’s Central Crime Branch arrested four people for siphoning diesel meant for city and state bus depots, seizing about 60,000 litres from three tankers; police said about 50 litres were drawn from each tanker using duplicate keys, Deccan Herald reported.

## Pumps: chips and short delivery

The cheapest theft needs no adulterant: deliver less fuel than the meter shows. In April 2017 Uttar Pradesh’s Special Task Force raided pumps in Lucknow and found electronic chips in dispensers that cut delivery while showing the full amount. Police estimated each tampered pump made about Rs 14 lakh a month, from chips costing about Rs 3,000; these are police estimates. The oil ministry told the Lok Sabha in July 2017 that 101 cases of chips or other high-tech tampering had been found between April 2015 and June 2017, 51 of them in Uttar Pradesh, and that 44 outlets had been terminated after joint inspections. In one case before the Allahabad High Court, nozzles were short by 200–220 ml in every 5 litres. The oil companies’ marketing discipline guidelines now require random sampling of at least 2% of outlets a month and suspend sales when a density or filter-paper test fails.

Customers can ask for two checks at any pump. A drop of petrol on filter paper should evaporate in about two minutes without a stain, and dealers must provide the paper. The fuel’s density, read with a hydrometer in a 500 ml jar and corrected to 15°C, should match the figure in the outlet’s register.

## Spurious biodiesel

When diesel prices rose in 2020-21, cheaper “biodiesel” appeared at roadside outlets. Much of it was made from furnace oil, base oil, mineral turpentine and white spirit, and sold for about Rs 50–55 a litre when diesel cost Rs 80–97. Gujarat police seized 89,000 litres at seven outlets in Rajkot in June 2020 and 37 lakh litres in Anand in July 2021, and the state banned retail sale of biodiesel on 3 July 2021. The oil ministry told the Rajya Sabha in December 2021 that it had received complaints from Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Telangana, Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu, and had written to every state’s chief secretary. At Mundra, customs found an import declared as “mixed glycol” to be diesel.

## Karnataka: more ethanol than the label?

On 6 November 2026 Karnataka’s minister for food, civil supplies and legal metrology, Rizwan Arshad, announced the results of petrol testing by the state Department of Legal Metrology. It collected 63 samples from Bengaluru, Bengaluru Rural, Kolar, Chikkaballapura, Ramanagara, Mangaluru and Tumakuru and sent 14 to accredited laboratories, with reports from the Shriram Research Institute in Bengaluru. 11 of the 14 showed more than 20% ethanol, the highest 31.2% at an Indian Oil outlet. The state said samples first sent to oil-company laboratories came back without the ethanol content disclosed.

Under the Indian standard for E20 petrol (IS 17021), ethanol must be between 19% and 21%. On the state’s figures, 9 of the 14 samples were above that band and one, at 13.5%, below it. The state argues that most pump equipment is designed for up to 15% ethanol and that vehicles are certified for no more than E20. It has asked for action against the oil companies, a fund to compensate motorists for damage, ethanol content displayed at pumps, a choice of blends and batch-wise blending records, and has empowered officials from the Food Commissioner to police officers of deputy superintendent rank to take samples under the 2005 Control Order. Rahul Gandhi called it “420-petrol chori”, The Hindu reported.

Indian Oil, Bharat Petroleum and Hindustan Petroleum rejected the findings in a joint statement, and the petroleum ministry issued a release on 7 November. Their main points:

- The samples were taken in October without oil-company representatives, and neither retained samples nor the lab reports were shared. The Control Order requires six one-litre samples, two each for the dealer, retention and testing, under jointly signed labels.

- The laboratory is not one specified under the Control Order. Its certificates cite gas chromatography but not the method, dilution or reference correction that IS 17021 requires.

- The reported densities, 0.742–0.749 g/cc, are below the 0.752–0.760 the companies find in their own E20 surveys. Ethanol is denser than petrol, so a higher ethanol share should raise density, not lower it.

- Their own surveys covered 893 samples in 27 states and union territories (Indian Oil 330, BPCL 280, HPCL 283), all within 19–21%. The 56 Karnataka samples among them were 19–20%, averaging 19.30%.

The density point is the most testable claim on either side, and it cuts against the state’s results: if the reported densities are right, the samples are hard to square with 25–31% ethanol. The companies’ procedural points are about chain of custody, not chemistry. Neither side has yet published full lab reports. A joint retest under the Control Order procedure, with witnesses and retained samples, would settle most of it; neither side has yet said one is planned.

### How much would excess ethanol cost a driver?

Ethanol carries less energy than petrol, about 21.2 megajoules a litre against about 32. Near E20 each extra percentage point of ethanol cuts the energy in a litre by about 0.36%. At the average of the 11 readings above 20% (25.2%), a litre holds about 1.9% less energy than E20; at the highest reading (31.2%), about 4.1% less. For comparison, E20 holds about 6.7% less energy than pure petrol.

The minister put the cost of lost mileage at Rs 62,000 crore a year, by applying a 10% loss to 6,200 crore litres of fuel at Rs 100 a litre, and added Rs 39,000 crore of repairs. Three problems. A 10% loss is about 5 times the energy shortfall at the average reported reading, even if every litre sold were blended that high, and more than the 6.7% gap between E20 and pure petrol; the same volume and price with a 1.9% loss gives about Rs 11,700 crore. The tests cover 14 samples, not all petrol. And 6,200 crore litres is more than India’s entire petrol sales, about 5,370 crore litres on PPAC’s latest statewise data, so it cannot be Karnataka’s volume. The repair estimate came without a method. The state’s figure is better read as a ceiling on a worst case than as an estimate.

## Why ethanol changes the adulteration game

Kerosene fraud worked because a subsidised fuel was cheaper than the one it diluted. Ethanol creates a smaller version of the same gap. Oil companies buy ethanol in 2026-27 at Rs 57.97 to Rs 68.86 a litre, while petrol retails at about Rs 94.77–105. Blending happens at oil-company depots, not pumps, so extra ethanol would have to be added at the depot or in transit, which is why the state’s demand for batch-wise blending records matters. Ethanol also attracts water. If water gets into a pump’s underground tank, an ethanol-water layer can separate and sink to the bottom, where the suction pipe draws from, so the fuel delivered can vary from the fuel supplied. The 2024 marketing guidelines tell pump inspectors to test ethanol content after removing water from the tank and to stop sales if it is out of range. Whether any of the Karnataka results reflect fraud, measurement error or water in tanks cannot be told from what has been published.
