Top line

  • 11 of 14 Karnataka petrol samples the state says were above 20% ethanol; the oil companies dispute the tests.
  • 51 of 101 pump-tampering cases found between April 2015 and June 2017 were in Uttar Pradesh.
  • 1.9% energy loss at the average excess-ethanol reading, far below the state's 10% mileage figure.
Updated 9 November 2026. The pipelines section now notes the draft Petroleum (Amendment) Bill, 2026 (released for comment on 30 September 2026), which would make pilferage from and damage to petroleum facilities a separate crime; it is a draft, not law.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Pumps: about half of the chip-tampering cases were in Uttar Pradesh

What it means: The oil ministry told the Lok Sabha that 101 cases were found, with 44 outlets terminated after joint inspections.

What to do: Customers can ask for a filter-paper evaporation test and a density check at any pump, as the post advises.

Karnataka: 11 of 14 petrol samples read above 20% ethanol

What it means: On the state's figures, nine of the 14 samples were above the 21% top of the E20 band and one, at 13.5%, was below it.

What to do: A joint retest under the Control Order procedure, with witnesses and retained samples, would settle most of the dispute.

Mileage: the state's 10% loss figure is about five times the energy shortfall

What it means: Per litre, the highest reading holds about 4.1% less energy than E20, and the average of the 11 readings above 20% about 1.9% less.

What to do: The Rs 62,000 crore figure should be read as a worst-case ceiling rather than an estimate, as the post argues.

What would fix this

  • Every state sampling drive should follow the Control Order procedure, with oil-company and dealer witnesses and retained samples, so that its results can be checked rather than argued over.
  • Oil companies should publish their fuel-quality survey results by state, including ethanol content, density and the number of samples, so that the public sees the same data the companies cite in a dispute.
  • Pumps should display the ethanol content of the current tank batch next to the price, as Karnataka has asked.
  • Pipeline operators should report pilferage attempts and arrests every year, by company and state, so that it is possible to tell whether tapping is rising or falling.
  • The Railway Protection Force, tower companies and public-sector mines should publish the diesel thefts they record each year, in cases and litres, as the coal ministry already does for coal recovered in raids.
  • States should report how many dispenser inspections, short-delivery findings and terminations they make each year, building on the 2017 count of 101 chip cases.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

There is no national series of fuel theft or adulteration: the 38% kerosene estimate dates from 2005, the chip count stops in 2017 and Indian Oil’s pipeline figure covers only 2020-21. Earlier money estimates of the kerosene trade, such as about Rs 5,000 crore a year at 2005-06 prices in an IISD study, are left as dated estimates rather than updated. No national count of diesel theft from trucks, trains, towers, mines or construction sites was found, nor any RPF total for diesel; the truck-fleet loss rates are vendor claims, and no construction-site case or state police figure turned up. Most pipeline, pump and diesel cases here are arrests or police claims; outcomes in court were found only for the Manjunath and Sonawane killings, and the Sonawane verdict rests on one report. The year of the pipelines amendment is given as 2011 or 2012 in different sources. In Karnataka, neither the state’s lab reports nor the oil companies’ survey data were seen in full; both are as reported by the press and the ministry release. Whether the Rs 62,000 crore figure was meant for Karnataka or for India could not be confirmed. The energy figures above use approximate textbook heating values, and real mileage effects also depend on the engine.
