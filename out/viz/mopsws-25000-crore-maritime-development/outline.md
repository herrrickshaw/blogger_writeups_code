On 24 September 2025, the Union Cabinet approved the Guidelines for the Maritime Development Fund (MDF) — Office Memorandum No.29/CM/2025, Case No. 195/29/2025 — turning a number the finance minister had announced in the Union Budget seven months earlier into an operative rulebook. The MDF had existed since Budget 2025-26 as a headline figure, ₹25,000 crore for the maritime sector. What the Guidelines add is the machinery: how the money is split, who is allowed to touch it, what returns each investor is promised, and what a shipyard has to do to draw on it. This piece works through that machinery from the primary document — the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways' own MDF Guidelines — and checks the numbers it implies against what is publicly known about the problem it is meant to solve.

## Why MoPSW wrote this now

This blog covered the legal side of India's maritime push last week: between March and August 2025, Parliament repealed and replaced four shipping laws, the oldest dating to 1856, in one of the most complete sectoral legal overhauls in decades — and none of it moved the number that actually matters, the roughly 5% of India's own overseas trade cargo carried on Indian-flagged ships. See [India Rewrote Its Entire Maritime Legal Code in Five Months. Its Ships Still Carry 5% of Its Own Trade](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/09/india-rewrote-its-entire-maritime-legal.html) (this blog's own reporting) for that side of it. The MDF is the capital-side follow-through: government aims to lift Indian-flagged ships' share of the country's cargo to 20% by 2047, under the Maritime Amrit Kaal Vision 2047 the Guidelines cite as one of the investment framework's anchors.

The gap the IIF specifically targets is a financing-cost gap. Indian shipyards report borrowing at roughly 10–10.5% for ship construction loans, against roughly 4–8% for Chinese yards (helped by state leasing arms such as ICBC Leasing and CDB Leasing financing up to 85% of a vessel's value around 5.5%) and roughly 5–6% in South Korea. That spread alone is estimated to add 15–20% to the cost of a ship built in India before any yard-productivity difference is counted. The IIF's interest-subvention design is a direct response to that spread, not a general-purpose subsidy.

## Two funds, two jobs

  r0: Maritime Investment Fund (MIF) | 20,000 | Equity, via SEBI-registered AIF | Co-invests as growth/expansion capital in shipbuilding, ports, shipping and related projects
  r1: Interest Incentivization Fund (IIF) | 5,000 | Interest subvention/reimbursement | Cuts the effective cost of term and working-capital loans already taken by shipyards in India


They are structurally unrelated beyond sharing an umbrella name and a Governing Council. MIF is a fund that owns a piece of a company or project; IIF is a rebate on a loan a shipyard has already taken from a bank. A shipyard could in principle draw on both — equity from MIF into an expansion project, interest relief from IIF on the debt financing it — but the Guidelines treat them as separate schemes with separate implementing structures.

## How the ₹20,000 crore MIF is built

MIF is registered as a closed-end Category I/II Alternative Investment Fund with SEBI, structured as a trust under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 — it can be one AIF or several by sub-sector, and may set up IFSCA feeder funds for global investors. The Government of India's 49% contribution (₹9,800 crore of the base ₹20,000 crore) is designated concessional capital: it takes a 2% initial hurdle rate, well below what a commercial investor would expect, and absorbs the first losses on the fund up to the limit of GoI's own contribution. Other investors — institutional and private, including Major Port Authorities — sit above that cushion and target a roughly 10% cumulative return before any performance fee applies.

The waterfall the Guidelines describe, in order: return of capital to all investors pro rata; a 2% concessional return to everyone; then distributions to non-GoI investors (including Major Port Authorities) up to their ~10% hurdle, followed by GoI's own catch-up to the same ~10%; and only after that, residual profit is split between the Fund Manager and investors under an indicative 85:15 carry. GoI and Major Port Authority contributions are explicitly excluded from any catch-up provision — the Fund Manager may negotiate a catch-up with other investors, but not with the government's own tranche.

Reading the first-loss provision correctly. The Guidelines cap the first-loss absorption at GoI's contribution to the fund — 49% of whatever the corpus actually is, not a fixed rupee figure independent of corpus size. If the corpus grows via the greenshoe option below ₹20,000 crore's base, the loss cushion for commercial investors technically grows too, but so does the amount the Fund Manager must first prove it can deploy well before drawing on it.

Fundraising runs through Sagarmala Finance Corporation Limited (SMFCL), which holds GoI's contribution in a fiduciary capacity. The Guidelines set a first close of at least ₹5,000 crore within 12 months of SEBI registration, and a final close within 36 months of the first close. The corpus can be enhanced up to ₹30,000 crore through a greenshoe option if that growth would otherwise dilute GoI's 49% share below that level. Since the Guidelines were approved, the Governing Council has been constituted, the request for proposals for a fund manager was issued on 20 February 2026, and SBI Ventures was appointed Fund Manager of the MIF in May 2026 (19 May on the Directorate General of Maritime Administration’s page; a PIB-based summary gives 26 May). This blog found no report of a SEBI registration or a first close by early November 2026, so the 12-month clock to a ₹5,000 crore first close has not visibly started.

## Where MIF money is and isn't allowed to go

A minimum of 50% of the investable corpus is reserved for five priority sub-sectors — shipbuilding (including clusters), ship repair, ship ownership, port expansion, and inland water transport/coastal shipping, alongside international shipping — with an aspiration to raise that to 70% depending on the pipeline. Concentration limits apply without the Advisory Board's prior consent: no more than 15% of aggregate capital in a single portfolio company, 40% in a single maritime sub-sector, or 20% in one business group. Investment outside India is capped at 10% of investible corpus and restricted to technology- or innovation-driven projects (or companies registered in IFSC-type jurisdictions) that complement the domestic sector, and needs prior Governing Council approval. The mode of investment is equity; fund managers retain discretion on the specific instrument within that.

  r2: Fund Manager (separate Indian entity) | Runs day-to-day investment decisions, structures instruments, proposes hurdle/carry adjustments to the GC
  r3: Investment Committee | Senior Fund Manager staff (CEO, CIO, risk/sector heads), no investor representatives; approves individual investments and divestments
  r4: Advisory Board | Nominees from investors holding ≥10% of aggregate commitment; advises on conflicts, tenor extensions and investment-period changes, not day-to-day operations


Fee caps: management fee on GoI's and Major Port Authorities' contribution is capped at 0.6–0.8% per year (the Guidelines expect the lower end in practice); performance fee on the same contribution is a flat 15%. Other investors negotiate their own fee terms separately, so headline AIF economics for private capital could differ from the government-tranche numbers above.

One structure to watch, not a finding of wrongdoing. SMFCL sits in three places at once under these Guidelines: fiduciary holder of GoI's MIF contribution, chair of the Search-cum-Selection Committee that picks the MIF Fund Manager, and Implementing Agency for the separate IIF scheme (next section). The Guidelines do require Chinese-wall mechanisms and conflict-of-interest protocols (Section 2.10) where a fund manager runs other lines of business, but they do not spell out how SMFCL's own multiple roles are firewalled from each other. Worth watching once the Fund Manager is actually selected.

## The ₹5,000 crore IIF: a rebate on loans shipyards already take

The Interest Incentivization Fund runs from the Scheme's notification date through 2036, aiming to cut the cost of term (capital) loans and working-capital loans availed by shipyards located in India, covering both fresh and incremental loans sanctioned during that window. SMFCL is the designated Implementing Agency, drafting the detailed Standard Operating Procedures, empanelling banks and financial institutions, and running a digital portal for applications, approvals and claims. SMFCL's own service charge is capped at 0.75% of the ₹5,000 crore corpus across the fund's ten-year life — 0.10% in year one, roughly 0.072% a year for the remaining nine. Government of India's liability under the IIF is explicitly limited to that ₹5,000 crore corpus; the exact subvention rate and disbursement mechanism are left to the Implementing Agency's SOPs, not fixed in the Guidelines themselves.

## The bigger package MDF sits inside

The same Cabinet meeting that approved the MDF Guidelines on 24 September 2025 approved a wider ₹69,725 crore shipbuilding-maritime package, of which MDF is one of three financial pillars:

  cols: c0 Scheme | c1 Outlay (₹ crore) | c2 What it does
  r0: Maritime Development Fund (MDF) | 25,000 | Blended-finance equity (MIF) + interest subvention (IIF), covered above
  r1: Shipbuilding Financial Assistance Scheme (SBFAS) | 24,736 | Direct construction subsidy to shipyards, extended to 31 March 2036
  r2: Shipbuilding Development Scheme (SbDS) | 19,989 | Capacity expansion to 4.5 million GT/year, shipbuilding clusters, India Ship Technology Centre under the Indian Maritime University


A National Shipbuilding Mission oversees all of it, alongside a fourth, non-financial pillar of legal, taxation and policy reform — the same legislative overhaul this blog covered separately. Reading the MDF Guidelines in isolation understates the government's total financial commitment; reading ₹69,725 crore as if it were all equity or all MDF overstates what the Guidelines above actually govern.

## Does the arithmetic close the gap it's aimed at

Scale check: India's share of global shipbuilding output by gross tonnage is estimated at roughly 0.05–0.06%, against a combined China-South Korea-Japan share above 95% (China alone above 54%). India's own tonnage delivered grew from about 40,900 GT in 2024 to about 57,600 GT in 2025 — a real 41% increase, but off a base small enough that the increase itself is a rounding error against China's order book. ₹25,000 crore (roughly $2.9 billion) is a meaningful sum for a domestic industry this size, and it is not, on its own, capital at the scale of the state-backed leasing arms it is competing against.

Design check: the MIF's 2% GoI hurdle and first-loss cushion are a standard blended-finance de-risking structure, and the ~10% commercial hurdle is not an unusually low ask for infrastructure-adjacent equity in India. The open question the Guidelines leave to the Fund Manager is whether Indian maritime assets — a sector with a thin track record of institutional-quality exits — can actually clear a 12-month first close of ₹5,000 crore from investors who have rarely underwritten this asset class before. The IIF's design, by contrast, targets the financing-cost gap directly and narrowly: it is a subvention on loans that already exist, not a bet on new equity demand, which makes it the more mechanically straightforward of the two funds to judge once SOPs are published.

## What this piece does and doesn't cover

- It does not cover the separate, later SMFCL borrowing-limit approval. In November 2025, SMFCL's board/RBI approvals for the company's own NBFC borrowing ceiling (reported around ₹25,000 crore, with roughly ₹8,000 crore planned to be raised in the current financial year) were covered in the press. That is SMFCL raising debt on its own balance sheet as a lending institution — a separate transaction from the MDF's ₹25,000 crore corpus described in this piece, and the two ₹25,000 crore figures should not be added together or treated as the same commitment.
