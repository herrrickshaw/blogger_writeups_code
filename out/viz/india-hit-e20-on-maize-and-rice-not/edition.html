In 2022 the question was whether India had the feedstock for 20 per cent ethanol blending. The answer arrived in 2025-26: blending is at 20 per cent, and it was not sugarcane that got it there. Grain now supplies 72.5 per cent of allocated ethanol — maize alone 45.7 per cent, up from 6.2 per cent three years earlier — and surplus FCI rice supplies more of India’s fuel than sugarcane juice does.

India's E20 ethanol target: reached mainly with maize and rice, not sugarcane

Top line

  • 45.68% of allocated ethanol volume in ESY 2025-26 is maize, up from 6.2% in ESY 2022-23.
  • 59% of the ethanol capacity distillers offered was allocated; 728 crore litres found no buyer.
  • 72.47% of the allocation is grain-based, against 27.52% from cane.
Updated 7 November 2026. The 2026-27 tender, opened on 16 October 2026, changes the mix described below. The oil companies seek about 1,100 crore litres; they will buy no ethanol from cane juice, sugar syrup or B-heavy molasses, after a government direction to cut sugar diversion to zero; maize ethanol is paid ₹68.86 a litre instead of ₹71.86; allocation goes to the cheapest feedstock first, which puts maize last; and FCI rice is 72 lakh tonnes to June 2027, with the 40% rice floor replaced by a quarterly cap of 108 crore litres. The 2025-26 shares below remain the latest actual allocation. Details in India’s 2026-27 Ethanol Tender Drops Cane Juice, Cuts Maize by Rs 3 a Litre and Flips From Sugar-First to Cheapest-First.

What it means: Grain-based feedstocks carry nearly three quarters of the allocated volume, so cane is now the minority supplier.

What to do: Track the FCI rice release price and the mechanism that decides how much rice goes to distilleries rather than the public distribution system.

Maize rose from 6.2% to 45.68% of ethanol feedstock in three supply years

What it means: Maize moved from a marginal feedstock to the largest one in three supply years, driven by availability rather than by a maize-specific programme.

What to do: Watch maize acreage and imports, since a domestic shortfall would turn import substitution into an import line.

Distillers offered 1,776 crore litres, and 1,048 were allocated

What it means: Distillers have more capacity than oil companies are buying, so the marginal plant now competes for an allocation rather than being courted for output.

What to do: Watch whether the 728 crore litres of idle capacity drives a push for a higher blending mandate before any feedstock question arises.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should publish, for each ethanol supply cycle, volumes offered, allocated and actually delivered by feedstock, so that the 72.5% grain share and the 728 crore litres left unallocated come from an official release rather than trade reporting, and allocation is not mistaken for supply.
  • The Department of Food and Public Distribution should publish the FCI rice release price for distilleries, quantities released each year, and the rule for cutting releases in a deficit monsoon, so that the administered number setting a fifth of the blend is decided in the open.
  • The Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare should publish an annual maize balance sheet showing production, imports and use for ethanol, feed and food, so that the jump from 6.2% to 45.7% of ethanol allocation can be checked for its effect on acreage and imports.
  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should publish an assessment of what share of the vehicle fleet is compatible with blends above E20 before any higher mandate is considered, so that idle distillery capacity is not the main argument for moving past 20%.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Sources and caveats

The ESY 2025-26 cycle-1 feedstock allocation — maize 45.68 per cent and about 478.9 crore litres, surplus FCI rice 22.25 per cent and 233.3 crore litres, direct sugarcane juice 15.82 per cent and 165.9 crore litres, B-heavy molasses 10.54 per cent and 110.5 crore litres, damaged food grains 4.54 per cent and 47.6 crore litres, C-heavy molasses 1.16 per cent and 12.2 crore litres, against an allocation of about 1,048 crore litres from 1,776 crore litres offered — is as reported by ChiniMandi. The percentages sum to 99.99 and the volumes to 1,048.4 crore litres, which is the internal consistency check applied here; the grain and cane subtotals, the 59 per cent allocation rate and the 728 crore litre unplaced figure are computed from those numbers. The maize share of 6.2 per cent in ESY 2022-23, and the statement that blending has held at 20 per cent through ESY 2025-26 with monthly supply figures reported through the year, are from ChiniMandi and BioEnergy Times trade reporting. Half-year supply of about 515 crore litres against 1,059 crore litres contracted, with roughly 333 crore litres grain-based, is attributed to All India Distillers’ Association estimates reported via Business Today on 12 May 2026; note that the contracted figure of 1,059 crore litres differs slightly from the 1,048 crore litre cycle-1 allocation, which is why the two are kept separate above rather than combined. The framing question is from the Agricultural Economics Research Review title “Does India have enough feedstock to meet its E20 targets by 2025?”; AERR’s most recent listed issue is Volume 37 Number 1 (2024), so the paper predates the outcome described here and is cited for the question it posed, not for any figure. The ₹71.86 per litre maize cost figure is from this blog’s earlier piece and its own sources, not re-derived here. All feedstock allocation data is oil-marketing-company allocation, not delivered volume; allocation and actual supply diverge, and the half-year supply figures above show that divergence.
