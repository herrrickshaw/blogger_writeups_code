The short version. "Vision 2030: Natural Gas Infrastructure in India," an industry-group report submitted to PNGRB in 2013, projected India's "realistic" gas demand would grow from 242.7 MMSCMD in 2012-13 to 746 MMSCMD by 2029-30. On its own trajectory, 2023-24 demand should have reached 571 MMSCMD; actual consumption that year was about 188 MMSCMD, and FY2025-26 consumption was again about 188 MMSCMD.

PNGRB's 2025 High-Level Expert Committee, chaired by former PNGRB chairperson D K Sarraf, synthesised five demand forecasts into a base case of 260–300 MMSCMD by 2030 and 365–500 MMSCMD by 2040, rising to 630–700 MMSCMD by 2040 only if a long list of reforms is carried out. Every one of those 2040 figures is below the 2013 report's base case for 2030. The pipeline network the 2013 report called for mostly got built anyway, and runs at around half its capacity.

The 2013 report relied on LNG imports like those landed here to close India's gas demand-supply gap. How this piece was researched. Both reports were supplied as files and read in full: the 2013 "Vision 2030" report, and the 352-page 2025 "Vision 2040" committee report (split into two halves for size). Figures attributed to either report are direct citations; page numbers are the 2025 report's own printed page numbers. pngrb.gov.in, ppac.gov.in and pib.gov.in were blocked from direct fetch, so some actual-consumption figures come from trade press and an industry review citing PPAC data, and carry a lower confidence tier. Each figure's source is given where it is used.

## The 2013 report projected 746 MMSCMD of demand for 2029-30

The report's industry-group authors built their "realistic demand" projection sector by sector: power, fertiliser, city gas, industrial, petrochemicals/refineries/internal consumption, and sponge iron/steel. The total was projected to grow from 242.66 MMSCMD in 2012-13 to 746.03 MMSCMD in 2029-30, a compound annual growth rate of 6.8–6.83% (the report states both figures in different places), with gas reaching a 20% share of India's primary energy mix. This was its base case, not a stretch scenario.

Power was expected to be the single biggest driver, its share of total demand rising from 36% to about 47% over the period, while fertiliser's share was expected to fall from 25% to 15% even as its absolute volume grew from 59.9 to 110 MMSCMD (then plateau). City gas distribution's share was expected to nearly double, from 6% to 11%, reaching 85.6 MMSCMD by 2029-30. Regionally, the report expected the Western market's share of demand to shrink from 42% to 32% as the Northern, Southern and Eastern markets caught up on the back of new pipeline connectivity, with the North-Eastern region's demand expected to more than quadruple off a very low base (5 to 24 MMSCMD).

On supply, the report projected total availability growing from 145.7 MMSCMD in 2012-13 to 474 MMSCMD in 2029-30 (a 7.2% CAGR), with domestic production rising only modestly (101 to 230 MMSCMD) while LNG imports did most of the work (44.6 to 214 MMSCMD), plus 30 MMSCMD via the TAPI cross-border pipeline from an assumed 2017-18 commissioning. The report notes that actual domestic output had already fallen to around 85 MMSCMD by January 2013, below its own 101 MMSCMD figure for 2012-13, "primarily due to steady reduction in supply from KG-D6 fields."

Because demand was projected to outrun supply throughout, the report's central number is arguably its demand-supply gap: 97 MMSCMD in 2012-13, narrowing after 2017-18 as new LNG terminals and TAPI came online, then widening back out to roughly 272 MMSCMD by 2029-30.

On infrastructure, the report counted about 13,030 km of transmission pipeline and 337 MMSCMD of design capacity as of 2012, and called for this to expand to roughly 28,000–32,727 km and 815 MMSCMD of design capacity within five to six years, forming a National Gas Grid connecting all major demand and supply centres. Its policy recommendations centred on legally unbundling pipeline transportation from gas marketing within two to three years, establishing an open-access code, moving from a zonal to an entry-exit tariff system, developing a gas-trading hub, and progressively removing the Gas Utilisation Policy's administrative allocation of domestically produced gas in favour of a freer market.

## Actual consumption: about 188 to 201 MMSCMD from FY2024 to FY2026

Directly comparable Indian gas-consumption figures for every one of those years were not all available to this piece, but the recent anchor points show the scale of the gap. The 2025 committee report puts consumption at 188 MMSCMD in FY2024 (p. 203), the same figure trade-press reporting citing PPAC data gave for FY2023-24, and at 99 MMSCMD of domestic gas plus 102 MMSCMD of LNG in FY2025 through December 2024, about 201 MMSCMD in total (p. 20). Trade press citing PPAC data put 2025 consumption at roughly 197.1 MMSCMD, with domestic production at roughly 98.9 MMSCMD.

A June 2026 industry review hosted by the NSE, using PPAC's gas balance, reports FY2025-26 consumption of 68,542 MMSCM against 71,314 MMSCM in FY2024-25: about 188 MMSCMD, down from about 195 MMSCMD (this blog's arithmetic). Business Standard reported that consumption fell in 2025 because of a long monsoon and cheaper alternative fuels. One other estimate (Polymerupdate/CARE) gives 212 MMSCMD for FY2025-26.

Set against the 2013 report's numbers for the same years (571.09 MMSCMD for 2023-24, 598.11 for 2024-25, 625.92 for 2025-26), actual demand ran at roughly one-third of what the report expected, and on either FY2025-26 estimate the gap has not narrowed. Actual domestic production, at about 99 MMSCMD, was well under half of what the report projected for domestic supply in the same window (182 MMSCMD for 2021-22 in its supply table).

## The 2025 committee synthesised five demand forecasts

PNGRB constituted the High-Level Expert Committee on 3 May 2024, chaired by Shri D K Sarraf (former PNGRB chairperson and former CMD of ONGC), with members Shri Gurdeep Singh (CMD, NTPC), Shri Sandeep Kumar Gupta (CMD, GAIL), Shri Manoj Jain (Managing Director, Torrent Gas), Dr A K Balyan (former MD, Petronet LNG), and Shri Suresh P Manglani (Executive Director & CEO, Adani Total Gas), with ICF Consulting India as knowledge partner. Its terms of reference asked it to develop demand and supply scenarios to 2040, assess pricing and taxation across the gas value chain, and recommend measures to raise gas's share of India's energy mix to the government's target of 15% by 2030, from roughly 6% today. The Chairman signed the report on 4 August 2025, after the committee consulted more than 75 stakeholders.

The report says it did not build its own demand-supply model. It synthesises five existing forecasts: an in-house PNGRB paper, the 2022 ICF/PNGRB National Gas Grid Technical Assessment, and outlooks from S&P Global Commodity Insights, Shell Energy India, and the IEA. "While it did not undertake a standalone, independent modelling exercise to project natural gas demand and supply for the year 2040, it recognised that any forward-looking roadmap for the sector must be firmly anchored in robust, data-driven evidence" (p. 21).

The committee's headline synthesis, stated twice in the report: "India's natural gas demand is projected to reach 260 to 300 MMSCMD by 2030 and could rise further to 365 to 500 MMSCMD by 2040 in the base case. Achieving demand up to 630 to 700 MMSCMD by 2040 is contingent upon the timely implementation of enabling policy measures and progressive regulatory reforms" (p. 147). Every source agrees city gas distribution (CGD) drives most of the growth: "From the year 2030 to 2040, majority of the growth (more than 50%) in natural gas demand is expected to come from CGD alone" (p. 140).

## The 2025 forecasts for 2040 are below the 2013 forecast for 2030

Every 2040 figure in the committee's synthesis, including the 630–700 MMSCMD full-reform ceiling, is below the 746 MMSCMD the 2013 report gave as its base case for 2029-30. On PNGRB's own current thinking, India is not expected to reach by 2040 the level of gas demand a 2013 industry report expected by 2030. The 2013 report implied roughly 3.1× demand growth from its 2013 base to 2029-30; the 2025 report's base case implies roughly 1.3–1.5× growth over a comparable span. Of the individual forecasts in the table above, only the NGG Technical Assessment's accelerated case (795 MMSCMD in 2040) exceeds it.

The 2025 report never cites the 746 MMSCMD figure, or the 2013 report by name: a full-text search of all 352 pages for "Vision 2030," "746," and "2029-30" returned nothing. Neither report explains the other's numbers, because the newer one does not engage with the older one.

## Supply: domestic output flat, LNG imports cover the growth

All five sources' domestic-production estimates for 2040 are flat or falling even as demand roughly doubles. The report says: "Domestic natural gas production is expected to rise to ~120 MMSCMD in the coming years, but may revert to ~100 MMSCMD by 2030. As a result, India's reliance on imported LNG (RLNG) will continue to grow" (p. 138). S&P Global has domestic output peaking at 123 MMSCMD in 2027 before declining to 98 MMSCMD by 2040, with LNG imports rising to 261 MMSCMD to cover the gap (p. 144). Shell's outlook has LNG imports reaching roughly 269 MMSCMD by 2040 (p. 145). "Currently, India's natural gas production meets only ~50% of its demand. As demand is expected to grow significantly by 2030 and 2040, dependence on LNG will increase to bridge the demand-supply gap. Such a growth would result in doubling of LNG imports by 2030" (p. 143). The 2013 report had expected domestic production to reach 230 MMSCMD by 2029-30.

## Pipelines and LNG terminals: near the 2013 length target, unevenly used

The pipeline network is the one place the 2013 vision came close on length. The 2025 committee report counts 33,475 km of authorised gas transmission pipeline as of December 2024, of which 25,124 km was operational and 10,676 km under construction (p. 156–157). PNGRB's monthly MIS report gives 28,157 km operating against 34,978 km authorised as of June 2026 (see this blog's [gas pipeline map](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/10/india-has-27400-km-of-gas-pipeline.html)), already within the 2013 report's 28,000–32,727 km target, though reached over far longer than the "five to six years" it set.

A network built for demand approaching 600–750 MMSCMD is moving demand closer to 200 MMSCMD. PNGRB's chairman has publicly put nationwide gas-pipeline utilisation at around 50%, with some individual lines running under 10% full. The committee report gives named figures for five major cost-plus pipelines (FY26 utilisation): the East-West Pipeline at 41%, GAIL's HVJ integrated system at 71.69%, the KG Basin Pipeline at 20.03%, Dadri-Panipat at 27.85%, and GSPL's high-pressure network at 100.5% (Table 6.3, p. 167). LNG terminal utilisation shows the same spread: Dahej ran at 100.3% (April-December 2024) while Mundra ran at 23.6% and Kochi at 22.0% (Table 6.4A). India's LNG regasification capacity stood at 52.7 MMTPA operational, with 10 MMTPA under construction and a further 25.2 MMTPA proposed, for a total of 87.9 MMTPA (p. 175–177).

The report's 2040 pipeline vision adds roughly 8,000 km across two national gas-grid projects and 19 named corridor projects, plus an estimated 15,000 km of local spur lines, so that all but 139 of India's districts sit within 50 km of the trunk network (p. 165). It puts the cost at "approximately US$39.73 billion (Rs. 3.34 Lakh Crore) in the accelerated scenario till 2040" against roughly US$26.0 billion in a business-as-usual case (p. 166). On storage: "Currently, India's LNG storage capacity covers approximately 12 days of total gas consumption" (p. 203); the report estimates roughly 10–11 additional large LNG storage tanks would be needed to reach a 20-day strategic buffer by FY2030. On city gas, it cites CGD entities' submitted work plans projecting 120 million domestic PNG connections and 17,500 CNG stations by 2030, against roughly 14.19 million connections and 7,513 stations as of December 2024 (p. 33).

## Why demand fell short of the 2013 projection

The 2013 report's central bet was that gas would substitute for oil and, especially, that gas-fired power generation would expand rapidly on the back of new LNG import capacity. That did not happen as the report expected. Gas-fired power plants built in the 2000s and early 2010s were left stranded through much of the following decade by a combination of high landed LNG prices and limited domestic gas allocation, while India's power-sector growth leaned heavily on coal, and increasingly on solar and wind, rather than gas.

Fertiliser demand grew, but the report's own projection already expected its share of gas demand to shrink. City-gas and industrial demand, while growing, took far longer to reach the scale the report assumed, with a slower build-out of city gas distribution networks and last-mile industrial connections than a report written at the start of India's CGD expansion anticipated. The report's own note that domestic production had already fallen below its base-year number, months after that number was set, shows its assumptions were under strain before the projection period had properly started.

## Pricing and tax: LNG oversupply expected from 2028, gas outside GST

The 2025 report's pricing chapter is descriptive rather than prescriptive and proposes no new pricing formula. Its central forecast is that global LNG oversupply from 2028 will work in India's favour: "A massive wave of new LNG export capacity, combined with sluggish demand growth, is expected to push global LNG markets into oversupply in the 2028-2030 time period. This impending surplus will likely exert downward pressure on LNG prices, creating a strategic advantage for India" (p. 188), citing global LNG liquefaction capacity rising from about 474 MMTPA in early 2023 to 666.5 MMTPA by end-2028.

On domestic pricing, the report describes the April 2023 guidelines: the Administered Price Mechanism (APM) ceiling is set at 10% of the average Indian crude basket price, capped and floored, with the ceiling "revised to US$6.75/MMBtu with effect from April 2025," up from US$6.50 (p. 191–192). Under the same guidelines the ceiling rose to US$7.00/MMBtu from April 2026, as reported by the Free Press Journal and others; the computed April 2026 price was US$10.76, so the cap applied, and one report puts the computed October 2026 price at about US$11.22, still capped at US$7.00. The report also runs the arithmetic for one large consumer: at a landed cost of US$8.76/MMBtu for APM gas versus US$15.34/MMBtu for imported gas, "the cost of urea produced through imported gas is Rs. 36/kg and the cost of urea produced through domestic gas is Rs. 23/kg" (p. 64).

On taxation, the report's recurring complaint is that natural gas remains outside GST nine years after GST's 2017 rollout, producing a cascading tax effect it says pushes the total tax burden on natural gas to 30–35% of landed cost in some states (p. 196). Its recommendations include bringing gas under GST with full input-tax credit, removing the 14% excise duty on CNG compression (which it says adds Rs. 8–10 per kg), and exempting customs duty on LNG imported for gas-based power and city gas distribution (pp. viii–x, 194, 197–198). It also proposes mandatory colour-coded retail-station signage (green for "Green Fuel," blue for "Clean Fuel," black-grey for "Other Fuel") to steer consumer fuel choice at the pump (p. viii–ix).

## Market reform: an independent system operator instead of unbundling

The report's most detailed policy chapter proposes an eight-point plan for what it repeatedly calls a genuinely free gas market, diagnosing the current one as fragmented: "Multiple physical hubs in the gas market—17 delivery points across five regions—fragments the whole market into 17 local markets" (p. 229). Its recommendations: prohibit resale and destination-restriction clauses in RLNG supply contracts; require transparent, non-discriminatory third-party access to RLNG import terminals with an online bulletin board and secondary-market capacity trading; synchronise new pipeline development with the National Gas Grid; and establish an Independent System Operator for gas pipelines to oversee capacity booking and scheduling.

Where the 2013 report asked for legal unbundling of pipeline transportation from gas marketing, the 2025 report says its operator model is not the same as ownership unbundling, even though it cites the American experience of that kind of unbundling as its inspiration: "If we do not want to follow the unbundling route, but want to maintain independence between supply and transport, then the ISO model is apt" (p. 296). On pipeline tariffs, it recommends moving bid criteria from lowest-tariff to capital-cost bidding with Discounted Cash Flow tariff-setting, switching from Return-on-Capital-Employed to an equity-based return capped at 14% post-tax on a normative 70:30 debt-equity structure, and Viability Gap Funding for corridors that are strategically important but not commercially viable on their own.

## GAIL's chairman dissented, and the committee cited his conflict of interest

The report publishes a formal dissent in full, alongside the committee's point-by-point response (Annexure 11, pp. 294–299). Shri Sandeep Kumar Gupta, GAIL's chairman and managing director and a committee member, objected on several grounds: that the committee should have built its own demand-supply model rather than synthesising others'; that it should not comment on RLNG-terminal regulation or transportation-marketing separation while related matters are before courts; that the proposed 14% post-tax equity return on pipeline tariffs is too low (he preferred 15–16%, matching power-transmission norms); and that removing resale and destination restrictions on domestic gas sale agreements threatens energy security.

The committee's response states that Gupta, as GAIL's CMD, has a direct commercial stake in several of the disputed recommendations, noting that "GAIL... is a promoter-shareholder in an entity that is contesting PNGRB's Regulations and also owns another RLNG terminal" (p. 295), and adds: "it would have indeed been difficult for him to agree to recommendations which may be contrary to GAIL's business interest/practices" (p. 295). It says some of his objections concern "business of GAIL." On the resale-clause objection, the committee calls it "a dissent" and declines to change the recommendation (p. 299). A second, narrower dissent came from Shri Suresh P Manglani of Adani Total Gas, objecting only to the RLNG third-party-access recommendation.
