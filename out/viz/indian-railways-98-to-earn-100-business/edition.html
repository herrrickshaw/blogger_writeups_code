Indian Railways is budgeted to spend ₹98.43 to earn every ₹100 in FY2025-26 — an operating ratio that has stayed above 96% every year since 2016-17. In 2015, a government-appointed committee said the root problem was that Railways doesn't actually know what it costs to run any given train, and recommended switching to modern cost accounting to find out. Nine years later, in response to an RTI request asking whether its flagship Vande Bharat trains turn a profit, the Railway Ministry's answer was almost word for word what the committee had warned about: "train-wise portability is not maintained."

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Railways spent ₹107 for every ₹100 earned in 2021-22

What it means: The ratio has stayed above 96% every year since 2016-17, and reached 107% in 2021-22.

What to do: Track each service's cost against its revenue so the ratio is managed deliberately, not only reported annually.

Freight rates rose 91% in a decade while passenger fares rose 28%

What it means: Freight is funding passenger losses that are classed as social obligations rather than commercial failures.

What to do: Rationalise freight rates through direct budgetary support rather than continued cross-subsidy, as the Hindu Centre analysis proposes.

A 2015 committee asked for train-wise costing; a 2024 reply shows it is still missing

  1. June 2015Debroy Committee finds the accounting system cannot say what it costs to run a train.
  2. April 2024RTI reply on Vande Bharat: "train-wise portability is not maintained".

What it means: The 2015 committee said Railways could not cost individual trains, and the 2024 RTI reply shows that is still true.

What to do: Adopt the committee's accrual, double-entry accounting so each train's cost and viability can be determined.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Railways should publish annual revenue and working cost for each Vande Bharat service, train by train, so that the question the 2024 RTI could not get answered — whether the flagship product pays for itself — is answered in Railways’ own accounts.
  • The Railway Board should publish an annual statement of social-service-obligation losses by class — Second, Ordinary and suburban — alongside the freight surplus that covers them, so that the cross-subsidy behind a 98% operating ratio is stated in rupees rather than inferred.
  • The Ministry of Railways should print the operating ratio both as reported and excluding one-off adjustments, such as advance receipts and deferred pension, in its budget documents, so that years CAG says would otherwise have crossed 100% are visible without reading audit reports.
  • The Ministry of Railways should publish a status table for every Debroy Committee recommendation of June 2015 — accepted, implemented, pending or rejected, with dates, so that progress on accrual accounting and an independent regulator can be checked a decade on.
  • The Railway Board should report scrap-sale and non-fare revenue as separate lines in its annual accounts and monthly earnings statements, so that figures like ₹6,813.86 crore and ₹777 crore can be checked against a primary Railways source rather than news aggregators.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. The FY2025-26 scrap-sales and non-fare-revenue figures come from secondary news sources without a primary CAG, PRS, or Ministry of Railways citation this piece could verify directly; they are presented as illustrative rather than authoritative. Whether Railways has made any accrual-accounting progress since the Debroy Committee's 2015 report, short of the train-level cost tracking the Vande Bharat RTI shows is still missing, was not established here. The Debroy Committee's regulatory-separation and zone-restructuring recommendations are reported as originally proposed in 2015; this piece does not track which of them, if any, have since been implemented.
