India sent the EU USD 6.3 billion of CBAM-covered goods in 2024, most of it steel. Under the rules in force, verified emissions data cuts the 2026 charge on Indian hot-rolled coil from about €270 a tonne to €93. India’s own carbon credits, expected at €6–13 a tonne and not yet traded, are not recognised by the EU and would deduct little if they were.

What it means: Not verifying costs about €177 a tonne more in 2026 and about €306 from 2030, so the default value is the expensive choice for any mill that can measure its emissions.

What to do: Get installation-level emissions data checked by an EU-accredited verifier, since vendors quote €5,000–50,000 per installation.

What it means: Iron and steel is 85% of India's USD 6.3 bn of CBAM-covered exports to the EU, so the steel price is where the exposure sits.

What to do: Processors of chapter 73 goods, such as pipes and fasteners, should ask the supplying mill for installation emissions data now.

For aluminium, the default can be cheaper than verified data

What it means: Because CBAM counts only direct emissions, a smelter above 2.06 tonnes in 2026 pays less on the default, though the cost is small either way at about €30–90 a tonne.

What to do: Smelters should compare their verified direct emissions with the default of 1.87 tonnes before deciding whether to verify.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Steel and the commerce ministry should fund EU-accredited verification for every Indian installation that ships CBAM goods to the EU, starting with steel, where it saves about €177 a tonne in 2026. The scheme Goyal announced on 23 October should be notified with rules and an outlay, and should cover traders that buy from mills, not only manufacturers.
  • The Bureau of Energy Efficiency should publish plant-level direct emissions intensities, from the measurement and reporting it already collects under CCTS, in the format the EU uses, so that EU importers can see what an Indian plant emits rather than falling back on defaults.
  • In the trade agreement’s technical dialogue, India should seek EU recognition of NABCB-accredited verifiers and a review of India’s steel default values against verified plant data, both of which the dialogue’s terms allow.
  • MoEFCC should notify the steel targets and CERC should publish the floor and ceiling prices before the first trading window, so that a CCTS price exists to be assessed. The government should be clear that this is about domestic emissions, not a CBAM offset, because at current estimates it cannot be one.
  • If India wants CBAM revenue to stay at home, it needs a charge that is actually paid per tonne of emissions, such as the export levy ORF and CEEW’s stakeholders have proposed, agreed with the EU in advance as deductible. At 2.4 Mt and the verified 2026 rate in the table above, steel alone would mean about €224 million a year now going to the EU.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted, except where a think-tank or the government is cited.
