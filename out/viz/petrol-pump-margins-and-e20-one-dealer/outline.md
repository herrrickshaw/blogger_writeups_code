The question is whether the retail fuel business got more profitable after E20 arrived. The evidence found does not support that for the people who run the pumps, and it does not support it for the oil companies either. The dealer got one rise in seven years, it was a litigation settlement, and in real terms it gave back less than half of what inflation had taken since 2017. The oil companies’ margin was high while crude was cheap and went negative when crude spiked in 2026. The party that gained directly from E20 is the ethanol supplier, whose price is set by the government.

How much to trust it. The dealer-commission history comes from PPAC’s own table, posted on 3 December 2024, and inflation from MoSPI’s consumer price index. Everything else comes from search-result summaries of press, brokerage and agency pages, and the OMC margin figures are analyst or press estimates. Where this piece does arithmetic of its own, it says so.

## What PPAC’s own table shows

PPAC publishes the dealer commission on petrol and diesel as a dated table going back to April 2004, sourced to ministry and oil-company circulars. Until July 2011 it was a flat rupee amount per kilolitre. Since December 2013 for petrol and October 2014 for diesel it has two parts: a fixed amount per kilolitre plus a percentage of the product’s billable price. The percentage part rises on its own when the product price rises. The fixed part moves only when the oil companies revise it.

In December 2025 rupees, the fixed part of the petrol commission was worth Rs 3.91 a litre in August 2017 and Rs 2.69 by September 2024. ## Why 2026 is hard for the station

- Supply limits. Dealers in Punjab and Rajasthan alleged that oil companies cut supplies, and a 90-day central order reportedly capped diesel at 200 litres per vehicle a day. Jio-bp and Nayara capped diesel per customer in September. Lower volume means lower commission, as it is paid per kilolitre.

- The UPI fee. A merchant fee on UPI was due to take effect on 15 October; on 13 October NPCI deferred it until after Diwali, with no new date. The national dealers’ body wrote to the Finance Minister on 16 September asking for an exemption, and some dealers said they would stop UPI above Rs 2,000. The summaries disagree on the fee’s design (a flat Rs 5 above Rs 2,000 in one, a percentage fee in this blog’s earlier piece), and no outcome was found. Fuel is the category most exposed because the dealer’s margin is only about 3.9% of the ticket.

- E20 handling. Dealers say ethanol draws moisture, and that water above about 0.5% makes it separate in tanks built for petrol. Oil companies and the automobile dealers’ federation reject the contamination claims, and say tests run up to 12 times a day at 87,000-plus outlets. Petrol sales were reportedly stopped at 2,573 outlets as of 25 August, from unnamed ministry sources. No compensation scheme and no costed figures for tanks, filters or dispensers were found.

- Frozen prices and shrinking private share. Public-sector prices have been frozen since May, after about Rs 7.4 to 7.5 a litre of rises. The private networks’ share fell: Nayara’s petrol share from about 6% to 4% by April, with price rises of its own.

## So did margins improve after E20?

- Dealers: one step of about Rs 0.65 a litre on petrol in October 2024, 20 months after the E20 launch and unrelated to it. Real income is squeezed by 2026 supply limits, the pending UPI fee and E20 handling complaints.

- Oil companies: margins were high in FY24 to H1 FY26 because crude was low and prices held, and negative in 2026. The Petroleum Ministry says E20 costs more at normal crude.

- Ethanol suppliers: the clear gainers: Rs 1.72 lakh crore of purchases across three ethanol supply years.

- A fair test is hard because crude, the rupee, the March excise cut and the May price rounds all moved together with E20, and “the E20 launch” could mean the 2023 pilot, national sale, or April 2026.

## Where the data stop

- No dealer net profit. No published study of dealer costs, non-fuel income, or payback exists in what was found. The Rs 1 to 1.5 a litre is a blog estimate.

- No audited OMC margins for FY23 to FY25, and the 2026 figures are analyst estimates that differ widely.

- Dealer commission after December 2024 rests on a single price sheet repeated by a press item; PPAC’s table was posted on 3 December 2024, so a later revision could have been missed. The slab-based incentives are not quantified.

- Private and CNG margins are mostly not found, and there is no dealer-owned or company-owned split.

- Premium grades (XP95, Speed, Power) and non-fuel income were not quantified.
