The pipeline connecting a factory's smokestack to an underground storage site is not where India's CO2 sequestration bill actually gets large. CEEW's own network modelling puts that piece at $0.70–4.19 a tonne. A separate India-specific study puts the all-in cost — capture included — at ₹8,000–13,000 a tonne or more. The pipeline is a rounding error next to the capture cost, and the government's own ₹19,700 crore scheme covers a small fraction of what NITI Aayog says the buildout actually needs.

Top line

Updated 27 October 2026. An arithmetic slip is corrected. The ₹19,700 crore CCUS scheme, about $2.2 billion, is about 2.2% of the $100 billion low end of NITI Aayog’s $100–150 billion investment estimate and about 1.5% of the high end; the piece had called it “roughly 1–2% of that low-end figure”. The conclusion, that the scheme is a first instalment rather than the full bill, is unchanged.
Updated 12 October 2026: pipeline lengths checked against PNGRB and PPAC data. CEEW’s report (“CO₂ Pipeline Network for Carbon Capture and Storage in India: Evaluating a Parallel Network Along Existing Pipelines”, Chordia and Mallya, 2026) carries two figures, not one. Its summary says the new CO₂ network would largely use the right-of-way of about 32,000 km of existing or planned natural gas pipelines; the design that links the 765 emission sources uses about 18,000 km of existing gas trunk lines. The 18,000 km in this article is therefore the design figure, and 32,000 km is the wider corridor base. The report also recommends a national CCS policy with a dedicated regulator and amending the 1962 pipeline right-of-user Act to cover CO₂. PNGRB’s data bank puts authorised natural gas pipelines at 34,803 km at 31 March 2026 (33,027 km common carrier, 997 km tie-in, 779 km dedicated), with 27,905 km operational; a year earlier (June 2025) it was 34,233 km authorised and 25,429 km operational. CEEW’s 32,000 km is close to the common-carrier total, though that match is this blog’s inference, not something the report was seen to say. Neither the CEEW report nor the PNGRB table could be opened directly in this research environment; every figure here comes from search-result summaries and needs checking against the PDFs.
Updated 7 November 2026. The ₹20,000 crore CCUS outlay announced in the 2026-27 Budget (Argus) still had no reported Cabinet approval or scheme guidelines as of 7 November 2026, so the ₹19,700 crore scheme cleared by the Expenditure Finance Committee is not yet operational. PNGRB’s later data put operational natural gas pipelines at 28,157 km of 34,978 km authorised in June 2026, up from 27,905 km in March (see this blog’s gas pipeline map); the March figures in the table below are kept as dated.

Pipeline transport is $0.70–4.19 a tonne, far below global capture-and-storage cost

What it means: The post's own CEEW figures put pipeline transport well below the $40–60 global capture-and-storage benchmark.

What to do: Check capture and all-in costs, not pipeline tariffs, before pricing any Indian CO2 storage project.

Storage estimates range from 1.2 Gt to 359 Gt depending on scope

What it means: The post shows that India's storage figures differ by scope, with the regulator's viable estimate far below the theoretical totals.

What to do: State the scope of any storage figure before citing it as India's storage limit.

Government funding covers a first instalment, not the $100–150 billion need

What it means: The ₹19,700 crore scheme is a first commitment against NITI Aayog's $100–150 billion estimate, not the full bill.

What to do: Track whether the ₹20,000 crore CCUS outlay gets Cabinet approval and guidelines, which were not reported as of 7 November 2026.

What would fix this

  • PNGRB, with the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas, should publish draft regulations extending common-carrier authorisation and open access to CO2 pipelines, with a consultation deadline, so the shared multi-shipper network NITI Aayog's hub-and-cluster model depends on has a legal basis like the one gas pipelines already have.
  • PNGRB should issue technical and safety standards specific to CO2 pipelines, including rules for reusing existing gas-pipeline right-of-way, since CEEW's network design rests on roughly 18,000 km of gas trunk corridors and no CO2-specific safety regime was found.
  • DGH should publish a single table setting the 317 Gt, 359 Gt and 1.2 Gt storage estimates side by side by storage medium and scope, stating which it treats as viable and why, so no single figure is repeated as India's storage ceiling.
  • The Ministry of Power, as nodal agency, should publish for every project funded under the ₹19,700 crore CCUS scheme the tonnes captured and capture, transport and storage cost per tonne, so the unexplained gap between India's ₹8,000–13,000 all-in estimate and the global $40–60 figure is settled with Indian project data.
  • NITI Aayog should publish an investment path to its 750 million tonne 2050 target, showing by decade how much of the $100–150 billion is expected from government, viability gap funding and private capital, so the current scheme can be judged against a stated first-phase share.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Sources and caveats

This piece carries an unusually significant sourcing limitation, stated plainly rather than smoothed over: all four sources the reader specifically pointed this piece toward — CEEW's CO2 pipeline network study (ceew.in), the peer-reviewed "CCUS in India" techno-economic paper (pubs.geoscienceworld.org), Carbon Herald's coverage of India's CCUS budget (carbonherald.com), and Carbon Management Europe's publication on capture/transport/storage costs (carbonmanagementeurope.org) — were all blocked from direct fetch in this research environment, alongside iea.org, mopng.gov.in, dghindia.gov.in, niti.gov.in and pib.gov.in. Every figure in this piece attributed to those sources rests on how their findings were summarised in search-engine results, not a full direct read of the original publication, methodology, or underlying assumptions. This is a materially lower-confidence sourcing basis than this blog's usual practice, and every section above flags it individually rather than presenting uniform confidence. Section 1's pipeline-network design figures are from CEEW's and MoPNG's own studies, both reached only via search summary. Section 2's transport cost-per-tonne figures and cost-component breakdown are from CEEW's study, likewise only via search summary, and are the single most load-bearing set of figures in this piece that this piece could not independently verify against the source's own stated methodology. Section 3's three competing storage-potential figures are individually sourced to CEEW, the peer-reviewed paper, and DGH's own roadmap, none read directly. Section 4's India-specific all-in cost figures are from the same peer-reviewed paper, also via search summary only, and the gap between that figure and the commonly-cited global $40-60/tonne benchmark is reported as unresolved rather than explained. Section 5's funding and regulatory-gap findings draw on PIB, NITI Aayog, and secondary legal commentary (Mondaq, Lexology), with the CO2-specific regulatory-gap finding resting on secondary literature rather than a primary PNGRB or MoPNG confirmation. Section 6's capital-cost figures rest on secondary reporting without a traced primary source. Nothing in this piece is investment, financial, or engineering advice; a reader making a real CCUS investment, project-financing, or policy decision should go directly to CEEW's, NITI Aayog's, DGH's, and the peer-reviewed literature's own primary publications rather than this summary.
