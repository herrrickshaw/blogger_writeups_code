Two earlier pieces on this blog treated price as a barrier (fractional investing) and diversification as a virtue (the thali). This one puts them together properly, using actual portfolio theory: if price is the only filter applied, does buying every stock in a price band — the "stable" — actually produce a better risk-adjusted outcome than the alternative, or does portfolio theory's own central result say the whole exercise is asking the wrong question?

Top line

  • 2.27 Sharpe ratio for the above-₹10,000 price band, the best of five equal-weighted portfolios.
  • 0.56 for the under-₹1,000 band, the only one below 1.0, despite holding 1,987 stocks.
  • 22.4% annualised volatility in the under-₹1,000 band, the highest of the five.

What it means: Ranking the five bands by Sharpe ratio puts the highest-priced stocks first, a lead the post traces to a 44.4% return rather than lower risk.

What to do: Do not choose a price band on the strength of this ranking, because the post finds price is not a recognised risk factor.

The under-₹1,000 portfolio has the highest volatility, at 22.4%

What it means: The cheapest band pairs the highest volatility with the lowest return, 19.0%, which is why its Sharpe ratio is the weakest.

What to do: Weigh a large number of holdings against the filter behind them, since 1,987 stocks did not fix the weaker risk-return profile.

Simulated bands score above published fund Sharpe ratios, with caveats

What it means: The post warns the comparison is not like-for-like, because real funds pay expense ratios and use rolling windows the simulation does not.

What to do: Check any fund's Sharpe ratio against its own stated window and costs before using it as a benchmark for a price-band basket.

What would fix this

  • SEBI should require every mutual fund factsheet to report the Sharpe ratio over a standard window, with the risk-free rate used stated alongside it, so a single fund’s ratio does not range from −0.31 to 1.68 depending on which source an investor reads.
  • AMFI should publish, free on its website, standardised 3- and 5-year volatility and Sharpe ratios for every equity scheme, so investors comparing funds are not dependent on aggregator sites with unstated methods or paid subscriptions.
  • AMFI should publish category-level risk-adjusted returns for index funds and actively managed funds, computed after expense ratios on one stated method, so the diversification claim behind “Mutual Funds Sahi Hai” can be checked against comparable figures.
  • NSE Indices should publish annualised volatility and Sharpe ratios for its main indices on a declared window and risk-free rate, so fund and portfolio figures can be benchmarked against the index on the same basis.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

This analysis is based on historical price data and does not constitute investment advice. Past risk-adjusted returns (Sharpe ratios) are backward-looking and specific to the 3.5-year window measured; they are not a prediction that any price band will continue to outperform or underperform. Equal-weighted portfolios (an equal rupee amount invested in every stock in a band) do not reflect how any real investor would actually construct a portfolio and are used here purely to isolate price as the sole selection filter, consistent with this blog's earlier price-segmentation work. Local warehouse price coverage was incomplete for the largest band (1,987 of 4,862 stocks under ₹1,000 had usable price history), a limitation shared with the live-data beta figures in the companion piece and noted there in more detail.
