Indian refiners' gross refining margin — the money made turning crude into fuel — has roughly halved in the space of a year, from $10–12 a barrel in FY2023-24 to $4–6 in FY2024-25, and stayed subdued into FY2025-26. Over the same period, LPG demand grew faster than petrol demand, LPG's own subsidy economics improved sharply, and polymer spreads widened. None of that is a coincidence refiners can ignore: the same crude barrel that makes petrol can, with the modifications this blog has already covered piece by piece, make more LPG and more petrochemical feedstock instead. This piece pulls those separate findings together into the actual margin argument for doing so.

Indian refining margins: $4–6 a barrel, with LPG and petrochemicals beating petrol

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • $4–6/bbl was the Indian OMC gross refining margin in FY2024-25, down from $10–12/bbl a year earlier.
  • 60.7% of LPG consumption was imported in 2021-22, up from 48.7% in 2017-18.
  • +60% widening in the LDPE-naphtha spread, Oct 2024–Mar 2025 vs the same window a year earlier.
Updated 7 November 2026. Two of this piece’s premises have reversed since it was written. Refining margins did not stay compressed: after the Hormuz disruption, BPCL reported a gross refining margin of $32.53/bbl and HPCL $32.15/bbl for July–September 2026, against about $9–11 a year earlier. And LPG’s supply economics turned the other way: the Saudi contract price for India’s propane-butane mix rose from about $543/tonne in February to $790 in June, the government put the loss on a domestic cylinder at about ₹700 in June even after a price rise, and cumulative LPG under-recoveries passed ₹59,000 crore by 31 July. LPG import dependence is still about 60% (33.2 Mt consumed, 13.1 Mt produced in 2025-26, PPAC). The structural case for more LPG and polymer yield from the barrel stands; the near-term margin arithmetic in Sections 1 and 3 does not. Indian refiners' fuel margin fell to $4–6/bbl from $10–12/bbl

What it means: Refining margin per barrel has fallen far enough to give refiners a reason to shift output toward LPG and petrochemicals.

What to do: Track the next quarters of margin data before treating the fall as lasting, since the post says these margins are cyclical.

What it means: Domestic LPG production stayed flat while imports met nearly all of the rise in consumption.

What to do: Treat the growing import share as a gap that domestic LPG output from a refinery could fill.

Cheaper propane and butane and state support narrowed the LPG supply gap

What it means: Lower propane and butane prices and government compensation both moved in LPG's favour as its fuel margin was falling.

What to do: Check whether the narrower gap holds once the monthly government compensation stops.

The polymer spread over naphtha widened sharply in the latest window

What it means: Over the latest six-month window the polymer side of the barrel was the steadier and wider-margin route.

What to do: Expect petrochemical spreads to be cyclical and re-check them before committing capital.

What would fix this

  • PPAC should publish quarterly gross refining margins for each PSU refinery on a stated, common method, so the fall from $10–12 to $4–6 a barrel can be read from the government’s own data rather than a ratings agency’s tracking, and compared fairly with private refiners’ disclosures.
  • PPAC should bring its Ready Reckoner LPG production, consumption, import and import-dependency series up to the latest completed year in the same table format, so the rise from 48.7% to 60.7% import dependency through 2021–22 is not continued only by secondary reports of 60–64%.
  • PPAC should publish each refinery’s annual product yields, including LPG, petrol and petrochemical feedstock as shares of crude processed, so whether refiners are actually shifting the barrel from petrol towards LPG and polymers can be measured rather than inferred from capex announcements.
  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should publish monthly the LPG under-recovery per cylinder and the government compensation paid to each OMC, so swings like the fall from ₹98.6 to ₹33 a cylinder, and the monthly compensation that began in November 2025, are on the official record.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Margin figures in this piece are drawn from secondary industry and ratings-agency reporting (CareEdge, trade press), not primary refiner disclosures, and cover specific, sometimes non-identical reporting windows (fiscal year, calendar quarter, or a named six-month comparison) rather than a single reconciled period — readers should treat the direction of each figure as more reliable than a precise cross-comparison between them. This piece does not forecast future margins in either fuel or petrochemicals; both are historically cyclical, and the data presented describes a recent, specific window, not a permanent state.
