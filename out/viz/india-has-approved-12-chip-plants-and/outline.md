This blog’s [piece on the electronics schemes](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/08/indias-mobile-phone-pli-beat-every.html) found that India’s mobile-phone incentive beat every target while its chip programme had only a few plants producing. In July the Cabinet approved a second round, Semicon 2.0, with Rs 127,500 crore. This piece sets out where each of the first round’s 12 approved projects stands in October 2026, how much of the first round’s money has been spent, and what the second round changes.

Investment figures are the totals announced at Cabinet approval; Tata’s Assam plant is also listed at Rs 27,120 crore in some sources. The four August 2025 approvals sum to Rs 4,594 crore, matching the reported total; Crystal Matrix and Suchi were approved together at about Rs 3,936 crore. Statuses are from news reports and ministers’ statements; no official project-wise table is published.

At SEMICON India in September, the government said five units were in commercial production: Micron, CG Semi and Kaynes in Sanand, and CDIL in Mohali and Suchi in Surat, which started then. All five assemble, test or package chips; none makes wafers. The one silicon fab, Tata’s at Dholera, is due to be commissioned in 2028, and HCL and Foxconn’s plant at Jewar is also aimed at 2028.

Measured by money rather than plant count, the picture is starker. The producing plants account for about Rs 33,524 crore, 20% of the approved Rs 164,169 crore. The Tata fab alone is 56%. Most of what has been approved is capacity that does not yet exist.

The central government pays its support in instalments as projects reach milestones, so spending lags approvals. A parliamentary committee reported that the ministry spent Rs 681.11 crore of Rs 1,503.36 crore allocated to the programme in 2023-24, surrendering more than half. The Union Budget shows Rs 638 crore actually spent in 2024-25, a revised estimate of Rs 4,300 crore for 2025-26 and Rs 8,000 crore budgeted for 2026-27, with a further Rs 1,000 crore for Semicon 2.0. PRS Legislative Research put utilisation at 23% of the budget in 2023-24 and 9% in 2024-25.

Adding the three years gives about Rs 5,619 crore by March 2026 if the revised estimate is fully spent, about 7% of the Rs 76,000 crore outlay; spending before 2023-24 was not found but is likely small. Yet the outlay is effectively committed: if every approved project received the first round’s maximum support of 50% of cost, the total would be about Rs 82,084 crore. Actual rates and state top-ups vary, so that figure is a ceiling, but it explains why new approvals needed a second round even though most of the first round’s money has not yet been paid.

## What Semicon 2.0 changes


[T2 "What Semicon 2.0 changes" 7 rows; use rows by number in a chart "table" spec]
  cols: c0  | c1 Semicon India (ISM 1.0) | c2 Semicon 2.0
  r0: Outlay | Rs 76,000 crore (2021) | Rs 127,500 crore (July 2026)
  r1: Silicon fabs | Up to 50% of project cost | 40% of eligible capital spending, for 300-mm wafer fabs
  r2: Compound, photonics, sensor and discrete fabs | Up to 50% | 35% of eligible capital spending
  r3: Assembly, test and packaging | Up to 50% | Covered as a pillar; rate not found in the sources checked
  r4: Equipment, materials, chemicals and gases | Not covered | 30% of capital spending for equipment R&D facilities, plus an incentive tied to domestically sourced components
  r5: Chip design | Design Linked Incentive | 9% of net sales for five years, capped at Rs 30 crore an application
  r6: Window | Open-ended approvals | Open for applications for three years; projects preferably within six; mid-term review after three


The second round is larger but pays less per project. Silicon fabs get 40% of eligible capital spending instead of up to 50% of project cost, and compound and specialty fabs get 35%. It also pays for things the first round did not: equipment, materials, chemicals and gases, which are where much of a fab’s supply chain lies, and chip design through a sales-linked incentive. Its time limit is tighter: applications close after three years, and projects are preferably to be completed within six.

The lesson of the first round is that approval is the easy part. The second round’s money will be spent as slowly as the first’s unless projects reach their milestones faster, and the five producing units so far are the smaller, quicker assembly plants.
