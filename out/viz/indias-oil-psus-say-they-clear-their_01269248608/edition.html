India's oil PSUs report clearing their mandatory 25% MSME procurement quota by a wide margin — the aggregate, government-wide numbers run into the 40s. Only one of the five major companies actually publishes its own individual figure. A federal audit found two of them hadn't even uploaded their procurement plans as required. And a government portal built specifically to make each PSU's compliance public doesn't, on the evidence checked for this piece, actually show it.

Top line

  • 51.07% of IOCL's eligible annual procurement in FY2025-26 came from MSEs, against a 25% floor.
  • 43.58% aggregate CPSE MSE procurement in FY2024-25, up from 36.06% in FY2023-24.
  • Eight audited CPSEs, including ONGC and IOCL, had not uploaded MSE procurement plans in a 2018 CAG audit.
Updated 28 October 2026. The 2018 change to the MSE procurement order has been checked against its Gazette notification: the Public Procurement Policy for Micro and Small Enterprises (MSEs) Amendment Order, 2018, dated 9 November 2018 (F. No. 21(22)/2018-MA), raised the annual target from 20% to 25% of procurement value and added the 3% sub-target for women-owned MSEs as a new paragraph 4A. That notification predates the Gazette index this blog now uses (which starts in September 2022) and was read through the search-index text of the Gazette copy published by Bharat Petroleum; the 4% SC/ST sub-target and the L1+15% matching rule come from the original 2012 Order, whose text was not opened.
Updated 22 October 2026. Newer aggregate figures exist for FY2025-26. A July 2026 Lok Sabha reply, as reported by KNN India and citing MSME SAMBANDH data, puts MSE procurement by central ministries and CPSEs at about ₹1,15,798 crore, or about 49% of total procurement, again well above the 25% floor. But the same figures put SC/ST-owned MSEs at about 1.7%, below their 4% sub-target, while women-owned MSEs were at about 3.8%, above their 3% sub-target. That qualifies the "comfortably above target" reading below. These figures come from a single report and cover all central ministries as well as CPSEs, so they may not match the CPSE-only series used here. The original text below is unchanged.

A 2018 CAG audit found eight CPSEs had not uploaded MSE procurement plans

  1. 8 December 2017MSME SAMBANDH portal launched to publish CPSE-by-CPSE MSE compliance
  2. 9 November 2018Amendment Order raises the annual MSE target from 20% to 25% of procurement value
  3. 2018CAG Report No. 18: eight audited CPSEs, including ONGC and IOCL, had not uploaded MSE procurement plans

What it means: CAG found that DC-MSME has no statutory power to penalise a non-compliant CPSE, and that MSEs rarely receive a response before a tender is awarded.

What to do: Check current compliance at ONGC and IOCL, since the 2018 audit does not describe their status today.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should require BPCL, HPCL and GAIL to publish annual MSE procurement shares, with SC/ST and women-owned sub-shares, in the format IOCL already uses, so that all five major oil PSUs can be checked against the 25% target, not two.
  • The Ministry of MSME should publish MSME SAMBANDH’s CPSE-wise compliance data as a downloadable annual file, each row stating the fiscal year, so that the portal launched in December 2017 to show company-level compliance can be used without live access and undated figures like ONGC’s 29.35% are anchored to a year.
  • The Department of Expenditure, with MoPNG, should issue a clarification stating how the MSE purchase preference and MoPNG’s local-content preference apply when one bidder qualifies for both on the same tender, the interaction this post could not find described anywhere in the public record.
  • The Ministry of MSME should amend the MSE Order to set a fixed response time, before tender award, for MSE grievances over denied relaxations, and objective criteria for the ‘public safety’ or ‘critical equipment’ exception, the two gaps CAG Report No. 18 of 2018 flagged.
  • The Ministry of MSME should notify the revised 469-item reserved list with an effective date, so that procurers stop working from a 358-item list that still includes obsolete entries such as blotting paper and tin boxes for postage stamps.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Sources and caveats

This piece was researched entirely through web search rather than direct page fetches: this session's network blocked direct access to essentially every primary domain relevant to this topic, including sambandh.msme.gov.in, dcmsme.gov.in, msme.gov.in, cag.gov.in, mopng.gov.in, pib.gov.in, iocl.com, and several trade-press sites, so every claim above rests on search-result summaries and cross-checking across independent secondary outlets rather than a primary document read in full. This is a materially lower-confidence sourcing basis than this blog's usual practice, and is flagged accordingly rather than papered over. The MSE Order 2012/2018's targets and mechanisms (Sections 1-2), the 358/469-item reserved-list detail, and the ₹5 lakh/₹5 crore/₹10 crore local-content thresholds are corroborated across multiple independent secondary legal and procurement-advisory sources, cross-checked against each other where more than one was found, though none was read as a primary gazette or notification text directly. MoPNG's PPLC local-content policy (Section 1) is drawn from IOCL's own hosted copy of the policy document. Aggregate and IOCL-specific MSE procurement figures (Section 3) are from Ministry of MSME's own PIB year-end reviews and IOCL's own disclosure page respectively, both reached via search-engine-visible content; the conflicting Business Standard figure is reported as found, unreconciled. The MSME SAMBANDH portal's existence and intended function (Section 3) are corroborated via PIB coverage of its 2017 launch; this piece's inability to pull current company-level data from it directly is reported as this piece's own access limitation, not a confirmed finding about the portal's current functioning. CAG Report No. 18 of 2018's findings (Section 4) are from secondary coverage summarising the report, not the report's own text read directly. Nothing in this piece is legal, procurement, or vendor-registration advice; a reader making a real MSME vendor-registration or public-procurement compliance decision should consult the relevant ministry's, CPSE's, or portal's own current, primary materials directly, not this summary.
