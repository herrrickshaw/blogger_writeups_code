## 1. Blending moves fuel tax from state VAT to a 5% GST

 Petrol sits outside GST. States tax it under their own VAT laws, ad valorem on the pre-tax price, and headline rates vary widely: about 13% in Tamil Nadu and 13.7% in Gujarat, 29–31% in Karnataka, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh, and over 35% in Telangana. Petrol also carries central excise, which goes to the Union.

 Ethanol bought by the oil marketing companies (OMCs) for blending sits inside GST, at a flat 5%: 2.5% CGST and 2.5% SGST. At the pump the blend is still sold and taxed as petrol at the state’s full VAT rate, but on the input side a rising share of every litre is 5%-GST ethanol rather than VAT-rated petrol. The state collects no VAT on that ethanol fraction when the OMC buys it; it gets only the 2.5% SGST. As the blend rises from E10 to E20 and beyond, the gap between the VAT the state would have collected on that volume as petrol and the SGST it collects on it as ethanol widens, in proportion to each state’s own VAT rate and petrol consumption.

 
 GST is destination-based. SGST on ethanol accrues to the state where the fuel is consumed, not where the ethanol is distilled. A cane-surplus state that sends ethanol to a deficit state does not keep that ethanol’s SGST; place-of-supply rules route it to the consuming state, subject to IGST settlement on the interstate movement. Production capacity and SGST revenue are two separate maps.
 

 Central excise (update of 13 October 2026). The model was built when central excise on petrol was about ₹19.90 a litre. The Finance Ministry cut it by about ₹10 a litre for domestic sale by a notification of 26 March 2026, with no change in retail prices; one report puts the relevant duty at ₹3 a litre, down from ₹13 ([Moneylife](https://moneylife.in/article/petrol-excise-duty-down-to-3-diesel-nil-no-change-in-retail-prices-as-govt-aims-to-cut-omc-losses/80059.html), [TaxTMI](https://www.taxtmi.com/news?id=71850)). Check the current Central Excise tariff before quoting a rupee figure. The state VAT arithmetic below does not depend on excise; the Union excise-loss range in Section 2, computed at the old rate, is overstated.

 

### Where the ethanol is made

 Supply comes from two feedstock families. Sugarcane and molasses distilleries, built off sugar mills, are concentrated in Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Gujarat and Bihar. Grain distilleries, using maize and damaged or surplus FCI rice, are the fastest-growing segment; Uttar Pradesh added roughly 50 crore litres a year of new grain capacity in 2025 alone, much of it around the Gorakhpur GIDA industrial cluster, alongside Punjab, Bihar and Madhya Pradesh.

 Installed capacity stood at roughly 2,000 crore litres a year for ethanol supply year 2025–26 (CareEdge, May 2026), with about 400 crore litres a year more expected by FY27. The Department of Food and Public Distribution (DFPD) sanction register is far larger, at about 4,530 crore litres across 1,212 approved projects. The same CareEdge report (“E85 Impact: Ethanol Overcapacity to Persist,” 14 May 2026) states as its headline finding that India has roughly 700 crore litres a year of structural overcapacity, with distilleries running at 60–65% utilisation, a condition it expects to last three years or more. Regional modelling from the same dataset puts Maharashtra about 277 crore litres a year in surplus and Tamil Nadu about 77 crore litres a year in deficit. Because of the destination rule above, Maharashtra’s surplus earns SGST for whichever state burns it.

 The marginal litre past E20 is increasingly grain, principally maize, the costliest feedstock per litre, which ties ethanol economics to poultry and animal-feed markets. FCI surplus rice covers an estimated ~211 crore litres a year at typical lifting rates (about 3.9 percentage points of national blend); rice-for-ethanol allocation was suspended once before, in July 2023.

 2026-27 tender (update of 7 November 2026). The tender issued on 16 October 2026 seeks about 1,100 crore litres for November 2026 to October 2027, roughly 55% of the ~2,000 crore litres of capacity, and invites no ethanol made from cane juice, sugar syrup or B-heavy molasses. Cane-belt distilleries can bid only C-heavy molasses this year, priced at ₹57.97 a litre, while grain distilleries take the rest ([The Federal](https://thefederal.com/category/business/india-sets-zero-sugar-diversion-ethanol-2026-27-259885), [Swarajya](https://swarajyamag.com/world/india-bars-ethanol-made-from-sugarcane-juice-syrup-and-b-heavy-molasses-in-2026-27-tender-as-sugar-prices-rise)). The SGST arithmetic is unaffected, since it depends on where fuel is sold, and no change to the 5% GST rate on blending ethanol was found.

## 3. A higher ethanol SGST rate narrows the loss; at 18% two states turn net-positive

 Two framings of the same lever appear in this series. The first is a dedicated state levy of 1–5% on top of today’s embedded 2.5% SGST, funded from the roughly ₹15 a litre cost gap between ethanol and the petrol it replaces, so that it need not move the pump price. The second is a single total SGST-equivalent rate on ethanol, raised from 2.5% to 5%, 7.5%, 10%, 12.5%, 15% and the standard 18% GST slab. A dedicated 5% levy is the same as a 7.5% total rate; both arrive at 18.2% of the gap closed. None of these rates is enacted or under GST Council discussion at these levels, as far as this series could determine.

 Bihar and Odisha both round to 0.31 bn litres of ethanol, but their petrol volumes differ (1.57 against 1.56 bn litres), so their figures diverge slightly at 7.5% and 10%.

 Uttar Pradesh, India’s largest petrol market, tops the list on volume despite a 19.36% VAT rate. At 10% its SGST on ethanol would rise from ₹196 crore to ₹784 crore a year, an extra ₹588 crore, against a net VAT shortfall of about ₹1,776 crore. Tamil Nadu ranks third on volume despite one of the lowest petrol VAT rates.

 The net position (the last two columns of the [top-15 table](#state-losses)) follows VAT rates as much as volume, because a low-VAT state has a smaller gap to close. Tamil Nadu improves from −₹825 crore today to −₹108 crore at 15%, an 86.9% closure, the best of any state modelled; Gujarat reaches −₹123 crore. At 18% both cross into surplus: Tamil Nadu to +₹63 crore (107% of its gap closed) and Gujarat to +₹8 crore (101%), while Uttar Pradesh closes 72% of its gap. Maharashtra and Karnataka show the opposite pattern: even at 15% they remain ₹1,240 crore and ₹1,178 crore short, more than Tamil Nadu’s whole gap today. A higher rate compresses the remaining gap fastest where it was already smallest.

 

### Karnataka’s 2024 VAT increase is a precedent for petrol, not for ethanol

 In June 2024 Karnataka raised its sales tax on petrol from 25.92% to 29.84% and on diesel from 14.34% to 18.44%, a ₹3 a litre increase expected to bring in an extra ₹2,500–3,000 crore a year. That shows a state can raise fuel taxation for meaningful revenue, but it was a change to petrol VAT, which is within state control under Article 246 and decided in a state budget. Ethanol sits inside GST, so its rate and its CGST:SGST split are GST Council decisions needing a three-quarters weighted majority, with states holding two-thirds of the weight and the Centre one-third. No single state can raise the ethanol SGST on its own.

## 5. Among donor states, the ethanol loss is largest in Maharashtra, Karnataka and Telangana

 Maharashtra, Karnataka, Gujarat and Tamil Nadu get back a fraction of what they contribute in GST, as this blog’s [devolution piece](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/08/who-collects-indias-gst-and-who.html) documented. One response on record is a state minister’s formal proposal to the GST Council to change the 50:50 CGST:SGST split to something closer to SGST 70–80% and CGST 20–30%. That split divides a single state’s own GST collection at the point of sale. It has nothing to do with the Finance Commission’s separate, larger pool of Union taxes (income tax, corporation tax, customs, Union excise, plus CGST), redistributed under Article 280 with weights toward population and income distance. A bigger SGST share would help each state in proportion to its own GST base, so it would help the largest collectors most in absolute terms and do comparatively little for Uttar Pradesh or Bihar, which is likely to widen the donor-state grievance unless paired with a change to the devolution formula.

 What donor states ask for on the record is different:

 
 
- A higher vertical share, from 41% to 50% (Karnataka, via its Chief Minister’s economic adviser).
 
- Cesses and surcharges in the shareable pool. The official devolution rate is 41%, but the effective share states receive is closer to 29–30%, because cesses and surcharges, outside Article 270’s divisible pool, have grown as a share of central revenue.
 
- New horizontal weights. Karnataka has proposed GSDP contribution as an indicator, under which its share would rise from roughly 3.64% to 5.24%.
 

 Update of 13 October 2026. The 16th Finance Commission’s report, tabled on 1 February 2026, kept the states’ share at 41% for 2026-31 and gave contribution to GDP a 10% weight in place of the 2.5% tax-effort criterion ([PRS India](https://prsindia.org/policy/report-summaries/report-of-the-16th-finance-commission-for-2026-31), [Moneylife](https://moneylife.in/article/16th-finance-commission-retains-41-percentage-tax-devolution-interse-shares-to-shift-as-criteria-are-reworked-report/79561.html)). The asks above therefore point to the Commission after 2031; the effect on Karnataka’s 3.64% to 5.24% figures was not checked against the report.

 The ethanol gap is different in kind: a GST Council matter, not a Finance Commission one, and one that falls heavily on some of the same states.

## 6. The proposal: four levers, and the share of the gap each closes

 The posts folded into this piece describe a package: change how ethanol is taxed so states stop losing VAT, and use that recovery to make lower CNG VAT affordable, on the reasoning that both fuels displace imported crude and should not be taxed more heavily than the fuels they replace.

 
 
[T12 "6. The proposal: four levers, and the share of the gap each closes" 6 rows; use rows by number in a chart "table" spec]
  cols: c0 Lever | c1 Who decides | c2 Share of the E20 gap closed
  r0: Bigger SGST share of all GST (70–80%) | GST Council | Not modelled; does not target the ethanol gap
  r1: Dedicated ethanol levy of 1–5% | GST Council | 3–18%, by rate and blend
  r2: Total ethanol SGST rate of 15% | GST Council | 45.4%
  r3: Total ethanol SGST rate of 18% | GST Council | 56.3% (Tamil Nadu and Gujarat net-positive)
  r4: Option B: state cess from the whole OMC blending margin | Not stated; no GST reclassification needed | About 40% (about 41% at E30)
  r5: Option A: blended petrol taxed wholly under state VAT at retail | GST Council | Full, by definition


 

 

### Option A: tax the blended litre as petrol at retail

 The root of the mismatch is not the 5% rate but which regime governs the product at retail. Under Option A, the GST Council would agree that blended motor spirit (E10, E20, E30 as sold at the pump) is a single retail product taxed wholly under state VAT, like pure petrol, whatever its ethanol fraction. The distillery-to-OMC supply would stay inside GST. Because no GST slice is left at retail, this closes the gap completely, but it is the heavier lift: a structural carve-out needing a three-quarters weighted-majority Council vote, and OMCs would have to re-plumb invoicing and input-tax-credit treatment for the ethanol they buy.

 

### Option B: a state compensation cess funded from OMC margin

 The [OMC-books piece](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/07/ethanol-blending-omc-books-e20-today-vs.html) put the OMC and refiner contribution gain from blending at roughly ₹7,200 crore at E20 and ₹11,100 crore at E30, funded from the roughly ₹15 a litre cost cushion between ethanol and petrol. Option B would redirect a defined share of that gain through a cess collected by OMCs at retail and remitted to the consuming state, calibrated to each state’s petrol VAT rate. Because it draws on margin rather than a higher tax on ethanol, it avoids the “27% rate needed” problem. But even the whole OMC gain, which OMCs would resist giving up, closes only about 40% of the gap at E20 (7,200 against 17,863) and about 41% at E30. It is a partial fix.

 

### Linking ethanol and CNG

 A state asked to accept lower CNG VAT, a revenue giveaway on its own, is easier to persuade in the same Council session that offers it a way to recover ethanol losses. Telangana is the clean case: a 35.2% petrol VAT and a 14.5% CNG VAT, so its ethanol-side recovery and CNG-side giveaway sit close enough to self-fund within its own ledger. Odisha, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh are also in the high-CNG-VAT cluster and carry petrol VAT of 28%, 29% and 24% (net E20 losses of ₹636 crore, ₹1,116 crore and ₹410 crore); Jharkhand and Puducherry are not in the petrol model. Whether those states would come out even has not been modelled. For states without a clean match the proposal is a pooled fund, administratively like the 2017–2022 GST compensation cess pool, with ethanol recoveries flowing in and CNG relief drawn out. CNG VAT itself is a state budget decision, the same lever Karnataka used in 2024, so that half of the package needs no Council consensus; the ethanol half does.

 

### Crude for petrochemicals

 The ethanol programme substituted a cumulative 316 lakh tonnes of crude and saved more than ₹1.97 lakh crore of foreign exchange between ESY 2014-15 and June 2026, per the government ([Deccan Herald](https://deccanherald.com/business/ethanol-blending-leads-to-rs-197-lakh-crore-forex-savings-centre-4080737)); earlier figures were 244 lakh tonnes and ₹1.84 lakh crore, and a separate ministry estimate of ₹1.44 lakh crore saved on crude import costs has not been updated. The next milestones are E27 around 2028 and E30 by 2030. Crude-derived petrochemical feedstock demand does not go away: naphtha and reformate, the same petrol-range refinery streams burned as fuel, feed the ethylene, propylene and BTX chains behind India’s imported polymers and chemicals (see this blog’s [petrochemical import-tree piece](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/07/indias-petrochemical-import-tree-from.html)). Transport demand that moves to ethanol or CNG/CBG frees crude and refining capacity that could in principle feed that demand. This is a directional argument; quantifying it would need refinery product-slate data that this series did not have.

 

### What the proposal does not solve

 
 
- Council approval is not a formality. Option A, any ethanol rate change and a pooled CNG fund all need a three-quarters weighted-majority vote, and a proposal that helps some states (Telangana, Maharashtra, Karnataka) more than others is the kind that stalls.
 
- Revenue-neutral in aggregate is not revenue-neutral per state. Some states would pay into a pool more than they draw out.
 
- Below 18%, a rate rise leaves every state short. At 15%, Maharashtra and Karnataka still lose more than ₹1,100 crore each; Option B alone leaves about 60% of the gap open.
 
- CNG prices are not purely a VAT problem. Transport and terrain costs in the hill states mean harmonised VAT would not equalise the price map.
 
- Drivers pay a cost none of these levers returns. The model holds the pump price flat, so the mileage penalty, about 7% at E30 per the OMC-books piece, falls on consumers. At E20 against pure petrol (Delhi price, 15,000 km a year) the vehicle_fuel_mileage model puts the extra fuel spend at ₹1,160 a year for a two-wheeler, ₹3,989 for a hatchback, ₹4,255 for a sedan and ₹4,910 for a compact SUV (₹0.08–0.33 per km). A three-way gain for states, consumers and OMCs would need part of the ₹15 a litre cushion passed through as a lower retail price, a pricing-formula question this series did not model.
