India is the largest export market on earth for California almonds and the second-largest almond consumer after the United States. It bought roughly 283,000 tonnes in calendar 2024, worth about US$1.09 billion — and grew about 4,100 tonnes itself. Almost the entire market is an import market, 95 per cent of it arrives still inside the shell for reasons that are pure tariff arithmetic, and the single biggest demand event of the year is now weeks away.

India bought 69% more almonds from 2019 to 2024 at a unit price a quarter lower

What it means: Volume grew faster than value, so India bought far more almonds for not much more money.

What to do: Compare years only on the calendar-year, product-weight basis, because the USDA series sits much lower.

Shelled almonds pay a ₹100 per kilo duty against ₹35 for in-shell

What it means: Duty is levied per kilo on the weight crossing the border, so shipping shells is cheaper than paying duty on kernels.

What to do: Ship in-shell, since freighting the shells costs less than the ₹100 duty on each kilo of kernel.

The US supplied 86 to 94 per cent of in-shell import value, Australia far less

What it means: The Australian quota is not binding on the market as a whole, but it gives an early-year edge to whoever ships first.

What to do: Check the in-shell premium each season, since a shell premium over kernel parity erodes the case for shipping shells.

What would fix this

  • DGCI&S should publish monthly almond imports at eight-digit tariff-line level by port of discharge, free of charge, so that the port-wise split this post could not establish without paid shipment-level records becomes public trade statistics.
  • DGCI&S should publish a kernel-equivalent weight alongside product weight for in-shell almond imports, using a stated conversion factor, so that its calendar-year series can be reconciled with USDA’s marketing-year figures instead of the two sitting 35 to 55 per cent apart.
  • The Department of Commerce should publish, each quarter, how much of the 34,000-tonne Australian almond tariff-rate quota under ECTA has been used, so that importers can see remaining first-come, first-served quota before booking cargo rather than discovering it at the border.
  • CBIC should publish a dated history of effective customs duty for each tariff line, listing every notification that changed it, so that the almond duty sequence — ₹35, ₹42, back to ₹35 — is available from one official page and erroneous figures like ₹41 stop circulating.
  • The Department of Commerce should post the full India–Australia ECTA tariff schedules, including staging annexes, as searchable text on its own website, so that in-quota rates such as ₹17.50 per kilo can be checked against the treaty itself rather than inferred.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

Three gaps are worth stating rather than papering over. No free public source publishes a port-wise percentage split specifically for almonds — the shipment-level records exist commercially, but the port-of-discharge field sits behind a paywall, so the port table above describes roles and capability, not measured share. The precise ECTA staging annex could not be retrieved directly, because the Australian trade department page blocks automated access; the ₹17.50 rate is corroborated from the customs notification and the Almond Board’s India profile rather than from the treaty text. And no calendar-2025 Comtrade data has been released at the time of writing, so the series above ends at 2024 — Comtrade reporting runs 12 to 18 months behind.

Container loadability figures circulating in the trade — roughly 19 tonnes of in-shell cubing out a 40-foot high-cube, against about 22 tonnes of kernel weighing out — are derived from bulk density and payload limits rather than from an actual packing list, and should be checked against one before anyone prices against them.
