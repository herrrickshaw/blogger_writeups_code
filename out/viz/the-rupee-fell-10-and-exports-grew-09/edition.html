Rupee and exports in FY2025-26: the rupee fell 10%, merchandise exports grew 0.9%

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 0.93% merchandise export growth in FY2025-26, while the rupee fell about 10.4% against the dollar.
  • 4.15 world-GDP elasticity of Indian exports, against 1.07 for the real exchange rate.
  • 56.2% of merchandise exports come from industries whose import intensity is above average.
Updated 7 November 2026. The rupee has kept falling since FY2025-26 ended, to a record closing low of ₹95.71 to the dollar in mid-May 2026, and this time exports did surge: April–September 2026 merchandise exports were $259.89 billion, up about 18.5% from $219.30 billion. The growth was led by petroleum products, electronics and engineering goods, with petroleum lifted by the oil-price spike after the Hormuz disruption, so the jump is better read as world prices and demand than as the currency at work, consistent with this piece’s argument. The FY2025-26 figures below are unchanged. Sources: Maritime Gateway (Commerce Ministry data), report of the 95.71 close (snippet-only; check against RBI reference rates).

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Merchandise exports barely moved despite a roughly 10% weaker rupee

What it means: Even without petroleum, export growth stayed far below what a 10% price advantage would be expected to buy.

What to do: Judge exchange-rate policy against export volumes, not against the headline rupee move alone.

Imported inputs absorb much of the rupee gain for most exporters

What it means: When the rupee falls, firms that import heavily see input costs rise almost as fast as their rupee revenue.

What to do: Trade-intensive firms should hedge and diversify input suppliers, as the Exim Bank's sectoral advice says.

Only food and agro gets the full textbook benefit from a weaker rupee

What it means: Sector import intensity runs from 8.7% in food and agro to 80% in petroleum, which sets each sector's currency exposure.

What to do: Trade-intensive sectors should hedge and diversify input suppliers before relying on a weaker rupee.

Demand moves Indian exports about four times as much as the exchange rate

What it means: In the Exim Bank's own model, foreign demand matters far more to exports than the exchange rate does.

What to do: Exporters should push market access and buyer financing rather than rely on currency competitiveness alone.

What would fix this

  • India Exim Bank should publish a note reconciling Occasional Paper 188 and Occasional Paper 228, which reach opposite conclusions on whether depreciation or appreciation raises exports, so that its own research does not leave both answers standing unexplained.
  • India Exim Bank should re-estimate the Occasional Paper 228 model with data beyond 2021 Q3 and an explicit imported-input cost variable, and publish the results, so that the import-intensity explanation for its +1.07 REER elasticity is tested rather than offered after the fact.
  • The Ministry of Commerce and Industry, through DGCI&S, should publish an annual breakdown of India’s merchandise exports by invoicing currency and by sector, so that the dollar-invoicing channel this post identifies can be measured for India directly rather than inferred from cross-country research.
  • The RBI should shorten the lag on its published foreign-exchange intervention data and report spot and forward operations separately, so that intervention during episodes like the FY2025-26 slide from about ₹85.5 to ₹94.4 can be assessed close to when it happened.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Table: 4. How the central bank actually operates on the rate
LeverHow it works
Spot interventionBuying or selling dollars against rupees with authorised dealer banks. Directly moves the rate, directly moves rupee liquidity.
Forward marketThe RBI says the effect on inter-bank spot rates is the same, but the forward leg gives it “greater manoeuvrability for modulating domestic rupee liquidity” — the FX effect without the immediate liquidity effect.
Indirect executionThe general preference is to deal through selected banks rather than openly, because confidentiality “enhances effectiveness.” Intervention data is published monthly, with a long lag.
SterilisationOMOs, CRR changes, and the Market Stabilisation Scheme — the MSS being the preferred tool during heavy inflows. It mops up the rupees created when the RBI buys dollars.
Capital-account managementFor durable effect, intervention is paired with rules on FII limits, ECB ceilings, NRI deposit rates, and restrictions on rebooking cancelled forward contracts.
Table: 6. What Exim banks actually do — and what they do not
InstrumentWhat it does for the exchange-rate problem
Lines of Credit to foreign governments, banks and institutionsThe overseas buyer gets financing tied to purchasing from India. Demand is created contractually rather than through price competitiveness — the currency becomes irrelevant to the sale.
Pre- and post-shipment credit , in rupees or foreign currencyLetting an exporter borrow in the same currency it will be paid in is a natural hedge; it removes the mismatch rather than betting on the rate.
Buyer's credit and project-export financeConverts a price-sensitive spot sale into a long-dated contracted flow, which strips out short-term currency risk.
Guarantees and letters of creditShifts payment risk off the exporter, who is usually too small to carry it.
