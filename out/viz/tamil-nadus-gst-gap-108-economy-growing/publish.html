Tamil Nadu's GSDP grew 10.83% in real (inflation-adjusted) terms in FY2025-26, on top of 11.19% the year before — and 13.1% in nominal terms (current prices, ₹31.19 lakh crore to roughly ₹35.3 lakh crore — estimates for FY2025-26 range ₹35.3–35.7 lakh crore depending on source, see Section 2) — well above the national average either way, with manufacturing alone up over 15%. Its domestic GST collection that same year: ₹1,30,248 crore, down 0.7% from ₹1,31,115 crore in FY2024-25. GST is a tax on nominal transaction values, so the more directly comparable figure is nominal GSDP growth (13.1%), not real growth — and against that benchmark, the gap between economic growth and GST growth is wider, not narrower. That gap — not a collapse, but a real and measurable divergence between how fast the state's output is growing and how fast its GST revenue is growing — is the actual opportunity here. This piece lays out what the numbers show, what Tamil Nadu's tax administration is already doing about it, and where the real remaining lever sits.

What it means: Tamil Nadu's domestic GST collection grew more slowly each year and fell 0.7% in FY2025-26.

What to do: Read the four enforcement measures against monthly collection data before judging whether they are working.

Real growth of 10.83% did not reach GST collection in FY2025-26

What it means: Real GSDP grew 10.83% and manufacturing over 15% in FY2025-26, yet GST collection fell 0.7%.

What to do: Test whether the gap is capture rather than growth by comparing manufacturing GST with e-way bill volumes.

Tamil Nadu's registered businesses grew faster than most of its peers

What it means: Tamil Nadu's taxpayer base widened faster than Maharashtra and Gujarat, so the revenue gap is not a shortage of registrants.

What to do: Find a services-side compliance tool that reaches the 53% of gross state value added that goods-movement measures miss.

What would fix this

  • The Tamil Nadu Commercial Taxes Department should publish annual GST collections from the state split between goods and services, by broad HSN and SAC section, so that whether the gap sits in the services sector — 53% of state GVA — can be tested rather than inferred.
  • GSTN should publish state-wise collections by registrant turnover band and year of registration, so that the timing-lag explanation for Tamil Nadu’s 38.7% jump in GSTINs alongside flat SGST can be checked against what new registrants actually pay.
  • The GST Council should publish its estimate of the revenue effect of the September 2025 rate cuts, state by state, so that Tamil Nadu’s 0.7% fall can be separated into the rate-cut effect and the capture gap this piece identifies against 10.8% real GSDP growth.
  • The Tamil Nadu Commercial Taxes Department should publish quarterly results from RFID and FASTag e-way-bill cross-verification and the Ship-To GSTIN rule, including mismatches found and tax recovered, so that the state’s goods-movement measures can be judged on what they collect.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
