India grows more crop than ever, and its soils are running short of organic carbon. Biochar, crop waste charred slowly in little or no air and ploughed back in, is the one remedy that could address both and earn carbon credits as well. The question is where it would actually take hold. This piece models ten crop residues across eighteen states. It asks how much spare residue each pairing leaves, how badly the soil there needs carbon, and whether anyone locally is already making, testing or buying biochar.

The short answer is that it depends on what you value most. Punjab’s rice straw is the biggest single feedstock by a wide margin: about 72 lakh tonnes of char a year if all of its spare straw were charred. Punjab tops the index on the base weights, but its soils are not the most depleted, and almost nothing is being done with biochar there beyond the agricultural university’s own kiln design. Only two pairings stay in the top ten under every set of weights tried: rice straw in Odisha, where soils are acid and carbon developers are already working with farmers, and cotton stalk in Maharashtra, where ICAR’s cotton institutes and two credit developers are active.

## Bigger harvests are taking more out of the soil

Between 2003-06 and 2022-25, the nutrients carried off in harvests of six major crops rose fastest in Maharashtra (+175%), Madhya Pradesh (+130%) and Bihar (+127%). These are rough estimates: tonnes harvested multiplied by FAO’s per-tonne coefficients for nitrogen, phosphate and potash, which count the straw as well as the grain. Punjab’s rose a more modest 28% from an already high base.

The table shows a gap far larger than any other: potash. Rice and wheat take up more potassium than nitrogen, and most of it sits in the straw. In Punjab, the six crops remove about 179 kg of K₂O a hectare, while farmers apply about 7 kg per hectare of cropped land, because Punjab’s fertiliser is nearly all urea: 29.8 parts nitrogen to one of potash in 2024-25. The two figures use different hectares, so the gap is indicative, not exact. When straw is burned, the National Policy for Management of Crop Residues counts the loss from each tonne at 5.5 kg of nitrogen, 2.3 kg of phosphorus, 25 kg of potassium, 1.2 kg of sulphur and about 400 kg of organic carbon. Biochar keeps part of that carbon in the field for decades. Potassium does not burn off at kiln temperatures the way nitrogen does, so char that goes back to the field returns much of it too.

The link between harvest growth and low soil carbon is weaker than the story suggests. Across the eighteen states, the rank correlation between fertiliser use per hectare and the organic-carbon index is only -0.24. Between growth in nutrient removal and the index it is -0.10, close to nothing. The states with the least soil carbon are Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan and Haryana, long-farmed plains and dry lands where heat burns organic matter off fast, not the states whose harvests grew fastest. Soil carbon reflects decades of climate and practice. A decade of harvest growth barely shows in it.

## Where the spare residue is

The feedstock model takes the average harvest of 2022-23 to 2024-25 from the Ministry of Agriculture’s UPAg data. It multiplies each crop by a residue-to-product ratio: 1.5 tonnes of straw per tonne of milled rice; 1.5 for wheat; 2.3 for maize stalk and cob; 3.8 tonnes of stalk per tonne of seed cotton; 0.33 tonnes of bagasse per tonne of cane; and 1.1 to 2.5 for pulses and oilseeds. It then keeps only the surplus. For rice, that is the state share in the MNRE biomass atlas: 99% in Punjab, where almost no straw is fed to animals, and under 5% in Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Gujarat and Maharashtra, where most of it is. Other crops take TIFAC’s national surplus fractions. Char yields are 26% to 35% of dry weight, depending on the residue.

The national surplus comes to 210 million tonnes a year, within the 178–230 Mt range of published estimates. Charred, it would make about 64 Mt of biochar, or 57 Mt counting bagasse at half. Rice straw is 31% of that, and 42% of the rice-straw char is in Punjab.

## What the carbon-market rules allow

India’s draft offset methodology for biochar under the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (BM AG04, “Production and Use of Biochar”) accepts in-field crop residue such as cereal straw, maize straw, stalks and prunings. It also accepts processing residue: rice husk, maize cob, nutshell, bagasse and spent grain. It excludes food crops grown as feedstock and mixed municipal waste. Production must reach a hydrogen-to-organic-carbon ratio below 0.7, and the credit is the tonnes of char times its organic-carbon share times 44/12. The methodology is a draft and is not yet among the scheme’s approved methodologies.

The rule that matters most for this map is leakage. Residue that something else already uses, such as bagasse in a sugar mill’s boiler, has to show that diverting it does not push that user onto fossil fuel. Residue that was previously left to rot or burned does not. That is why bagasse counts at half weight here: the mills already burn it. The European Biochar Certificate’s positive list (version 10.1) is broader still: “harvest residues such as straw, cabbage, leaves, stalks, husks” are allowed in every certificate class, with a caution on pesticide and heavy-metal residues. So every residue in the model qualifies under both systems; the only question is how much of it is spare.

At an assumed 35% organic carbon for rice-straw char (PAU measured 30–36%) and 50% for the rest, the eligible char would store about 94 million tonnes of CO₂ a year before any project, permanence or leakage deductions. That is a ceiling, not a forecast. CEEW counts about 114,000 Indian biochar credits issued so far, and Platts assessed removal credits at about $135 a tonne.

## Where the soil needs it

The soil-need score weights four things:

- Organic-carbon index, 40%. The 2023-24 Soil Health Card index for organic carbon, from MoSPI’s EnviStats 2024. It runs from 1 if every sample tested low to 3 if every sample tested high.

- Acid soils, 30%. Their share of each state’s area, from ICAR’s 2025 survey. Biochar’s liming effect gives its largest yield gains on acid soils.

- Fertiliser use per hectare, 15%.

- How lopsided nitrogen use is against potash, 15%.

The index puts Tamil Nadu (1.12), Uttar Pradesh (1.13) and Rajasthan (1.18) at the bottom. That matches ICRIER’s reading of the 2024-25 cards, which found 88% and 87% of samples low in Tamil Nadu and Uttar Pradesh. Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, West Bengal and Assam are mostly acid soil. In Odisha a GIZ project found biochar’s largest benefit on acid soils, and a groundnut trial on acid red soil gained 29% in pod yield.

## Who is already trying

There is no national ICAR biochar programme and no biochar line in the Rs 4,549 crore crop-residue scheme, which pays for machinery. The Ministry of Agriculture’s 2024 framework for voluntary carbon markets in agriculture does name biochar. Activity is local, and it clusters in central and western India. ICAR’s cotton institutes (CICR in Nagpur and CIRCOT in Mumbai) work on cotton-stalk char. In January 2026 Varaha signed an offtake with Microsoft for more than 100,000 tonnes of removals over three years from cotton stalk bought in Vidarbha, to be charred in up to 18 gasification reactors. Green Carbon’s project document for two plants near Nagpur plans 6,480 tonnes of cotton-stalk char a year. Punjab Agricultural University added a pyramid kiln, “Prali Char”, to its kharif 2026 Package of Practices: 12 quintals of straw give 8 quintals of char, and 2 tonnes an acre is credited with saving 16 kg of nitrogen. In a 962-household survey in Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra and Odisha, farmers using biochar reported paddy yields 4.3% higher and net returns 29% higher.

Fires are the other signal. Residue that is burned is residue nobody else wants. In the 2025 season Madhya Pradesh, not Punjab, had the most farm fires of the six states ICAR tracks: 17,067 of 33,028, more than half. The model does not add fires to the index, because CREAMS covers only six states and the surplus fractions already count burned straw as spare. They are shown here as a check.

## Four kinds of region

- Punjab and Haryana: the most straw, the least uptake. They hold the largest single feedstock, and Haryana’s soils are among the lowest in carbon. Policy money goes to machines and to non-burning bonuses, not to char. PAU’s kiln in the Package of Practices is the opening.

- Maharashtra, Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh: the most ready. Cotton, soybean and wheat stalk, ICAR and KVK work, and three credit developers. Madhya Pradesh had the most farm fires of the six states ICAR tracks in 2025, so much of its straw is plainly uncommitted.

- The acid belt, Odisha to Assam: the biggest agronomic payoff. Rice straw is modest per state, but acid soils are where trials show the largest gains. Odisha has projects. Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh rank second and fourth of the eighteen for soil need, and this search found no biochar work in either.

- Uttar Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and Rajasthan: the most depleted soils, the least activity. Uttar Pradesh’s cane and wheat residue is large, but almost all its rice straw already has a use, and its bagasse has a buyer in the mills.
