A quarter-acre of shallow ponds, harvested every 15–18 days, can out-earn an acre of most row crops — which is the entire case for calling spirulina “green gold.” It is also, right now, an Axiom-4 astronaut’s microgravity experiment, a malnutrition supplement with a real efficacy dispute attached, an unproven biofuel feedstock, and the organism a Kerala carbon-capture startup is using to scrub CO₂ out of building air. China still grows most of the world’s supply; South Korea, it turns out, mostly buys it in.

A spirulina batch is ready in 15-18 days, against 90-120 for a cereal crop

What it means: Batches take roughly 15-18 days, so one pond can harvest several times in a season, while cereal cycles take 90-120 days.

What to do: Check the batch-cycle figures against a grower's own harvest log before using them in any farm-income plan.

What would fix this

  • The ICMR–National Institute of Nutrition should publish the protocol, sample size and results of its spirulina supplementation trial, including the reported 40% fall in children’s beta-carotene levels, so the open dispute over efficacy can be judged on the trial itself rather than on press coverage.
  • The Women and Child Development departments of Karnataka and Haryana should publish the evaluation reports of their anganwadi spirulina pilots, with method and sample sizes, so the 46–67% malnutrition-reduction figure cited for them can be checked before the programmes are extended.
  • FSSAI should publish a notice setting out the category, standards and licensing route that apply to spirulina powder sold as food or supplied in nutrition programmes, so a product that is neither a certified drug nor a certified food is not distributed to children without a stated quality benchmark.
  • DGCI&S should report spirulina exports as an identifiable line in TradeStat, so the $6.69 million 2024 export figure and the roughly fourfold volume growth since 2020 can be checked against an official register rather than commercial trade-data aggregators.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. No verified HPCL-spirulina partnership was found; HPCL's actual algae-adjacent tie-up is with Sea6 Energy for tropical seaweed biomass (a different organism, converted via hydrothermal liquefaction), covered separately on this blog. The small-acreage income figures in Section 1 come from commercial farming guides and trade press, not an audited survey, and should be read as illustrative claims rather than typical outcomes. The malnutrition-supplementation efficacy figures and the National Institute of Nutrition's beta-carotene concern are both reported as stated by their respective sources; this piece has not adjudicated which is right, because the underlying clinical evidence wasn't accessible here. Biofuel-from-spirulina figures are lab-study results, not evidence of any commercial production pathway. China's production-share estimate has a wide range depending on source, and no single authoritative figure was identified. The claim that no major Korean spirulina grower exists is a negative finding from the sourcing used, not proof that none exists. Section 3's EID Parry tonnage (>8,000 t/yr) is a national, company-wide figure and is not directly comparable to Section 6's Tamil Nadu-wide tonnage (~1,500 t/yr) — the two describe different scopes (company-wide vs. single-state) rather than conflicting on the same measurement, though both still come from market-research aggregators rather than a company filing or government statistic. The wastewater-treatment removal percentages, the bioplastic degradation claims, and the antiviral/immune clinical results are all individual published-study findings, not consensus positions; none has been independently re-verified by re-reading the underlying primary paper in full. Two Korean algae-fuel claims raised during this piece's research did not survive checking and were dropped: an "Il Yoo Biotech" Euglena-SAF venture (not found under that name anywhere) and a Kumho Petrochemical microalgae-bioethanol plant at Yeosu (Kumho's real, verified Yeosu project is a CO₂ capture facility, not an algae plant). The Air Incheon algae-SAF deal and S-Oil's presence in Korea's SAF consortium are both confirmed as reported by trade press (qcintel.com), but the specific claim that S-Oil's SAF program is algae-feedstock-focused was not independently confirmed — industry reporting describes Korea's SAF consortium plant as waste-oil-based, with algae mentioned only as a general industry feedstock category.
