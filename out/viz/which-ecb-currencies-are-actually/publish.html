"Raising Green Debt Globally" identified 13 currencies deep enough for an Indian issuer to actually borrow in, ranked by headline policy-rate gap against India's 5.25% repo — and flagged Switzerland's franc, the widest gap of all, as "close to the least useful," on the reasoning that hedging costs would eat most of the saving. That was an inference from covered-interest-parity theory, not a measured number. This piece measures it: real 10-year INR volatility against the reachable currencies this site now has data for, to test whether the currencies with the biggest rate gap are also the ones actually riskiest to borrow in.

Top line

  • 5.50pp policy-rate gap against India for the Swiss franc, but 12.53% annualised rupee volatility, about double the dollar's 6.00%.
  • 12.09% yen volatility against the rupee: the same currency that gave unhedged borrowers a favourable five-year trend.

What would fix this

  • The RBI should add to its monthly ECB release the currency of each borrowing and the share the borrower reports as hedged, so unhedged exposure to the most volatile funding currencies, the Swiss franc and the yen, is visible in aggregate after the February 2026 liberalisation.
  • The RBI should publish monthly onshore deliverable-forward turnover and average forward premia for the rupee against the dollar, euro, pound, yen and Swiss franc, so the effect of the April 2026 NDF ban on hedging costs for ECB borrowers can be measured.
  • The RBI should publish a review, one year after they took effect, of the rupee NDF ban and the $100 million net open position cap, covering hedging volumes and costs for residents, so the narrowing of the hedge market is assessed with data rather than assumed.
  • SEBI should require listed companies to disclose unhedged foreign-currency borrowings by currency in a standard table in their annual reports, so investors can see which firms carry open Swiss franc or yen exposure, currencies roughly twice as volatile against the rupee as the dollar.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. "Gap ÷ volatility" is a simplification, not a formal Sharpe ratio or a hedged-cost model — it ignores the actual forward-points cost of hedging (which, by covered interest parity, should already be close to the rate gap itself, making the ratio closer to "unhedged risk per unit of theoretical unhedged reward" than a true cost-benefit figure). It also doesn't cover the remaining 8 of the green-debt piece's 13 reachable currencies (SEK, DKK, CAD, KRW, CNY already covered elsewhere on this site, HKD, NOK, AUD) — INR-pair volatility for those was not computed for this piece. Nor does it model how the April 2026 NDF ban and NOP cap specifically change deliverable-forward pricing or liquidity — only that the offshore alternative is now closed.
