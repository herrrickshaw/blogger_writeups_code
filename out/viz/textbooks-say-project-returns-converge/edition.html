The textbook story is that a project's return chases down toward its cost of capital as competitors pile in and margins get arbitraged away. The empirical research behind that story tells a slower, messier version: on average, roughly 79% of a company's deviation from the average return on capital carries into the following year, and across the full history of US public companies, the average return has sat slightly below its cost of capital on the traditional measure (and slightly above it once internally generated intangibles are counted), not neatly converged to meet it. India's own 2026 sector data, already covered on this blog, shows both halves of the story playing out in real time.

Top line

  • 0.79 average persistence factor: about four-fifths of a company's gap from average ROIC carries into the next year.
  • 0.10-0.30 fade rate per year across sectors, so convergence takes years, not a single planning cycle.
  • -0.8 pts average ROIC-WACC spread for US public companies, 1970-2024: slightly negative, not converged to zero.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Returns settle into a wide, persistent spread, not a tight band near zero

What it means: The average company sat slightly below its cost of capital over five decades, with wide dispersion around that average.

What to do: Judge a company's returns against its own five-year record, since one-year correlations overstate how fast the gap fades.

Competitive advantage fades slowly, at 0.10-0.30 a year, not in one year

What it means: On average about 79% of a company's deviation from average ROIC is still there the next year.

What to do: Treat any gap between returns and cost of capital as lasting years, not one planning cycle, when valuing a project.

India's cement margins are projected to compress as capacity chases demand

What it means: Government-infrastructure demand is pulling in capacity, and margins are giving ground as the textbook mechanism predicts.

What to do: Check the cement and steel figures against the earlier ICRA piece's sourcing notes before relying on them.

What would fix this

  • The Reserve Bank of India should publish, in its statistics on listed non-financial companies, a long industry-wise series of return on capital employed with 25th and 75th percentile values, so an Indian fade rate can be estimated from domestic data rather than inferred from US studies of 1970–2024.
  • SEBI should require listed non-financial companies to report return on capital employed in their annual reports on one standard definition, so the spread between returns and cost of capital can be compared across firms and sectors instead of being rebuilt from differently defined figures.
  • The Department of Fertilizers should publish plant-wise urea capacity utilisation every year, so the prediction that below-cost-of-capital returns in fertilisers lead to reduced supply can be tested against data rather than read from occasional sector commentary.
  • NHAI should publish, for each toll concession, the annual toll-rate revision applied and toll revenue collected, so the inflation-linked revisions of about 3.2 per cent for newer and 1.6–2.0 per cent for older projects can be checked project by project.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Corrected 25 October 2026. The -0.8-point average spread (and the -4.3 and +5.7 percentiles) applies to traditional ROIC only; on ROIC adjusted for internally generated intangibles the average is +1.9 points, so the claim that the average sat below cost of capital is measure-dependent. The year-five IPO finding no longer says the spread settles at a "lower level", the 0.79 persistence factor is described as persistence of deviation from average ROIC (from five-year correlations), and all figures now cite the April 2026 Counterpoint Global paper, read in full.

Sources and caveats

This piece's core empirical claims trace to Morgan Stanley Counterpoint Global Insights' published research (associated with Michael Mauboussin): Mauboussin and Callahan, "Competitive Advantage Period: The Neglected Value Driver" (14 April 2026), for the 0.79 average persistence factor, the 0.70-0.90 sectoral range and 0.10-0.30 fade rates (Exhibit 11), the 1990-2022 IPO cohort's year-five stabilisation, and the 1970-2024 ROIC-WACC spreads (Exhibit 9); and "Measuring the Moat" (15 October 2024) for the consumer-advantage and production-advantage framing. morganstanley.com refuses direct downloads, so both documents were read in full through the Internet Archive's stored copies of the same PDFs. Readers relying on any of these figures for a real analytical or investment decision should still check them against the paper itself. Aswath Damodaran's (NYU Stern) India-specific cost-of-capital dataset was also identified during this piece's research as a potential source of India-specific WACC figures, but aswathdamodaran.substack.com was likewise blocked from direct fetch, and the specific figures found via search (a reported 12.08% USD figure at one point in Damodaran's distribution of Indian companies' cost of capital, as of early 2025) could not be confirmed with enough precision about what exactly that figure represents (a mean, median, or specific percentile) to be responsibly cited in the body of this piece; it is noted here rather than in the main text for that reason. Section 4's connection to India's 2026 sector data draws on this blog's own separately-published and separately-sourced ICRA sector-outlook piece; that piece's own sourcing notes, not this one, carry the primary citations for each figure reused here. Nothing in this piece is investment, valuation, or financial advice; a reader relying on any specific figure for a real decision should verify it directly against the cited primary research, not this summary.
