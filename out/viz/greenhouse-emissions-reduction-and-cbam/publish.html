In thermodynamics, an ideal gas is the simplified model; a real gas is what you actually get once intermolecular forces and finite volume intrude. India's emissions-reduction and CBAM-compliance story has the same split — an EU-bound CBAM exposure of roughly $8.2 billion that is very real (worldwide export exposure in the same chapters runs to $20.7 billion), and a trade-agreement response that, on the evidence so far, is still mostly ideal.

The Q4 price is due on 4 January 2027, and from 2027 the price will be published weekly. These prices apply to the 2026 imports that must be declared by 30 September 2027. India’s iron and steel targets under CCTS are still a revised draft (26 June 2026) and fertiliser still has no target notification. Updated 26 October 2026. CCTS has nine designated sectors: aluminium (including secondary aluminium), cement, chlor-alkali, pulp and paper, petroleum refining, petrochemicals, textiles, iron and steel, and fertiliser. Seven have notified targets. Earlier versions of this piece counted secondary aluminium as a separate sector, giving eight notified and ten in all; the counts below are corrected, and secondary aluminium now sits with aluminium in the CBAM overlap table. The 490 entities and the batch dates are unchanged.
Updated 22 October 2026. This piece asks what CBAM will cost Indian exporters "once CBAM's definitive regime takes effect". That regime has in fact applied since 1 January 2026. Under the EU's October 2025 simplification (Regulation (EU) 2025/2083), the payment falls due later: CBAM certificates go on sale from 1 February 2027, and the first annual declaration, covering 2026 imports, is due by 30 September 2027, according to compliance guides (CBAM Guide, Andersen). So the open question is what Indian steel and fertiliser exporters will pay for goods already being shipped, not when the regime starts. The original text below is unchanged.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change should publish a draft GEI-target notification for the fertiliser sector with a fixed comment window, as it did for 255 iron and steel units in June 2026, so neither CBAM-exposed CCTS sector is left without a target.
  • MoEFCC should finalise the iron and steel GEI targets and publish a summary of objections received in the 60-day window and how each was dealt with, so the 2026–27 compliance year rests on a legally binding target rather than a draft.
  • The Department of Commerce should publish an implementation schedule for the India–EU FTA's CBAM provisions, including the amount and timeline of the financial assistance and the status of verifier recognition, so the framework language in the PIB release can be checked against what is delivered.
  • The Department of Commerce should publish an estimate of CBAM costs per tonne for Indian iron and steel and fertiliser exports to the EU, so the roughly $8.2 billion EU-bound exposure is translated into the compliance cost exporters will actually face.
  • The Ministry of Steel should publish the emission intensity of plants receiving Specialty Steel PLI support, so it is visible whether the ₹6,322 crore outlay is building cleaner steel capacity or only more of it, given CBAM taxes carbon intensity at the EU border.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
