India grows roughly 60 per cent of the world's jute and is the world's second-largest silk producer, and it has one of the three largest sheep flocks on the planet. None of those three facts means what it sounds like. India produces most of the world's jute and exports almost none of it — Bangladesh, the number-two producer, does 70 per cent of the world's jute exporting. India's silk output has grown for a decade and it still imports over $150 million of raw silk a year. Its sheep population is large and its wool production is falling, because a market can shrink in quality even while it holds steady in headcount. Two different structural problems, dressed up as one story about "natural fibres."

Top line

  • ~60% of world jute production is India's, yet Bangladesh ships about 70% of jute exports.
  • 92.2 million kg of raw wool imported in 2023-24, nearly three times the 33.69 million kg India produced.
  • 42,033 t of raw silk produced in 2025-26, yet India still imports upward of $150 million of raw silk a year.
Updated 7 November 2026. The note below said no jute-packaging order later than the one valid to 30 June 2025 could be found. One now exists: the Ministry of Textiles’ S.O. 5794(E) of 27 October 2026 amends the April 2025 order so that its requirements run “up to the 31st January, 2027 or until further orders”. Legal summaries say the percentages are unchanged (100% of foodgrains, 20% of sugar). Reports differ on which earlier end date the amendment replaced, so the gap between June 2025 and October 2026 is not settled here (Corpseed, TeamLease RegTech, UNI; the Gazette text itself was not read).
Updated 27 October 2026. Several jute and wool figures are corrected. Jute: the 22 April 2025 order cited as the “current” reservation was valid only to 30 June 2025 and no later order was found; the 20% applies to sugar production; and the ₹7,500 crore of purchases and 7 lakh workers were 2020 figures — the Cabinet’s 2023-24 decision gives about ₹12,000 crore, 4 lakh workers and 40 lakh farm families. Wool: India had 74.26 million sheep in the 2019 Livestock Census, not “over 64 million”, and its world rank is second or third depending on the source; wool production was 33.69 million kg in 2023-24, not 43–46 million kg, so imports of 92.2 million kg (an industry figure not checked against official trade data) are nearly three times domestic output. The state-wise decline compares the 2012 and 2019 censuses, and Andhra Pradesh has been dropped from it because the state was split in between.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Jute: India grows about 60% of the world's crop but barely exports it

What it means: India's jute goes almost entirely to a statutory domestic packaging market rather than to export.

What to do: Raising the MSP helps farmers directly, but it does not make jute compete on export terms.

Silk: output grew about 60% in a decade, yet India still imports raw silk

What it means: Total output grew about 60% over the decade, so volume alone does not explain the imports.

What to do: Closing the bivoltine supply gap, not adding raw tonnage, is what would reduce silk imports.

Only 11,229 tonnes of India's mulberry silk is the bivoltine grade industry needs

What it means: Most Indian cocoons are multivoltine, which does not substitute cleanly for the bivoltine grade industrial weaving needs.

What to do: Breeding for bivoltine cocoons is the fix the post points to, because raw tonnage was never the constraint.

India imports more raw wool than it produces, despite a large sheep flock

What it means: The wool gap is about grade rather than headcount, since India imports more raw wool by weight than it produces.

What to do: Inbreeding-driven quality decline, not sheep numbers, is what the post says needs fixing to cut wool imports.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Textiles should publish, with the same detail as the JPM Act reservation percentages, a comparable target and timeline for jute's export competitiveness — not just the domestic packaging mandate. A sector whose only stated success metric is a captive government order book has no way to show it is becoming exportable.
  • The Central Silk Board should publish state- and district-level bivoltine-versus-multivoltine cocoon shares, not just the national aggregate used in this piece, so extension programmes can target the specific districts where the grade gap is worst rather than applying a uniform national push.
  • The wool sector's inbreeding problem needs a named, funded breed-improvement programme with a published baseline — the diagnosis (inbreeding degrading carpet- and apparel-grade wool) is already in the public record; this piece did not find a correspondingly specific, funded remediation programme to cite against it.
  • Banana, pineapple-leaf and hemp fibre need a tracked national production statistic, the way jute, silk and wool already have one, before any claim about their commercial potential can be checked against reality rather than a research-scheme description.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Sources and caveats

India's ~60% share of world jute production and Bangladesh's ~70% share of world jute exports are from WorldAtlas and Wings2Fashion compilations of global jute-trade data; this piece did not independently verify these shares against a single UN Comtrade or FAO table. National raw jute production figures (97.6 lakh bales 2021-22 to 91.8 lakh bales more recently; a separately cited 83.08 lakh bales against a prior year's 92.52 lakh) are from Wikipedia's jute-production-in-India summary and indianagri.com's crop data page; the range across sources reflects differing crop-year vintages, not a single reconciled figure, and is presented as such. West Bengal's ~50% production share and its 89-of-120 composite-mill count (December 2025) are from Business Standard's reporting on the 2025 jute-industry input-cost crisis. The Jute Packaging Materials Act's 100%-foodgrain/20%-sugar-production reservation and the 22 April 2025 order (S.O. 1830(E)), valid to 30 June 2025, are from Drishti IAS, IAS Gyan and worldtradescanner.com's hosting of the order text; the ₹12,000-crore purchase, 4-lakh-worker and 40-lakh-farm-family figures are from the CCEA decision on jute reservation norms for 2023-24 (PIB 1984208). Raw jute MSP figures (₹5,335 to ₹5,650/quintal for 2025-26, ₹5,925/quintal for 2026-27, and the 147% cumulative rise since 2014-15) are from Tribune India's and agrijob.in's coverage of the relevant Cabinet approvals. India's raw silk production (42,033 tonnes 2025-26, +2.22% YoY; 26,000 tonnes in 2014; 31.9k MT 2017-18 to 38.9k MT 2023-24), the mulberry/Vanya split (32,250t / 9,783t, Vanya down 2.19%), bivoltine silk's rise to 11,229 tonnes (+10.52%), mulberry area (~269,670-276,231 ha, both figures cited as they appeared in different sources), Karnataka's lead position, and sericulture employment (9.76 million people, 52,500+ villages, FY25) are from Fibre2Fashion, Vision IAS, Tribune India and IBEF/inserco.org coverage of Central Silk Board statistics; this piece did not fetch a Central Silk Board primary release directly. Raw silk import figures (>$150 million/year, ~27.8 lakh kg in one cited year, sourcing from Vietnam ~$96m and China ~$59m) are from a Substack analysis (readon.substack.com) citing trade data; this piece flags that as a secondary source and did not independently confirm the exact import year against DGCI&S data. India's sheep population (74.26 million, 20th Livestock Census 2019), wool production (33.69 million kg in 2023-24, BAHS 2024), raw wool imports (92.2 million kg in 2023-24, ~18% from Australia, not checked against DGCI&S), the state-wise sheep-population decline between the 2012 and 2019 censuses (Himachal Pradesh, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Jammu & Kashmir), the land-use/climate pressure on pastoralists, and the inbreeding-driven quality degradation of carpet- and apparel-grade wool are from Drishti IAS's wool-sector explainer, Down To Earth's "Shear Desperation" reporting, and IBEF's wool-and-woollen export statistics. India's status as the world's largest banana producer (and therefore banana-fibre feedstock base), the Ministry of Textiles' R&D scheme covering banana, Himalayan nettle, bamboo and hemp, and Northeast India's positioning for allied natural fibres are from Fibre2Fashion, Springer Nature and Arunachal Times coverage of the sector; the $272.51-million 2024 sustainable-fashion market figure and its $1.6-billion-by-2033 projection are from an industry market-sizing report and should be read as a market-research estimate, not an official statistic. This piece did not find comparable national production-volume data for banana, pineapple-leaf or hemp fibre specifically, which is disclosed above as a finding in itself.
