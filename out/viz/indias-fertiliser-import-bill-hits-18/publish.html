Fertiliser imports are set to surge 76% in a single fiscal year to a record $18 billion. A Parliamentary Standing Committee report, tabled the same month, explains the mechanism in one line: 90% of urea's cost is natural gas, and India sources only a quarter of that gas domestically. This is the fertiliser side of a story an earlier piece on this blog already priced from the gas side.

India's fertiliser import bill: set for a record $18 billion, up 76% in a year

Top line

  • $18bn projected fertiliser import bill this fiscal year, a record and a 76% jump on the year before.
  • 90% of urea's production cost is natural gas, and only 26% of that gas is sourced domestically.
Updated 7 November 2026. The $18 billion below was a January 2026 projection. The full-year outcome, as reported in May 2026: India imported 28.2 million tonnes of finished fertilisers worth over $14.5 billion in 2025-26 (urea 11.2 million tonnes, $5.2 billion). Counting the inputs as well (26 million tonnes of LNG worth $13.3 billion, phosphoric acid $2.7 billion and rock phosphate $1.9 billion), the fertiliser-related import bill was $27.2 billion, second only to 2022-23’s $33.4 billion (Indian Express, via Vision IAS summary, 25 May 2026). On gas: the regulated price paid to ONGC and Oil India is capped, and the cap was raised from $6.75 to $7.00 per MMBtu from April 2026 (Free Press Journal); with crude well above $85 since, the 10%-of-crude formula now sits far above that cap.

What to do: What to do: back the committee's call for long-term supply agreements and joint ventures for phosphate and potash.

Urea's cost is 90% natural gas, and 74% of that gas is not domestic

What it means: Natural gas makes up 90% of urea's production cost, and only 26% of that gas is sourced domestically.

What to do: What to do: rework India's gas procurement mechanism to secure steady supply at competitive prices, as the committee recommends.

27 of 33 operating urea plants are more than 25 years old

What it means: The committee says the sector needs a technology transition within five years.

What to do: What to do: set up the dedicated task force the committee proposes to upgrade, modernise and revive these plants.

What would fix this

  • The Department of Fertilizers should publish monthly fertiliser imports by product — urea, DAP, MOP — in quantity and value, with source country, so that a headline like the $18 billion, 76% rise can be checked against an official release rather than officials quoted in press reports.
  • The Department of Fertilizers should publish an action-taken statement on each of the Standing Committee’s December 2025 recommendations, stating accepted, budgeted or rejected, so that whether the committee’s six fixes have moved beyond a report can be checked.
  • The Department of Fertilizers should publish plant-wise data for the 33 operational urea plants: commissioning year, energy use per tonne, and the share of gas from domestic versus imported sources, so that the 27 ageing plants and the 26% domestic-gas figure can be tracked plant by plant.
  • The Ministry of Mines and the Rajasthan government should publish a dated exploration and auction schedule for potash blocks in the Nagaur-Ganganagar basin, so that the GSI’s roughly 20,000 million tonnes of probable and possible reserves have a checkable path from estimate to mine while India imports 100% of its potash.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish
What this article does not establish. Whether the $18 billion import figure and 76% year-on-year increase have been confirmed in an official government release beyond the officials quoted in January 2026 press reporting — this article could not independently locate a PIB or Ministry of Chemicals and Fertilisers statement with the same figure. Two sources referenced in the underlying research notebook — on Russia's position as a fertiliser supplier to India, and on China's specialty-fertiliser export curbs — could not be retrieved by this article (access blocked) and are not used here; readers researching supplier-country risk should treat that as an open gap, not a settled picture. Whether any of the Standing Committee's six recommendations have been formally accepted or budgeted by the government is not addressed here — a committee report is a recommendation, not an implemented policy.
