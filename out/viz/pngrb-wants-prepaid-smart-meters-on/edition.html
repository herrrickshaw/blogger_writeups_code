An industry committee set up by PNGRB has recommended that every new piped-gas connection in India be fitted with a prepaid, remotely-shut-off smart meter within six months of a rule change, and that every existing meter be swapped over on a four-phase schedule running to 2030. Read in full, the committee's April 2026 report is candid about why: a 9% average default rate on conventional postpaid gas bills is costing city-gas companies money, and prepaid smart metering fixes that for them, not primarily for the consumer. This piece reports what the committee's report actually recommends, what it costs, and who its own text says should pay for it.

Top line

  • 9% average default rate on conventional postpaid PNG bills, which the committee says makes city-gas companies the main beneficiaries of prepaid meters.
  • 5–9× the price of a smart meter against a conventional one, per CGD entities' own submissions; the committee wants gas companies to bear it.
  • 2030 deadline for every domestic PNG meter to be smart, though nothing is binding until PNGRB amends its regulations.
Updated 7 November 2026. PNGRB has since put the committee's report on its own website, dated 17 April 2026 (Business Standard reported it on 20 April), so this piece's earlier description of it as a July 2026 report has been corrected. On 6 September 2026 PNGRB signed a memorandum of understanding with Energy Efficiency Services Ltd (EESL) under which EESL is to aggregate demand, run price discovery and coordinate procurement of smart domestic PNG meters with CGD companies, while PNGRB provides standards, certification and inspection support. No notified amendment to the T4S or CGD Authorisation Regulations mandating smart meters was found as of this update, so the deadlines below remain recommendations.

Defaults, not accuracy, drive the case for prepaid smart meters

What it means: Gas companies gain most from prepaid meters, because they remove the risk of unpaid bills.

What to do: Check whether PNGRB's own figures on defaults hold up before accepting the case for prepaid billing.

Smart meters cost far more upfront than a conventional meter

What it means: Smart meters carry much higher upfront prices, and the committee would have gas companies pay them.

What to do: Watch whether the final cost allocation keeps these meter costs off consumers' bills.

The committee finds no manufacturing bottleneck for the rollout

What it means: On the committee's own arithmetic, factories can supply new connections without a shortage.

What to do: Check whether capacity still holds once the much larger replacement volumes are counted.

What would fix this

  • PNGRB should publish the committee's smart-metering report on its website and open it to public comment with a fixed closing date before amending the T4S Regulations, so that consumers, not only the CGD companies represented on the committee, can respond before any mandate takes effect.
  • PNGRB should require every CGD entity to report audited annual figures for postpaid default rates, outstanding dues and recovery costs, by geographical area, so that the self-reported 9 per cent default rate and ₹8,500 average outstanding behind the case for prepaid meters can be verified.
  • PNGRB should publish a cost–benefit analysis alongside any amendment, comparing smart-meter capex and monthly charges with avoided default, recovery and billing costs at actual default rates, so that whether the lifetime saving holds in low-default areas — a question this post could not model — is answered before rollout.
  • PNGRB should require CGD entities to report each year the number of smart meters installed against each phase target and any meter-related charge or deposit levied on consumers, so that the committee's recommendation that consumers not bear the cost can be checked in practice.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Sources and caveats

This piece is based on a direct, complete reading of PNGRB's "Report on Roadmap for Implementation of Smart Domestic PNG (DPNG) Metering Across India," supplied to this piece as a file rather than found via search; every figure above traces to a specific page, table or recommendation in that report, which this piece has quoted or closely paraphrased. This piece could not independently verify the manufacturer pricing quotes, the CGD entities' self-reported default-rate data, or the manufacturing-capacity figures against any third-party source — all of it is exactly what the report itself states industry participants told the committee, not independently audited data. The report anonymises its three responding manufacturers as "Manufacturer A/B/C" and does not name them; this piece does the same. Because the mandate described here is a committee recommendation rather than a notified regulation, a reader who needs to know the current legal status of smart PNG metering requirements should check PNGRB's T4S Regulations, 2008 and any subsequent amendment directly, rather than treat this piece's account of the committee's recommendations as describing current law.
