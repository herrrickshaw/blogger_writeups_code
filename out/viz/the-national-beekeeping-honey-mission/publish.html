India became the world's second-largest honey exporter in 2024, up from ninth place four years earlier, on the back of a ₹500 crore Central Sector Scheme. The same four years contain a fact the scheme's own success story rarely mentions: a 2020 investigation by the Centre for Science and Environment, an independent non-profit, found ten of thirteen major honey brands sold in Indian shops failed the internationally accepted purity test for the exact adulteration those brands were accused of — and that test is still not required for honey sold in India; since August 2020 it has been mandatory only for honey exported to the United States.

Top line

  • 83% rise in natural honey export value from FY2020-21 to FY2023-24, to $177.55 million.
  • 1.6% of the colonies the climate could support are registered: 1.93 million, against an estimated 120 million.
  • 6.5 kg of honey per Apis cerana colony a year, against 25 kg for Apis mellifera.
Updated 7 November 2026. The export chart below stops at FY2023-24 ($177.55 million). Later figures, from secondary summaries of DGCIS data rather than an APEDA release this blog has read, put natural-honey exports at about $206.5 million (about 1.01 lakh tonnes) in FY2024-25 and about $208 million in FY2025-26, with the United States taking about 76% of the FY2025-26 value (Civilsdaily). The minimum export price for natural honey was cut from $2,000 to $1,400 a tonne in August 2025 and has since been extended, reportedly to 31 December 2026 (India Briefing, The Tribune). The one-market dependence matters for the adulteration argument below: the US is also the only market where the NMR test is mandatory.
Updated 27 October 2026. Three corrections. The 2020 investigation was by the Centre for Science and Environment, an independent non-profit, not a “government-linked” body. India’s 2020 honey standard did not check only C3 and C4 markers: it also had a rice-syrup marker (SMR) and foreign oligosaccharides, and CSE reported that the failing samples passed the C3, C4 and SMR tests. And the NMR test is not required for exports in general: the Export Inspection Council made it mandatory from 1 August 2020 only for honey exported to the United States, and FSSAI said in December 2020 that it is not required domestically.

Honey export value rose 83% in three years, to $177.55 million

What it means: The export figures are the scheme's main success measure, and they have grown strongly.

What to do: Report domestic retail honey testing results alongside export results, since the scheme's metrics currently track exports only.

Registered colonies are about 1.6% of what the climate could support

What it means: Registered colonies are a small share of the potential the post cites.

What to do: Set colony-growth targets for new beekeepers, tracked against the registered count.

The scheme leans toward Apis mellifera, which yielded nearly four times the honey

What it means: Apis mellifera also showed far lower colony absconding during the dearth period, which the study reports as 0% against 45%.

What to do: Weigh the honey yield gain against the temperament tradeoff when choosing bee species for subsidies.

Pests and disease, and unregulated markets, were the main constraints for beekeepers

What it means: The survey shows that disease and market prices limit beekeepers more than migration costs do.

What to do: Regulate pesticide use near apiaries and set up market access for beekeepers.

What would fix this

  • FSSAI should require NMR or equivalent molecular testing for honey sold in the domestic market, matching what is required for exports to the United States, so the purity standard protecting a shop buyer is the same one that protects the export brand, four years after CSE found 10 of 13 brands failing.
  • FSSAI should run and publish a national surveillance survey of branded retail honey using NMR testing, naming brands and results, so it is established whether adulteration has fallen since the 2020 investigation rather than inferred from stronger standards.
  • The National Bee Board should publish the number of samples tested each year at the 47 Mini Honey Testing Labs and 6 main labs, split between domestic retail and export lots, with failure rates, so labs sanctioned can be measured as tests performed.
  • The Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare should issue a standard inter-state transit document for migratory bee colonies that state border checkposts are directed to accept, so the 36.54 per cent of surveyed Mansa beekeepers stopped at borders can move hives without ad hoc checks.
  • The National Bee Board should publish how the ₹370 crore carried forward for FY2023–24 to FY2025–26 was spent, by Mini Mission and state, so the extension's outcomes can be compared with the infrastructure sanctioned in the first phase.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. Whether NBHM's 47 Mini Honey Testing Labs and 6 world-class labs are actually being used to NMR-test or equivalently screen domestic retail honey, as opposed to primarily supporting export consignments, could not be confirmed from the sourcing available — PIB's own progress reporting tracks labs sanctioned, not tests performed on domestically sold product. No post-2020 follow-up investigation equivalent in scope to CSE's original test was found in this piece's sourcing, so whether adulteration rates in major domestic brands have actually improved since 2020 is not established here, only that the regulatory standard (FSSAI 2.8.3, including the rice-syrup marker) was strengthened in response. The National Bee Board's own Operational Guidelines document (nbb.gov.in) returned a 403 error on every access attempt in this piece's research, so guideline-level procedural detail relies on secondary summaries rather than the primary document. The Apis mellifera/Apis cerana comparative figures come from a single Punjab-conditions study and may not generalise to other agro-climatic zones where Apis cerana is traditionally preferred, particularly hill regions. "1.2 million tonnes" and "120 million colonies" are stated potential-capacity estimates from academic literature, not government targets NBHM has committed to reaching. The Mobile Honey Processing Van detail and the 50g/700g/2kg per-capita consumption comparison come from a UPSC exam-prep aggregator (Vajiram & Ravi) summarising KVIC's own claims and unspecified consumption data respectively, not from a primary KVIC or FSSAI document this piece independently verified; both are presented as reported rather than confirmed figures. That same source's export-volume and export-value figures (from an unstated year) are not used here because they conflict with the PIB-sourced figures already cited in Section 2, which this piece treats as authoritative.
