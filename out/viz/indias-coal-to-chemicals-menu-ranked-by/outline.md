## What syngas can actually become

Gasifying coal — partial oxidation under heat and pressure, rather than combustion — produces synthesis gas (syngas): a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen that is chemistry's most versatile building block. From that single starting point, three broad families of products branch out:

India's coal is cheap, but its high ash content is exactly the handicap this piece says makes gasification harder here than it was for China. [Open (visible) Coal Mines](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Open_(visible)_Coal_Mines.jpg), Harshaddu, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Direct syngas products: hydrogen (separated out on its own), ammonia (syngas nitrogen-shifted and run through Haber-Bosch, then usually onward to urea and other fertilisers), synthetic natural gas (SNG, via methanation), and Fischer-Tropsch liquids (diesel, naphtha, waxes, built up directly from CO and H₂).

Methanol and its derivative tree: methanol itself (syngas run through a copper-catalyst synthesis loop) is the single largest coal-gasification product globally and the hub for a further family of derivatives — dimethyl ether (DME, a diesel/LPG substitute), acetic acid (via methanol carbonylation), formaldehyde, and, through the Methanol-to-Olefins (MTO) route, ethylene and propylene.

Oxygenates and other syngas-route chemicals: monoethylene glycol (MEG, feeding the polyester and PET chain), oxo-alcohols (via hydroformylation, feeding plasticiser and solvent chemistry), acetic anhydride, and ammonium nitrate (an ammonia derivative used as an industrial explosive and, in India specifically, the subject of a dedicated coal-gasification joint venture, discussed below).

## The finding that has to come before any ranking: it mostly doesn't pay for itself yet

Before sorting these products by relative viability, the honest starting point is that none of them clear a straightforward cost test today. The US-India Strategic Partnership Forum's own analysis of India's coal-gasification opportunity states it plainly: "at present prices of natural gas and imported products, the coal gasification route is not feasible" without some form of fiscal support or import protection. That is not a critics' assessment — it is the conclusion of a report making the broader case for India's coal-gasification push. The ranking that follows should be read in that light: as a comparison of which products come closest to viable, and which the government has already put real capital behind, not a claim that any of them beat imports on price alone right now.

## India's extra handicap: coal that's cheap but hard to gasify

India's coal is genuinely inexpensive by global standards — representative e-auction prices for non-coking coal have run around ₹1,800–1,900 a tonne for mid-grade coal in early-to-mid 2026, a fraction of the roughly ₹9,000–11,600 a tonne landed cost of imported sub-bituminous or thermal coal. But that headline price understates what a gasification plant actually needs to pay, because Indian coal carries a structural handicap that coal-to-chemicals programmes in China rarely had to solve at the same scale: ash content running up to 40%, against washing that "is not economic and feasible" below roughly 30% ash for some coal types even after processing, per government-directed washing compliance that itself remains low. The practical workarounds — fluidised-bed gasifiers designed to lift coal out of its own ash before combustion, and blending high-ash coal with lignite or petroleum coke — are described as viable, mature technology (Air Products, Siemens, and other established gasifier vendors are cited as capable), but they add real capital and operating cost on top of the raw coal price, a cost this piece could not find a single, India-specific rupees-per-tonne figure for.

## Reading the table: the government's money already agrees with the coal-intensity math

Ammonia/urea and synthetic natural gas are where India has actually committed capital — the Talcher Fertilisers revival, the Coal India–BHEL ammonium-nitrate joint venture (Bharat Coal Gasification & Chemicals Limited, building at Lakhanpur, Odisha), and the Coal India–GAIL SNG joint venture. These are also, not coincidentally, among the least coal-intensive products on the list, the ones with the clearest Chinese commercial precedent at scale (75% of China's ammonia/urea runs on coal), and in ammonia/urea's case, the ones the government has explicitly prioritised in its own gas-allocation policy — directing scarce natural gas to fertiliser producers ahead of methanol producers, which by extension makes the case for a coal-based methanol alternative stronger even as gas-based methanol capacity is left underserved.

BCGCL's Lakhanpur project, specifically, is the furthest along of any coal-to-chemicals project in the country, and its own disclosures are a useful real-world check on the coal-intensity math used elsewhere in this piece. The joint venture (Coal India 51%, BHEL 49%) signed its formal JVA on 28 February 2024; Mahanadi Coalfields Limited (MCL) signed a land-leasing agreement on 1 April 2026 for roughly 350 acres of coal-bearing land at Lakhanpur, Jharsuguda district, Odisha; and President Droupadi Murmu and Prime Minister Narendra Modi remotely laid the project's foundation stone on 20 June 2026. The plant is designed to produce 0.66 million tonnes a year (2,000 tonnes a day) of technical-grade ammonium nitrate using indigenously developed Pressurised Fluidised Bed Gasification (PFBG) technology, fed by 0.79 million tonnes a year of washed coal plus 1.19 million tonnes a year of reject coal from MCL's Ib Valley washery — a combined ~1.98 million tonnes of coal input against 0.66 million tonnes of product, or roughly 3.0 tonnes of coal per tonne of ammonium nitrate, the one figure in this piece drawn from an actual India-specific, high-ash-coal project rather than China-derived literature. All four Lump Sum Turnkey (LSTK) execution packages have been awarded — two to BHEL, two to Larsen & Toubro — and the project has received Single Window Clearance from Odisha's High-Level Clearance Authority plus a ₹1,350 crore financial incentive from the Ministry of Coal. Its estimated cost has also grown substantially since the 2024 JVA: from ₹11,782 crore at signing to a precise ₹25,016 crore in the PM's Office's own pre-event release, a more-than-doubling this piece treats as a documented cost escalation rather than an error in its earlier reporting. Target commissioning is September 2029. One detail meaningfully de-risks the project's demand side, and this piece did not have it in its first pass: Coal India itself has committed to buying the entire ammonium-nitrate output, for use as mining explosive in its own coal operations — a captive offtake arrangement, not a sale into the open commodity market this piece's viability framework otherwise assumes for every other product on this list.

Methanol sits in an interesting middle position: it has by far the largest import-dependence of anything on this list (over 90%, versus ammonia's roughly 13%), meaning the addressable substitution opportunity is large in absolute terms, and China's own 70%-coal-based methanol industry proves the chemistry works at scale. But it hasn't attracted the same scale of dedicated Indian coal-gasification capital that ammonia/urea and SNG have — JSL's 8 MTPA of syngas capacity is a byproduct of a steel-sector gasification project rather than a purpose-built coal-to-methanol plant, and this piece did not find an equivalent dedicated methanol joint venture in the National Coal Gasification Mission's published project list.

MEG and olefins were, in this piece's first pass, the two products with no funded Indian project behind them at all — and are the two most coal-intensive products on the list (3–3.5 and 6.2 tonnes of coal per tonne of output, respectively), the ones China's own industry describes as "less mature" or "facing economic challenges" even after years of buildout. That "no project" reading for MEG specifically no longer holds, as the next section describes — a private developer, not a PSU, turned out to be the one moving on it.

BCGCL is one of several named projects PIB has disclosed, and the fuller roster is worth listing because it shows the government's revealed priorities more completely than any one project can. Beyond BCGCL/Lakhanpur (ammonium nitrate, Odisha), the Coal India–GAIL synthetic-natural-gas joint venture — formally incorporated as Coal Gas India Limited (CGIL), CIL 51%/GAIL 49% — has a name and a number this piece did not have before: it is sited at Sonepur Bazari, in ECL's Raniganj coalfield area, Burdwan district, West Bengal, targeting 1.83 million standard cubic metres a day (80,000 Nm³/hour, 633.6 million Nm³ a year) of SNG at a cost of ₹13,052.81 crore (CIL's own equity share, ₹1,997.08 crore at a 70:30 debt-equity ratio), also targeted for FY29 commissioning at the time its JVA was signed on 5 August 2024.

## The scale the government is actually betting on

India's National Coal Gasification Mission targets 100 million tonnes of coal gasification capacity by 2030. As of the most recent reporting, roughly 22.6 million tonnes per annum is operational or under implementation — about 23% of the 2030 target — split across JSL's 8 MTPA, Talcher Fertilisers' 2.6 MTPA, and roughly 12 MTPA across eight projects approved under a dedicated ₹8,500 crore Scheme for Promotion of Coal/Lignite Gasification, sanctioned in January 2024 (7 of those 8 projects finalised, 3 with bhoomi pujan completed, as of April 2026). That ₹8,500 crore splits into three categories: ₹4,050 crore for government PSUs (up to three projects, each eligible for the lower of ₹1,350 crore or 15% of capex), ₹3,850 crore for private-sector and PSU projects (₹1,000 crore or 15% of capex per project), and ₹600 crore for demonstration and small-scale plants (₹100 crore or 15% of capex per project). This piece's earlier reporting flagged an unresolved discrepancy against a separately cited ₹37,500 crore figure; that discrepancy is now resolved by the government's own account: the ₹37,500 crore is a second, larger, separate Scheme for Promotion of Surface Coal/Lignite Gasification Projects, Cabinet-approved on 13 May 2026, targeting an additional 75 million tonnes of coal/lignite gasification (on top of the first scheme's contribution to the 100 MT goal), expected to mobilise ₹2.5–3.0 lakh crore in investment and create roughly 50,000 direct and indirect jobs across 25 projects. A PMO pre-event release ahead of the Lakhanpur foundation-stone ceremony puts the two schemes' combined cumulative outlay at up to ₹46,000 crore (₹8,500 crore plus ₹37,500 crore) — independent, primary-source confirmation that this piece's resolution of the earlier discrepancy is arithmetically correct, not just plausible. The government's own primary figure for the addressable import bill is considerably more precise than any industry estimate this piece found earlier: India's import bill for LNG, urea, ammonium nitrate, ammonia, coking coal, methanol, DME and related products stood at approximately ₹2.77 lakh crore in FY2025, per the Cabinet's own May 2026 press release — a figure this piece now treats as the authoritative baseline in place of the previously cited, weaker-sourced ₹60,000–90,000 crore industry estimate, which this piece no longer relies on.
