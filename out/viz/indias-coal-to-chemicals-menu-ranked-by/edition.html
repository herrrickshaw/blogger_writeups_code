Coal gasification can, in principle, make almost anything a refinery or gas plant can: ammonia and urea, methanol and its whole derivative family (DME, acetic acid, formaldehyde), monoethylene glycol, olefins, synthetic natural gas, even diesel. What it can't yet do, on the industry's own admission, is make most of that cheaper than simply importing it. This piece maps the full product tree coal gasification can reach, then sorts it — using India's actual import dependence, current global prices, and each product's coal intensity — by which ones come closest to paying for themselves at today's coal price, and which ones are, for now, a policy bet rather than an economic one.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Coal should commission and publish an impact assessment of the ₹8,500 crore coal-gasification incentive scheme before disbursing under the ₹37,500 crore scheme, since it told the Lok Sabha in March 2025 that no such assessment had been conducted.
  • The Ministry of Coal should publish a quarterly tracker for every National Coal Gasification Mission project, listing sanctioned cost, latest cost, land acquired, contracts awarded and target commissioning date, so that slippage such as BCGCL’s cost rising from ₹11,782 crore to ₹25,016 crore, or CGIL’s silence, is visible.
  • The Ministry of Coal should publish design coal input and product output for every incentivised gasification project, so that India-specific, high-ash coal intensities replace the China-derived ratios this post had to rely on, with BCGCL’s roughly 3.0 tonnes per tonne currently the only Indian figure.
  • The Ministry of Coal should publish a reconciled list of incentivised SNG projects stating whether the Niljai Ext. OC (Wani Area) award and the BPCL project at Majri are one project or two, so that the 100-million-tonne target is not counted twice.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

Checking this project's actual progress against BCGCL's, specifically, is revealing. CGIL/Sonepur Bazari received the same ₹1,350 crore Category-I financial incentive as BCGCL, in the same 12 December 2024 batch of Letters of Award. But that is where the visible progress converges: as of the most recent public disclosures this piece could find, ECL had acquired roughly 190 of the 250 acres of tenancy land the project needs (about 76%), a technology-licensor selection process was still open as of an October 2024 bid deadline, and the detailed feasibility report — also assigned to PDIL, the same consultant used for BCGCL — was described as still "in progress" at JVA signing. This piece found no foundation-stone ceremony, no confirmed LSTK or EPC contract awards, and no updated project cost or commissioning date for CGIL comparable to what BCGCL received in April and June 2026. That matters because BCGCL's own commissioning target has itself already slipped from the "FY29" both projects shared in 2024 to the more specific "September 2029" cited in the government's own June 2026 release — a schedule that has moved backward even for the more visibly advanced of the two projects. Absent an equivalent public update, this piece cannot establish whether CGIL is still tracking its original FY29 target, has slipped further, or has simply not been the subject of a recent government announcement; it treats the absence of an update as itself informative, not as evidence either way. A second, separate ammonium-nitrate initiative, named "Utkarsh," is nominally under way at Western Coalfields Limited (WCL) — and checking its progress turned up a genuinely different commercial structure and a materially earlier stage than either BCGCL or CGIL. Utkarsh is not a PSU-to-PSU joint venture at all: WCL has allocated roughly 350 acres at its Juna Kunada OC Mine, Majri Area, Chandrapur district, Maharashtra, on a lease-rent basis, and is seeking a private developer to build, own and operate the plant (a BOO structure) under a 2022 Expression of Interest, converting raw coal into "ammonium nitrate-melt." The only capacity figure this piece found for it — 0.66 MTPA, matching BCGCL's — comes from a single, brief PIB mention with no accompanying cost, contractor, or land-possession update since. Separately, and confusingly sharing the same Majri Area, Coal India and Bharat Petroleum Corporation Limited signed a non-binding memorandum of understanding on 2 December 2024 for a different product entirely — a ₹12,214 crore, 633.6 million Nm³/year synthetic natural gas project, described in trade coverage as CIL's third gasification partnership (after BHEL and GAIL) and, as of the same month, only as far along as an in-principle Pre-Feasibility Report approval from the CIL Board — a stage behind even CGIL's signed JVA. A near-identically costed SNG project (₹12,214.86 crore, also 1.83 MMSCMD) received a ₹1,350 crore Category-I incentive in the same December 2024 LOA batch as BCGCL and CGIL, described there as being at "Niljai Ext. OC, Wani Area" rather than Majri; this piece cannot establish with confidence whether that is the same BPCL project under a different area name or a genuinely separate one, and flags the inconsistency rather than resolving it. Taken together, ammonium nitrate is still the product with the most named Indian projects behind it, but Utkarsh specifically is the least institutionally advanced of the three ammonium-nitrate/SNG projects this piece has now checked — a private-developer tender with allocated land and one cited capacity figure, rather than an incorporated joint venture with a signed JVA, an awarded LOA, or any confirmed capital cost. A prospective Coal India–SAIL collaboration at Durgapur, West Bengal, targeting syngas as a DRI-grade reducing gas for steelmaking, is the earliest-stage of every named project this piece has now checked: it has not been incorporated as a joint venture at all (unlike BCGCL and CGIL, both named legal entities), carries no disclosed investment figure, no disclosed CIL equity stake, and independent trade analysis describes it as remaining "at an early feasibility and planning stage" without formal project sanction. It nonetheless matches an item on the Coal Minister's own product list cited earlier in this piece, and it is not the only coal-derived-syngas-for-steel initiative in motion: on 12 August 2026 — only weeks before this update — Coal India signed a separate, also non-binding memorandum of understanding with ArcelorMittal Nippon Steel (AMNS) to explore a coal-gasification-with-carbon-capture facility adjacent to AMNS's Paradip pellet plant in Odisha, aimed at supplying syngas for steelmaking there too. That MoU's own text commits only to preparing a Preliminary Feasibility Report and discloses no capacity, cost, coal-sourcing plan, technology choice, timeline, or which CIL subsidiary would execute it — meaning India currently has two separate, uncosted, unincorporated syngas-for-steel explorations (Durgapur and Paradip) running in parallel, neither of which has reached the JVA stage the ammonium-nitrate and SNG projects passed years ago. NLC India's lignite-to-methanol project (syngas, diesel, and green hydrogen alongside methanol, from lignite rather than coal specifically) is the one project this piece found that does target methanol directly, which corrects this piece's earlier framing that no dedicated Indian methanol project existed. Checking further on the ethanol demonstration project first identified via PIB's December 2024 Letters-of-Award release turned up something considerably larger: New Era Cleantech Solutions, the private company behind that ₹667 crore, 18 KTPA coal-to-ethanol demonstration unit (capturing roughly 20,144 tonnes of CO₂ a year via CCUS), is also building what it describes as India's first greenfield Integrated Coal Gasification and Carbon Capture Complex at Bhadravati, in the same Chandrapur district. That complex is a different order of scale entirely: roughly 1,650 acres, a total investment of ₹20,000 crore across two phases, environmental clearance already obtained, a captive coal mine already allocated, and a design throughput exceeding 5 million tonnes of coal a year once fully operational — larger than any single PSU-led project profiled in this piece. Phase 1 targets ammonia, ammonium nitrate, and monoethylene glycol (MEG); Phase 2 adds DME, urea, sustainable aviation fuel, and ethanol. Maharashtra Chief Minister Devendra Fadnavis performed the Bhoomi Pujan (ground-breaking) ceremony on 15 March 2026, attended by Union Coal Minister G. Kishan Reddy — three months before BCGCL's own foundation-stone ceremony, making New Era Cleantech's complex the earliest of any project in this piece to reach visible on-ground construction. A separate PIB-disclosed Category-II incentive of ₹1,000 crore, against a ₹6,976 crore project cost, for New Era Cleantech ammonium-nitrate and hydrogen capacity (0.33 MMTPA and 0.1 MMTPA respectively) at the same Bhadravati site is, on the balance of the evidence — same company, same location — almost certainly a financing component of this same larger complex rather than an unrelated fourth project, though this piece did not find a single source explicitly confirming that the ethanol demonstration unit, the ammonium-nitrate/hydrogen award, and the ₹20,000 crore complex are formally one integrated project rather than co-located separate ones. Whichever way that resolves, this correction matters for the sorting table above: MEG is no longer a product with no funded Indian project — New Era Cleantech's Phase 1 targets it directly, alongside ammonia and ammonium nitrate, making this the first private-sector entrant on this piece's list to move faster on the ground than any of the PSU joint ventures it has been comparing it against. One clarification worth making explicit, to avoid a coincidence reading as a connection: New Era Cleantech's own ₹20,000 crore is that one company's total capital outlay across two phases of one complex, and is entirely separate from the similarly-sized, unrelated national CCUS Mission — a five-year, ₹20,000 crore Union Budget 2026-27 outlay covering CCUS technology deployment across Power, Steel, Cement, Refineries, and Chemicals nationally, reportedly nodal to the Ministry of Power rather than Ministry of Coal. This piece found no evidence that New Era Cleantech's project has received or applied for funding under that national scheme specifically; its CCUS component to date has been funded through the Ministry of Coal's own, separate ₹8,500 crore coal-gasification incentive scheme (the ₹100 crore ethanol-unit and ₹1,000 crore ammonium-nitrate/hydrogen awards discussed above). Readers wanting the fuller picture on the national CCUS Mission — including a striking contrast with India's own 2022 climate strategy document, which assessed CCUS as "not matured" and fit only for demonstration projects — can find it in this blog's separate piece on India's Net Zero scenarios. Separately from all of the above surface-gasification projects, the Ministry of Coal is also running a distinct, earlier-stage technology track: India's first Underground Coal Gasification (UCG) pilot, at the Kasta coal block in Jamtara district, Jharkhand, run by Eastern Coalfields Limited with CMPDI Ranchi and Canada's Ergo Exergy Technologies, gasifying coal in-situ rather than in a surface plant — a two-year, two-phase R&D project that began with borehole/feasibility work in June 2024 and has not, as far as this piece could establish, reached a commercial-scale product decision.

What this piece does not establish. The coal-intensity ratios (tonnes of coal per tonne of product) used throughout this piece are drawn from general and China-specific coal-to-chemicals technoeconomic literature, not from India-specific plant data using India's actual high-ash coal — the real Indian ratios, once beneficiation and blending losses are accounted for, are likely higher than the ranges cited here, and this piece could not find a published India-specific figure to correct for that. This piece does not establish a verified, single production-cost-per-tonne figure for any product via India's coal-gasification route; the USISPF finding that the route is "not feasible" at present prices is one industry report's conclusion, not an independent cost audit this piece reproduced calculation-by-calculation. The ₹8,500 crore and ₹37,500 crore figures are now confirmed, via PIB, to be two separate, sequential schemes rather than one figure reported inconsistently — the earlier version of this piece flagged this as an unresolved discrepancy, which PIB's own record has since resolved. This piece does not independently verify BCGCL's escalation from ₹11,782 crore to over ₹25,000 crore beyond noting that both figures come from the project's own official disclosures at different dates; it does not speculate on the cause of the increase. The polyethylene price cited for olefins is a global reference price, not an India-specific CIF import figure, because this piece could not locate the latter with confidence. This piece does not establish that New Era Cleantech's ethanol demonstration unit, its ammonium-nitrate/hydrogen incentive award, and its ₹20,000 crore Bhadravati complex are formally one integrated project rather than three co-located but distinct developments — that connection is this piece's own inference from shared company and location, not a directly confirmed fact. Sources and caveats

The syngas product tree (direct products: hydrogen, ammonia, SNG, Fischer-Tropsch liquids; methanol-derivative tree: DME, acetic acid, formaldehyde, MTO olefins; oxygenates: MEG, oxo-alcohols, acetic anhydride) is drawn from the US Department of Energy's NETL Gasifipedia reference material and the Global Energy Monitor's Coal-to-Chemicals wiki — graded strong, being primary technical-reference sources. The core finding that coal gasification "is not feasible" at present natural-gas and import prices without fiscal support is drawn directly from the US-India Strategic Partnership Forum's own published report on coal gasification opportunities in India — graded strong as a primary-source quote, though it represents one industry body's conclusion rather than an independently reproduced cost model. India's coal-price figures (₹1,800–1,900/tonne e-auction non-coking coal, ₹9,000–11,600/tonne landed import coal) are drawn from CEIC's representative-price series and trade-press reporting on import costs — graded strong for the e-auction figures (a government-linked data series), moderate for the import figures. India's high-ash coal challenge (up to 40% ash, sub-30% ash not economically achievable via washing for some coal types, fluidised-bed gasifiers and blending as workarounds) is drawn from the USISPF report and a Business Standard analysis of the coal-gasification programme's technical challenges — graded strong, being consistent across an industry report and independent trade-press reporting. The product-level import-dependence and price figures (methanol >90% imported at ~$298/t; ammonia ~13% imported at ~$428/t; urea's 5–8 Mt/year import volume against 35–38 Mt consumption and spot-price spikes toward $700/t; MEG at ~$550–665/t with zero import duty through June 2026; acetic acid at ~$348/t) are drawn from a mix of commodity-price aggregators (Intratec, ChemAnalyst, IMARC-style pricing reports), trade-policy reporting (Global Agriculture, Reclimatize), and Argus Media's fertiliser-subsidy coverage — graded moderate, since aggregator-sourced commodity prices vary by methodology and this piece did not cross-verify each figure against a second independent source.
