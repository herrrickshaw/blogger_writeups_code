India's official farm-household income survey finds that wages, not crop production, are the largest source of a farm household's income — and it reaches that finding on a population it defines to exclude the rural households most dependent on wages in the first place.

India's farm income survey: wages top source, wage-dependent households excluded

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 49% of the average agricultural household's income is wages on the fuller expense basis, ahead of crop production's 37%.
  • ₹1,005 a month more in wages than crop net receipts on that same basis.
Updated 7 November 2026. A new round is now in the field: the National Statistical Office is running a fresh Situation Assessment Survey of agricultural households alongside the All India Debt and Investment Survey, with fieldwork from July 2026 to June 2027 (Informist; GKToday, search summaries). Until it reports, 2018-19 remains the latest official figure.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

What it means: On the imputed-cost basis, wages make up 49% of household income and crop production 37%.

What to do: Treat wage income as the main income line when sizing any farm-income policy built on this survey.

Wages beat crop income on both expense bases, by ₹265 to ₹1,005 a month

What it means: Wages stay ahead of crop income on either basis, and the gap is wider once family labour is costed.

What to do: Report the wage lead on the imputed-cost basis, since that is the fuller measure of what the household earns.

Imputing family labour cost cuts animal income more than crop income

What it means: Animal income falls by ₹1,141 a month once family labour is costed, against ₹740 for crop production.

What to do: Check any livestock income figure against the imputed-cost basis before using it in policy.

What would fix this

  • MoSPI should announce a fieldwork and publication date for the next Situation Assessment Survey of agricultural households, since the 2018-19 round remains, per the Agriculture Ministry's 2024 Lok Sabha reply, the latest official measure of farm household income.
  • The NSO should add a module covering the households the survey excludes — entirely agricultural-labour, coastal fishing, rural artisan and agricultural-services households — and publish their income by source, so rural income policy is not built only on a population that already skews toward cultivation.
  • The NSO should publish the 70th-round (2012-13) income table on both the paid-out and imputed-cost expense bases alongside the 77th round's, so a growth rate between the two rounds can be computed without guessing which basis the ₹6,426 figure uses.
  • The NSO should split wage income in its income-by-source table into farm and non-farm wages, so the ₹4,063 monthly wage figure can show how much agricultural households actually earn from farm labour versus other employment.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish
Don't compute a trend from this. The 2024 Lok Sabha reply also tables the 70th round (2012-13) total at ₹6,426/month against the 77th round's ₹10,218 — but which of the two expense bases the ₹6,426 figure uses was not established for this piece, so no growth rate between the two years is calculated here. Treat 2018-19 as a single dated snapshot, not one point on an established trend line.
