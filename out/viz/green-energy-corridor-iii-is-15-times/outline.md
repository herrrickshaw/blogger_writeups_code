On 30 September 2026 the Union Cabinet approved the third phase of the Green Energy Corridor, a ₹1,86,405 crore scheme to build transmission lines and substations inside states, plus 50 GWh of battery storage, so that up to 135 GW of solar and wind power can reach consumers by 2032-33. It is the largest transmission scheme the Centre has backed for renewable energy, and the first intra-state phase to pay for batteries.

Its scale is the story, and so is the record behind it. The first intra-state phase, approved in 2015-16 for ₹10,142 crore, has still not been finished in four of its eight states. The second, approved in January 2022 for ₹12,031 crore and due in 2025-26, is still being built. Together the two were meant to connect 44 GW of renewable capacity; by March 2026, 26 GW had been connected. Phase III gives the same state utilities about fifteen times as much money to spend, in seven years.

## Who pays

The Centre’s share is ₹54,082 crore, or 29% of the outlay. As reported, that is a 33% grant on lines and substations, the same rate as Phase II, and much less, 12%, on storage. Lines that are new will be put out to tariff-based competitive bidding, so private transmission companies can build, own and run them; upgrades of existing networks will be done by state utilities at cost plus a regulated return. Either way, what the grant does not cover is recovered through intra-state transmission charges, which end up in electricity tariffs.

One line in that split stands out. If the reported figures are right, ₹3,050 crore of the new scheme’s central money goes to liabilities from Phases I and II, which is about 38% of the ₹8,027 crore the Centre committed to those two phases. Part of Phase III, in other words, pays for the earlier phases’ unfinished work.

## What the earlier phases ran into

The Ministry of Power reported in 2025 that Phase I had built 9,161 of its 9,700 circuit kilometres and charged 21,925 of its 22,600 MVA of substations by 31 March 2025, with Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu finished. Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh and Maharashtra were given extensions to June 2025 and have asked for more. The reasons given in Parliament were right-of-way disputes, court cases including the one over the Great Indian Bustard’s habitat in Rajasthan and Gujarat, forest clearances, and land for substations.

None of those problems gets smaller with a bigger budget. Reports of the Cabinet decision put Phase III at 51,126 circuit kilometres of lines and 2,28,903 MVA of substations, against 20,450 ckm and 50,100 MVA for the two earlier intra-state phases combined: two and a half times the line length and more than four times the substation capacity. Phase I built 9,161 ckm in about nine years. Phase III, if those figures are right, needs about 7,300 ckm a year from 2026-27 to 2032-33.

## Where the power is being lost now

The urgency is real. Ember estimated that 470 GWh of renewable power was curtailed on the inter-state grid in January to March 2026, nearly two thirds of it because lines were not ready. In Rajasthan, plants connected on temporary access have faced near-total shutdowns between 11 am and 2 pm, and ICRA estimated in May 2026 that a third of the 54.8 GW of recently commissioned renewable capacity was running on that temporary route.

Much of that loss is on the inter-state system, which Phase III does not cover; it is built by the Centre’s transmission planners, not by state utilities. Phase III deals with the other half: the lines inside each state that connect solar and wind parks tendered by the states themselves, and that carry power on from inter-state substations to towns and factories. Both halves have to arrive for a solar plant to run at noon.
