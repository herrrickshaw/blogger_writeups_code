Putting children’s cough syrup in a lollipop sounds like a kindness. For infants it is the wrong answer three times over: cough medicines are not meant for under-twos, hard sweets are a choking hazard, and medicine that tastes like candy invites overdoses. The research points instead to tablets 2 mm across, films that melt on the tongue and medicine given during breastfeeding. Indian companies already make several of these — but mostly for export, not for Indian children.

Top line

  • 85% of mothers in a UK study preferred a solid vitamin B12 dose during breastfeeding to a syringe.
  • 24 children in Madhya Pradesh died in 2025 in deaths linked to Coldrif, which contained diethylene glycol.
  • 530% rise in poison-centre calls about children swallowing melatonin, 2012 to 2021.
Updated 7 November 2026. The proposed red under-four warning on cough-syrup labels moved a step: in early October 2026 the Drugs Consultative Committee, which advises the drug regulator, agreed in principle to a prominent red warning and to barring infant and toddler images on packs. It still needs an amendment to the labelling rules, which this blog has not seen notified.

What it means: Uncoated mini-tablets were accepted better than syrup in a 2013 trial of 306 children, and newborns swallowed them better in 2015.

What to do: Prescribers and manufacturers should offer solid mini-tablets or films, tested for acceptability, as the first alternative to syrup for infants.

Cough-syrup labels for under-fours have moved from advice to binding rules

  1. December 2023Regulator asks for a not-for-under-four label on the chlorpheniramine and phenylephrine combination
  2. April 2025A notification makes the under-four warning binding
  3. October 2025Health Ministry advises that cough and cold medicines should not go to children under two
  4. 18 August 2026Ministry extends the under-four warning to every formulation with the two drugs
  5. Early October 2026Drugs Consultative Committee agrees in principle on red labels with no images of infants

What it means: India's warnings have been extended step by step, and the labelling rules had not been amended by early November.

What to do: Regulators should amend the labelling rules to carry the agreed red under-four warning and drop images of infants or toddlers.

Almost half of infant melatonin ingestions involved gummies

What it means: Melatonin was involved in about 11,000 emergency visits by infants and young children in 2019 to 2022, and 47% of those cases involved gummies.

What to do: Parents and carers should keep melatonin and any sweet-shaped medicine out of children's reach, and the same logic argues against medicated lollipops for babies.

What would fix this

  • The Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation should make the warning against cough and cold medicines for children under two binding, by notification and on labels, rather than leaving it as an advisory that, according to complaints reported in 2026, some doctors have ignored.
  • The Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission should publish monographs and quality standards for mini-tablets, orodispersible films and dispersible tablets for children, so that Indian manufacturers have a clear route to make them and state regulators a standard to test them against.
  • The Ministry of Health and Family Welfare should prefer dispersible and other solid child formulations in the National List of Essential Medicines and in state free-medicine schemes, following the WHO’s guidance, so that public procurement does for Indian children what donor procurement has done for HIV and TB treatment abroad — and gives Cipla, Macleods, Laurus, ZIM, Shilpa and others a domestic market for what they already make.
  • The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India and the CDSCO should jointly bar the sale of medicines or supplements for children in lollipop, gummy or sweet form without child-resistant packaging and a dose limit per pack, before such products become common in India.
  • The Indian Council of Medical Research should fund acceptability trials of mini-tablets and films in Indian infants, so that the German and UK results are tested where the syrup problem is largest.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Sources and caveats

The clinical studies are from their PubMed abstracts: Streisand et al., Anesthesia & Analgesia 1989 (fentanyl lozenge); Nuvvula et al., Oral Health & Preventive Dentistry 2020 (systematic review of liquorice lollipops); Veraza et al., Cureus 2026 (diclofenac lollipop); Spomer et al., Archives of Disease in Childhood 2012; Klingmann et al., Journal of Pediatrics 2013 and 2015; Klingmann et al., European Journal of Pharmaceutics and Biopharmaceutics 2020; Maier et al., PLOS ONE 2022; Goyanes et al., International Journal of Pharmaceutics 2019 (as summarised in later reviews); Galgon et al., European Journal of Pharmaceutics and Biopharmaceutics 2022 (JustMilk). The laboratory lollipop formulations are from papers indexed on ResearchGate, Academia and De Gruyter and were not read in full. The regulatory concern about the fentanyl lozenge is as summarised in secondary literature. The honey review is Oduwole et al., Cochrane 2018. The WHO guidance is “Development of paediatric medicines: points to consider in formulation” (Technical Report Series 970, Annex 5). Arpraziquantel’s EMA opinion (December 2023) and WHO prequalification (May 2024) are from the Pediatric Praziquantel Consortium and Merck KGaA. The melatonin figures are from the CDC’s Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (2022 and 2024). The choking statement is the American Academy of Pediatrics’ guidance; the 2013 Pediatrics finding on hard candy is as reported in the press. India’s advisory of 3 October 2025 is from All India Radio; the chlorpheniramine–phenylephrine warnings are from Medical Dialogues, ETV Bharat and the Health Ministry’s post of 22 August 2026; the proposed red label is from Business Standard and Medical Dialogues (September 2026); the Coldrif figures are from The Tribune, Chemistry World and All India Radio. The description of how diethylene glycol enters syrups is general knowledge about the contamination cases, not a finding about any one product. The company table is from: Fierce Pharma, DNDi and i-Base (Cipla pellets, 2015); DNDi, Unitaid and i-Base (Quadrimune); the Clinton Health Access Initiative (dolutegravir 10 mg pricing); TB Alliance, Unitaid and the Treatment Action Group (paediatric TB combinations); ThePrint, 2 May 2023 (Laurus film); ZIM Laboratories’ website and FY2024 annual report and press reports of its EU approvals; Medical Dialogues and Business Upturn (Shilpa Medicare); Nutritional Outlook and Tracxn (Aavishkar); and CDSCO’s list of drugs approved in 2010 (ondansetron film). Company claims, including “first”, are the companies’ own. This piece is not medical advice: ask a doctor before giving any medicine to a child.
