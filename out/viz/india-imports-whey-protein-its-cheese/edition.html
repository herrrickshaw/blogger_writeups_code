Every kilogram of cheese leaves nine of whey, yet the government’s model cheese project does not count it, while India imports most of its whey protein at prices that tripled in two years. A small plant’s whey is too little to dry profitably; drinks and whey cheese at the plant, shared hubs for clusters and on-site drying at large dairies would put it to use.

India's whey protein: mostly imported, while cheese makers pour whey away

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 9 kg of whey is left over for every kilogram of cheese made, and the model cheese plan does not count any of it.
  • 80-90% of India's supplement-grade whey protein is reported to be imported.
  • Rs 2,300-3,000 a kg landed price for imported whey protein concentrate in mid-2026, up from Rs 700-800 in 2024.
Updated 7 November 2026. The India–New Zealand free trade agreement, in force since 20 October 2026, gives no tariff cut on whey or other core dairy (Outlook Business; see this blog’s piece on the agreement). Secondary coverage reports one related concession: a quota for milk albumins at half the normal duty. High-protein whey concentrates of more than 80% protein are generally classified as milk albumin, so some WPC80 from New Zealand may enter at the lower rate within that quota; the treaty schedule was not read and the quota size was not found.

What it means: Whey appears only as a process step, so the plant's costs, revenue and effluent treatment are all left out.

What to do: Add a whey mass balance, a chosen outlet and its cost to every small cheese project plan.

Imported whey protein prices roughly tripled between 2024 and mid-2026

What it means: The same whey holds only 3 to 5 tonnes of protein a year at the model plant's output, too little to pay for a dryer alone.

What to do: Pool whey from small units into shared hubs, and test any whey investment case at 2024 prices too.

Every small whey product in the Kenyan study returned at least 49% a year

What it means: All four small whey products were profitable in the Kenyan study, though the study is a 2025 preprint not yet peer reviewed.

What to do: Small units should start with drinks and whey cheese, then food use, contracted cattle feed and biogas for what is left.

What would fix this

  • NIFTEM should add a whey section to its model cheese and paneer project reports, with the mass balance, an outlet, its equipment and its revenue or disposal cost, and correct the milk price and chiller size in the cheese report.
  • The food processing ministry should let PMFME subsidy cover whey equipment bought as part of a cheese or paneer project.
  • The Department of Animal Husbandry and Dairying should name whey hubs as eligible under AHIDF and publish annual cheese, paneer and chhena output with an estimate of whey generated and used.
  • The food processing ministry should add whey protein and lactose to the products eligible under the PLI scheme, alongside mozzarella.
  • State pollution control boards should enforce the dairy effluent limit with a grace period tied to a whey plan, so that disposal stops being free.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
