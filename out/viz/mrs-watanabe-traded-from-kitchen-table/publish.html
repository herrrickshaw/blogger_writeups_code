In the early 2000s, thousands of ordinary Japanese housewives — nicknamed “Mrs Watanabe” by currency desks who couldn't tell one from another — started trading foreign exchange from home computers between school runs and grocery shopping, and ended up moving the world's most liquid market. It wasn't a niche curiosity: retail Japanese FX flow briefly ran at multiples of what Japan's actual share of interbank trading implied it should. India isn't Japan, and this isn't a call to speculate, and it isn't a call to trade currency on margin either. But the same three conditions that let an unremarkable Tuesday-afternoon trade in Osaka become a global macro variable — low-friction access, a skill that layers onto work already being done at home, and a market finally cheap enough to enter at small scale — are lining up in India today, at exactly the moment women are still a minority of the country's investors. The opportunity this piece argues for is narrower and safer than what Mrs Watanabe actually did: not currency speculation, but ordinary equity and mutual-fund investing, at a moment when entering that market has never been cheaper or simpler for a first-time Indian investor to do directly. This piece lays out what actually happened with Mrs Watanabe, what the real numbers say about who's investing in India right now, and why the pricing environment specifically (not sentiment, not a slogan) has made now the moment for that door to open wider for Indian women.

Top line

  • 24.9% of India's registered individual investors were women in FY26, per NSE data.
  • 45% higher average lump-sum investments by women than by men, and SIP tickets 22% higher.
  • 234 million demat accounts by July 2026, with roughly 30 million new accounts opened each year.
Updated 7 November 2026. NSE’s April 2026 Market Pulse puts women at 24.9% of investors in FY2025–26 (24.2–24.5% in FY2025), with Maharashtra at 29% (28.4%) and Uttar Pradesh at 19% (18.5–18.7%); the text and table now carry these figures. Demat accounts reached about 234 million by July 2026. Bihar’s 15.7% and the chart remain FY2025 figures; no FY2026 Bihar figure from NSE itself was found.

What it means: Women were 24.9% of registered individual investors nationally in FY26, with Uttar Pradesh and Bihar well below that.

What to do: Close the entry gap in lagging states, starting with Uttar Pradesh and Bihar.

Once invested, women put in more per transaction than men

What it means: Women who invest hold larger tickets than men, so the gap lies in how many women enter the market, not in their conviction once in.

What to do: Make the first entry step easy for women, since that is where the gap sits.

Demat accounts reached about 234 million, with 30 million added each year

What it means: Zero-brokerage trades, SIPs starting at ₹100–500 and UPI-linked funding have lowered the cost of starting.

What to do: Use these low entry costs to bring more women into equity and mutual-fund investing, not leveraged currency trading.

What would fix this

  • NSE should publish women’s share of registered individual investors for every state in one dated table each financial year, so that state comparisons such as Bihar’s 15.7% against Maharashtra’s 28.4% rest on a single reporting date rather than figures from different years.
  • SEBI should direct NSDL and CDSL to publish demat accounts by gender and state every quarter, so that the gender gap across India’s more than 150 million accounts is tracked from depository records rather than inferred from one exchange’s investor register.
  • AMFI should publish, by gender and state, the number of mutual fund investors, their assets and average SIP and lump-sum ticket sizes every year, so that findings like women’s 22% higher SIP ticket come from an official industry release rather than press reports.
  • SEBI should publish the gender and state breakdown of participants in investor-awareness programmes it funds, so that it can be checked whether outreach reaches the northern states where women’s participation lags most, such as Uttar Pradesh at around 18.5%.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
