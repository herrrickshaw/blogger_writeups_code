~70%of health spending goes on outpatient care, which PM-JAY does not cover, per the Parliamentary committee

~Rs 15,300average PM-JAY admission paid since 2018, against Rs 34,064 average out-of-pocket per hospital stay in 2025

Rs 0.5–1.1 lakh cra year: this blog’s rough cost of outpatient cover for PM-JAY’s 55 crore eligible people

On 7 August the Rajya Sabha’s Standing Committee on Health presented a report on how affordable healthcare is in India. Its central finding is that about 30% of people have no structured financial protection against health costs, and its central proposal is to extend Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY, which pays for hospital admissions, to doctor visits, tests and medicines. It also wants family cover raised from Rs 5 lakh to Rs 10 lakh a year and government health spending put on a time-bound path to 2.5% of GDP.

This blog’s [piece on India’s two tracks of health cover](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/08/indias-two-tracks-of-health-cover-and.html) looked at how PM-JAY and private insurance have grown. This piece looks at the gap the committee is pointing to: where out-of-pocket money actually goes, why most of it falls outside PM-JAY, and roughly what covering outpatient care would cost.

The 30% figure echoes NITI Aayog’s 2021 estimate that at least 30% of the population, about 40 crore people, were a “missing middle” with no health insurance: mostly self-employed and informal workers too well-off for PM-JAY and outside employer schemes. Since then PM-JAY has been extended to everyone over 70 and to some groups of workers, so the share may now be lower; the committee did not publish a new estimate. The committee also found that outpatient care in private facilities costs about 5 times as much as in public ones.

India’s National Health Accounts for 2022-23, released in May 2026, put total health spending at Rs 8.81 lakh crore. Households paid 43.4% of it directly, about Rs 3.82 lakh crore, or about Rs 2,764 for every person in the country. That share had fallen steadily for a decade, but rose in 2022-23 from 39.4% as government spending fell after the pandemic years. Government health spending was 1.48% of GDP on the new GDP series.

Pharmacies alone took 21.19% of current health spending, about Rs 1.62 lakh crore, nearly all of it paid by households. The NSS survey of 2025 shows the same pattern from the household side: government insurance now covers 45.5% of rural and 31.8% of urban people, but the average out-of-pocket cost of a hospital stay was Rs 34,064 (median Rs 11,285), and a NITI Aayog-commissioned study found that 65% of PM-JAY beneficiaries still paid something themselves, mainly for medicines, tests and transport.

## Why PM-JAY misses most of it

PM-JAY has issued 48.51 crore Ayushman cards and paid for about 13.25 crore hospital admissions worth Rs 2.03 lakh crore since 2018, or about Rs 15,300 an admission and about Rs 25.4 thousand crore a year. But hospital stays are rare and doctor visits frequent. The committee puts the outpatient share of health spending at nearly 70%, and an NIPFP analysis of the 2014 NSS survey found that households spent more than twice as much on outpatient care as on hospital care over a year, because they need it far more often. A scheme that pays only for admissions cannot reach most of what households pay.

This is a rough, illustrative calculation, not a costing. It takes out-of-pocket spending per person from the 2022-23 health accounts (Rs 2,764), assumes 70% of it is outpatient care (about Rs 1,935 a person), and applies it to PM-JAY’s roughly 55 crore eligible people. Because poorer households spend less, the low case assumes they spend half the national average. The answer is Rs 0.5–1.1 lakh crore a year in 2022-23 prices, 2.1 to 4.2 times what PM-JAY pays for hospital care in an average year, and many times the Centre’s Rs 9,500 crore PM-JAY budget for 2026-27.

Set against the committee’s other target, though, it is affordable. Raising government health spending from 1.48% to 2.5% of GDP would have meant about Rs 2.66 lakh crore more a year at 2022-23 GDP, so outpatient cover for PM-JAY’s eligible population would take 20–40% of that increase. How it is delivered matters as much as the money. Outpatient claims are small and numerous, so paying for them through insurance claims can cost a lot to administer; providing free medicines and tests at public facilities such as Ayushman Arogya Mandirs reaches the same spending without a claim for each visit. The question of what drugs cost at the pharmacy counter, the subject of a separate committee report on drug pricing, bears on both routes.
