Ajvar — a roasted red pepper and eggplant relish that is practically a national dish across the Balkans — is not manufactured in India today. Every jar on an Indian shelf or import-grocery site arrived from North Macedonia, Serbia, Croatia, or Bulgaria. What makes that gap interesting is not the dish itself but the inputs: India already grows the two vegetables ajvar is made from at industrial scale, already runs a directly analogous roasted-and-ground pepper industry in Karnataka, and already has government-built processing infrastructure sitting underused. This piece maps what it would actually take to close that gap, and how big the opportunity realistically is.

Top line

  • 12.6–12.8 million tonnes of eggplant a year makes India the world's second-largest producer, after China.
  • €21.9 million is all Serbia sold India in 2025, against €403.3 million the other way.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

India already grows both vegetables ajvar needs at industrial scale

What it means: India produces roughly 12.6–12.8 million tonnes of eggplant a year from over 760,000 hectares.

What to do: Site a first plant near a leading eggplant state so that the supply chain stays short.

Serbia sends India very little back, so the import channel stays small

What it means: Serbia's exports to India were €21.9 million in 2025, so imported ajvar can only ever be a small trade.

What to do: Build for the domestic shelf rather than the specialty import trade, which caps volume at this scale.

Karnataka supplies a sizeable share of India's capsicum, one state among many

What it means: Capsicum is grown across many states, so no single region is needed to supply a plant.

What to do: Source capsicum from Karnataka's greenhouse belt in Kolar and Chikkaballapura.

What would fix this

  • CBIC, with the GST Council, should issue a clarification on the HSN sub-heading and GST rate for vinegar-preserved roasted-vegetable relishes under heading 2001, so a would-be manufacturer is not left choosing between the 5% and 12% rates that public sources currently give for the same kind of product.
  • DGCI&S should publish imports of prepared vegetable relishes at the 8-digit tariff level by country of origin, so the size of the imported ajvar-type market can be measured directly rather than bounded only by India's total imports from Serbia or North Macedonia.
  • The Ministry of Food Processing Industries should publish, for each of the 42 sanctioned Mega Food Parks, the number of processing-unit plots available and their lease terms, so an entrant can see where the shared infrastructure this piece relies on is actually open to new units.
  • NIFTEM should publish a processing handbook for roasted red pepper and eggplant relish under the PM-FME programme, as it has for Byadgi chilli, so small processors in pepper- and brinjal-growing districts have a documented process and food-safety baseline to start from.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. No evidence was found of any Indian manufacturer currently producing ajvar or a directly branded equivalent at commercial scale — this piece describes an opportunity, not an existing or announced venture. No India-specific retail price data for imported ajvar jars could be sourced (only US Amazon listings for brands like Kostana, Biljana, and Podravka were found; Indian specialty-grocery pricing for the same products was not located). The correct HSN classification and GST rate for a domestically produced ajvar-style relish is genuinely ambiguous from public sources consulted: vinegar-preserved vegetable preparations generally fall under HSN 2001, but search results returned conflicting GST rates (5 per cent cited in one source, 12 per cent in several others) likely reflecting sub-heading or formulation differences this piece could not resolve — any actual manufacturer would need to confirm classification and rate directly with a customs/GST professional before pricing a product. The Byadagi chilli industry comparison is offered as evidence of adjacent processing capability and export-quality precedent, not as evidence that any Byadagi processor is planning or capable of pivoting to ajvar specifically. The bilateral trade figures in Section 6 are total goods trade between India and Serbia/North Macedonia, not ajvar-specific or even food-specific import figures, and should be read only as an upper bound on how large the specialty-import channel this piece is discussing could possibly be.
