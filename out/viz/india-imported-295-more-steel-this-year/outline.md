India imported 3.49 million tonnes of finished steel in April to August 2026, 29.5% more than a year earlier, and stayed a net importer even though exports rose 34%. China was the largest source, at 31.8% of the total. This happened with a safeguard duty on flat steel in force: 12% in its first year, 11.5% since 21 April 2026, 11% from next April. Domestic steelmakers have asked for a duty above 20%. The steel ministry, according to a Reuters-based report of 7 October, is unlikely to tighten curbs, because domestic prices are already high.

Two corrections to the story as it circulates. The “seven-year high” in Chinese steel imports is a headline from 2024 and 2025, not from this year; no 2026 China tonnage was found for this piece. And the ministry’s regular open houses on “steel import issues” are not about the safeguard duty at all. They deal with the Steel Import Monitoring System, its simplified SARAL SIMS route and exemptions from quality control orders. The import rules that decide who can bring steel in are as important as the duty, and much less discussed.

Updated 9 November 2026. Newer official data: the Ministry of Steel’s monthly release of 5 November (PIB 2333256, provisional JPC data) puts finished steel imports for April–October 2026 at 4.90 million tonnes, up 28.9% from 3.80 million tonnes a year earlier, and exports at 3.98 million tonnes (+15.5%), so India is still a net importer. In October alone imports rose 65.8% to 0.76 million tonnes while exports fell 32%. Consumption rose 7.5% to 99.3 million tonnes against 3.9% growth in finished steel output (95.5 million tonnes). Reuters-based reports in early November name South Korea, not China, as the largest source so far this fiscal year, at about 1.4 million tonnes, followed by China, Japan and Russia; the 31.8% China share below is for April–August. A seventh open house was held on 28 October, and a Reuters-based report of 2 November says the government is considering extending the safeguard duty on select steel products; no notification had been issued. Finance ministry Notification No. 02/2025-Customs (SG) of 30 December 2025 (Gazette of India, Extraordinary, CG-DL-E-30122025-268941), read in the Gazette of India’s own text via the SansadSaar index of the archive.org Gazette of India collection. It sets 12%, 11.5% and 11% for the three years; a trade summary that gave the second-year rate as 5% was wrong. The duty covers hot-rolled, cold-rolled, metallic-coated and colour-coated flat products. Imports at or above set CIF prices (US$675 a tonne for hot-rolled coils, sheets and plates, $695 for plate-mill plates, $824 for cold-rolled and $861 for metallic-coated), and imports from listed developing countries (with exclusions that differ by product, China among them), are exempt; CBIC Circular 23/2026-Customs of 7 May 2026 explains how to claim the exemptions. Stainless steel and some special steels are outside the scope.

The duty is lower than it sounds in two ways. It steps down each year, so imports pay 0.5 percentage point less this year than last. And it has exemptions: an import priced above a set CIF threshold, or from a listed developing country, does not pay it. The size of the exemptions matters: no published figure for the share of imports that actually paid the duty was found, so it is not known how much of the 3.49 million tonnes was dutiable. Stainless steel, which more than doubled in imports, is outside the safeguard altogether.

## The other import rule: registration and quality orders

Imports of covered steel products have to be registered on the Steel Import Monitoring System. The ministry has made that lighter: SARAL SIMS lets small importers register consignments of up to 10 tonnes, with an annual limit of 1,000 tonnes, and the regular form was cut from 56 fields to 20. The ministry also said it was revoking the requirement of a no-objection certificate for grades not covered by a quality control order. Quality control orders, which require BIS certification for steel products, have been suspended or relaxed for some products in 2026, among them various stainless steel items from 27 April, as reported. The reported end date of that stainless exemption differs between sources (26 October 2026 in one, 31 March in another), and stainless steel producers have asked for the order to be reinstated, citing a 65% year-on-year rise in April stainless imports.

This is the policy that the open houses on steel import issues deal with. Each session lets importers and industry bodies raise registration and exemption problems one at a time. The ministry publishes the date and the signup rules; no published record of what was raised or decided was found.

## Who pays

A higher duty protects producers and costs users. Domestic steel prices rose in August and September, after a firmer demand picture and higher coking coal prices, and MSME users in Coimbatore have asked for a six-month suspension of import and safeguard duties, citing a rise of ₹8,000 to ₹10,000 a tonne between August and September. That figure is a trade-body claim, as reported, and is not a measured price series. The ministry’s reported position, no further tightening for now, sits between the two sides, and an anti-dumping case on hot-rolled steel from China, Japan and Russia is already open as a second route.

The data needed to judge the question is thin. Import volumes by product and country appear monthly in trade press from the Joint Plant Committee, but the safeguard exemptions, the SIMS registrations by importer type and the open-house outcomes were not found in any published form that would let an outsider test who benefits.
