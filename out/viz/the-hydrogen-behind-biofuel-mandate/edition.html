Biofuel rules cover road fuel, aviation and shipping in very different ways, and only the EU, the UK and a few of their member states tie a numbered hydrogen quota to them. This maps the rules and the hydrogen incentives country by country, with each status flagged, from summaries because no primary text was opened.

Top line

  • 1.2% of jet fuel the EU requires to be synthetic from 2030, the clearest hydrogen number in any fuel rule.
  • A$2/kg Australia's legislated hydrogen incentive, for output from 1 July 2027.
  • $38 bn cumulative public funding for low-emission hydrogen, per the IEA, skewed to supply.

Japan's 10% aviation target for 2030 is not the obligation it approved

What it means: The headline aviation numbers mix obligations in law with targets and indicative plans.

What to do: Read the statute or notice before quoting Japan's or India's 2030 figure as a rule.

What it means: Two per-kilogram credits have dates that bind when a producer must start building.

What to do: Confirm the US construction start rule with Treasury guidance before committing capacity.

Synthetic fuel quotas reach no more than 1.2% of fuel by 2030 in the EU and UK

What it means: Both quotas rise steeply after 2030, so the 2030 figure understates the later obligation.

What to do: Plan for the 2035 and 2040 steps, not only the 2030 figure.

What would fix this

  • The IEA and REN21 should publish a single, dated table of national blending and SAF obligations with a legal-status column (in force, adopted, proposed), so figures like the 60-country count are refreshed past 2023.
  • The European Commission should publish the counting rule for e-fuel hydrogen used in refineries toward the transport targets, and a table of which member states have legislated their e-fuel quotas.
  • The US Treasury should say in the final 45Z regulations how the 45V exclusion applies when a fuel plant buys hydrogen from a separate plant, so the stacking question has an answer.
  • India’s Ministry of New and Renewable Energy and the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should state whether a green hydrogen consumption obligation for refineries has been notified, and at what levels, so the missing link to the SAF targets is either closed or acknowledged.
  • Japan’s METI should name the legal vehicle and entry-into-force date of its SAF supply obligation, so the gap between the 10% target and the 1% to 5% rule is clear.
  • ICAO should publish a default life-cycle value for power-to-liquid fuel in its CORSIA table if one exists, or state that none does, so e-fuel mandates have a common yardstick.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
