In February 2022 the Union Budget promised to replace the Special Economic Zones Act, 2005 with a new law. The draft that followed, the Development of Enterprise and Service Hubs (DESH) Bill, would have turned SEZs into “development hubs” that could serve the Indian market as well as export, dropped the rule that every unit must be a net earner of foreign exchange, and offered a 15% corporate tax rate. It is still the version of SEZ reform that exam-prep explainers, such as Drishti IAS’s, describe.

The Bill never reached Parliament. Four and a half years later, the SEZ Act of 2005 is still the law. What has changed instead is a series of rules and notifications, each taking one piece of the DESH idea in a narrower form, while a government committee drafts an “SEZ 2.0” whose report has not been published. This piece sets out what the Bill proposed, why it stalled, which parts have arrived anyway, and what the zones themselves look like now.

## What the Bill proposed

SEZs were built for export. A unit inside a zone imports inputs duty-free and does not pay domestic indirect taxes, but anything it sells into the rest of India, the “domestic tariff area”, is treated as an import and pays full customs duty on the finished good. It must also show positive net foreign exchange over five years. For most of the zones’ history the main attraction was an income-tax holiday, which the government let lapse: new zones stopped qualifying after March 2017 and new units after 2020, as this blog’s [piece on a shrinking Ahmedabad textile SEZ](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/09/a-textile-sez-built-to-re-employ-mill.html) describes.

The DESH draft, as reported, tried to answer both problems. Zones would become development hubs, split into enterprise hubs and service hubs, and the list of permitted services would widen beyond IT. The net foreign exchange test would go. Goods sold into India would pay duty only on the imported inputs they contained, not on the finished product, with an equalisation levy where needed to keep competition fair with factories outside the hubs. States would gain a bigger role in proposing hubs, approvals would run through a single online window with deadlines, and a 15% corporate tax rate would apply to hub units until 2032.

There was also a trade-law reason. In October 2019 a World Trade Organization panel, in a case brought by the United States, found that India’s SEZ scheme, along with four other export programmes, gave prohibited export subsidies, because its tax and duty benefits were tied to exporting. India appealed in November 2019, but the WTO’s Appellate Body has had no quorum since December 2019, so the appeal sits unheard and the ruling has no force. Removing the export conditions was meant to put the zones beyond that challenge.

The Finance Ministry objected to the money. In October 2022 it rejected the 15% tax rate, reportedly because firms outside the hubs would demand the same, and it also resisted letting hub units sell into India on payment of duty on inputs alone. NITI Aayog questioned dropping the net foreign exchange test. By November 2023 the government was reported to be considering abandoning DESH and amending the existing Act instead. An amendment bill was then tipped for the new government’s first 100 days in 2024 and for the 2025 monsoon session; the PRS Legislative Research bill tracker lists no SEZ bill after the 2019 amendment, and no report found for this piece says one was introduced.

## What has arrived anyway

Three changes since 2025 carry parts of the DESH idea, each in narrower form, and none needed Parliament.

Smaller zones for chips. Amended rules notified on 3 June 2025 cut the minimum area for SEZs making semiconductors or electronic components to 10 hectares (from 50, according to most reports), let semiconductor units count goods received or supplied free of charge toward net foreign exchange, and allowed relaxations on land that is mortgaged or leased to government agencies.

A one-year window to sell in India. The 2026-27 Budget announced, and Customs Notification 11/2026 of 31 March 2026 put into effect, a lower customs rate for goods that SEZ factories sell into India between 1 April 2026 and 31 March 2027. The reason given was spare capacity caused by global trade disruption.

The conditions are tight. A unit must have started production by 31 March 2025; its domestic sales under the scheme are capped at 30% of its highest yearly export value in the previous three years; the goods need at least 20% value addition; duty drawback is not allowed on them; and warehousing zones are excluded. The duty is still charged on the finished good, only at a lower rate, which is not the inputs-only model DESH proposed.

Returned goods. The Third Amendment Rules, notified on 15 October 2026, let an SEZ unit take back goods that a domestic buyer refuses, for example after a change in fashion or a breach of contract, if they come back within 30 days, and claim a refund of the duty paid. A Business Standard column argued that 30 days is too short to settle a failed delivery. A Fourth Amendment around 21 October eased rules for fund and aircraft- and ship-leasing units in the International Financial Services Centre at GIFT City.

The bigger rewrite is with a committee. In March 2026 the Department of Commerce set up a 17-member inter-ministerial panel, drawn from Commerce, Customs, NITI Aayog and DPIIT and chaired, according to the order, by the Additional Secretary for SEZs, to produce a concept paper and roadmap for “SEZ 2.0” within six months. Its remit includes reviewing the SEZ Act and aligning SEZs with the other duty-free schemes: export-oriented units, manufacturing in bonded warehouses (MOOWR), Advance Authorisation, EPCG and DFIA. In July it was reported to be close to submitting; topics included letting domestic buyers pay for SEZ services in rupees and allowing job work for domestic firms without an export link. No report had been published by 27 October.

On 31 July 2026, according to a Lok Sabha reply, 436 SEZs had been approved and 372 notified, of which 277 were operating and 95, about a quarter, were not. Haryana had the most idle zones (14), followed by Telangana (12), Karnataka (11), Tamil Nadu and Uttar Pradesh (9 each). Another 110 had been de-notified. The reasons the ministry gave were slow approvals from statutory and state bodies, a poor global business climate and “changes in fiscal incentives”, which is to say the end of the tax holidays.

The zones that work still matter. Employment in SEZs rose from 26.96 lakh in 2021-22 to 32.61 lakh in 2025-26, and SEZ exports of US$172 billion in 2024-25 were about 21% of India’s total exports of goods and services that year.

Some reports in 2026 said SEZ exports “fell” to US$133.45 billion in 2025-26 from about US$172 billion. That compares nine months with twelve: US$133.45 billion (Rs 11.66 lakh crore) is the April–December 2025 figure in the government’s own SEZ fact sheet, which says it was 31.5% higher than in the same months of 2024. For the full year, the Lok Sabha reply gives Rs 16.4 lakh crore in 2025-26 against Rs 14.63 lakh crore in 2024-25, a rise of about 12%.

The official figures do not quite fit together, though. Taken at face value, the two releases imply January–March 2026 exports of about Rs 4.74 lakh crore, 18% below the Rs 5.76 lakh crore of January–March 2025, after nine months of 31% growth. Either the last quarter fell sharply or the two releases measure different things, for example goods only against goods and services. The government’s figures for 2024-25 also differ by source: US$172.27 billion on the SEZ export page, US$173.07 billion in the fact sheet.

## What it adds up to

DESH tried to settle three questions at once: whether SEZs exist to export or to make things in India, who pays for the incentives, and whether the scheme can survive WTO rules. The government has answered the third by letting the tax holidays expire. It has refused the second, at least for corporate tax. On the first it is moving one notification at a time: a temporary, capped window for domestic sales, smaller zones for one strategic sector and more practical rules on returns. That is cheaper and reversible, but it leaves a quarter of notified zones idle and the export-only design of the 2005 Act in place. The SEZ 2.0 report is the next chance to decide what the zones are for, and its first test will be whether it is published.
