+42%rise in the price of a bag of NPK 12-32-16 or 10-26-26, to Rs 2,450

-0.6%change in DAP sales, its price held at Rs 1,350 a bag

13%monsoon shortfall, the worst since 2015; kharif area fell only 1.2%

Indian farmers bought less of every major fertiliser between April and September 2026 than a year earlier, according to industry figures reported by Rural Voice and The Wire. The falls were not even. Sales of NPK complex fertilisers dropped 22.2% and potash (MOP) 11.7%, while urea fell 4.1% and DAP, the phosphate fertiliser most exposed to import prices, barely moved (-0.6%). In September’s core-industries index, fertiliser output was down 11.7%.

The obvious explanation is the weather: the monsoon ended 13% short, the weakest since 2015. This blog’s piece on the kharif subsidy, “India Raised the DAP Subsidy 18% This Kharif. Imported DAP Still Costs About Rs 30,000 a Tonne More Than It Earns”, suggested another: a subsidy that does not cover import costs. This piece tests both against the product-by-product figures. The answer is that sales fell most where prices rose, and least where the price was held.

DAP and urea both have retail prices that did not change. Urea’s is fixed by the government at Rs 266.50 for a 45 kg bag; DAP’s is formally set by companies but has been held at Rs 1,350 a bag, which industry sources describe as an indirect cap. NPK complexes, which carry nitrogen, phosphate and potash in one granule, had no such restraint. In Madhya Pradesh the cooperative federation’s price for a bag of 12-32-16 or 10-26-26 went from Rs 1,720 last kharif to Rs 2,450, and 16-16-16 from Rs 1,475 to Rs 2,050; companies quoted similar prices elsewhere.

Per kilogram of nutrient, a farmer now pays about Rs 82 for 12-32-16 against Rs 42 for DAP and Rs 13 for urea. A farmer who switches from a complex fertiliser to DAP and urea pays less and gets no potash, which is what the sales show: NPK and MOP down sharply, DAP flat. Sales of single super phosphate (SSP), a cheap phosphate fertiliser, also held up (-1.4%).

## Why NPK prices rose and DAP’s did not

  r2: NPK 10-26-26 | 1,720 → 2,450 | 16,257 → 19,068 | 50,657 → 68,068 (+34%)


Seller’s revenue = bag price per tonne plus the nutrient-based subsidy at the season’s per-kg rates (nitrogen Rs 43.02 → 47.32, phosphate Rs 43.60 → 52.76, potash Rs 2.38). Sulphur is ignored for these grades. This blog’s arithmetic.

A company selling a tonne of DAP received about Rs 59,787 this kharif, 9% more than last year, entirely from the higher subsidy. Imported DAP cost about Rs 89,500 a tonne at May’s tender price, so every imported tonne lost money, and DAP imports fell 11.2%. A tonne of 12-32-16 now earns about Rs 71,942, 33% more, because the companies passed most of their higher costs to farmers. The shortfall on DAP was absorbed by companies and, through lower imports, by stocks; the shortfall on NPK was passed on, and farmers bought less.

Costs rose for a reason beyond phosphate prices. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz from 28 February cut global sulphur exports by about 45%, and China and Russia restricted exports of sulphuric acid and sulphur, according to Kpler. Sulphur and sulphuric acid go into phosphoric acid and into sulphur-bearing grades such as 20-20-0-13. Domestic NPK production fell 35.5% in the half-year and 44% in September alone, and NPK imports fell 31.4%.

Mt = million tonnes, April–September. 2025 values are back-calculated from the reported 2026 values and percentage changes where only those were given. Supply minus sales ignores opening stocks, so it shows direction, not the stock level. India produces no potash and imports little SSP.

Comparing what came into the country with what was sold separates the two explanations. For urea, production and imports exceeded sales by about 2.1 million tonnes, after falling 0.9 million tonnes short a year earlier: urea was available and farmers bought less of it, which points to the weak monsoon. MOP looks the same: imports rose 41% while sales fell. NPK is the opposite: about 5.2 million tonnes came in against 6.0 million tonnes sold, so sales ran ahead of new supply and were met from stocks. NPK sales were limited by price and supply, not only by the rain.

September, when the monsoon was retreating, sharpens the contrast. Urea sales rose 8% and DAP 6.9% on the month a year earlier, but NPK fell 39.3%. Rain alone would have moved them together.

## What it means for soils and for rabi

The government has tried for years to move farmers away from urea and DAP towards balanced nutrition; in Punjab, Haryana and Uttar Pradesh the ratio of nitrogen to phosphate to potash applied is about 20:5:1, against a commonly cited ideal of 4:2:1. A season in which complex fertilisers became much dearer relative to DAP and urea pushes the ratio the wrong way. The kharif area sown was 110.7 million hectares, only 1.2% below last year, so less fertiliser went on almost the same land.

The rabi season, which uses more DAP, starts with DAP stocks built up and NPK stocks run down. Whether DAP stays at Rs 1,350 depends on rabi subsidy rates that had not been announced when this was written, and on whether phosphate and sulphur prices ease.
