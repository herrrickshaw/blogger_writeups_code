Two data sources have anchored most of this site's recent research: FBIL's reference rates behind every currency-volatility figure, and DGCI&S TradeStat behind every import/export number. They've never been combined directly. This piece asks the obvious question sitting between them — does a trading partner's currency volatility actually predict anything about the shape of India's trade with that partner — using the five-country dataset already built for the rupee-trade series.

Import size does not predict currency volatility: China is largest, yet its FX risk is low

What it means: China's $131,620.29M in imports carries 7.03% FX volatility, close to the USA's 6.00%.

What to do: Track China's import concentration as a separate risk from its currency exposure, as the post's section 4 recommends.

What would fix this

  • FBIL should publish a free, downloadable daily history of every reference rate it computes, and add rates for the currencies of India's largest trading partners where it does not yet cover them, so currency volatility can be measured from an official Indian source rather than Yahoo Finance closes.
  • DGCI&S should add an invoicing-currency breakdown to TradeStat's country-wise trade tables, so the currency exposure behind each partner's imports and exports can be measured directly instead of being proxied by the volatility of that partner's home currency.
  • The RBI should publish quarterly, by partner country, the value of trade settled in rupees under its rupee-settlement arrangements, so the Russia “rupee trap” — 12.3 times more imports than exports — can be followed over time rather than inferred from a single year's totals.
  • DGCI&S should offer a bulk download of monthly country-wise import and export values for all partners across all years in TradeStat, so analyses like this one can move beyond five countries and one year, the sample limit this post concedes cannot support a reliable correlation.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. Five data points cannot support a causal or even a statistically reliable correlational claim; every coefficient reported here should be read as descriptive pattern-spotting within this specific dataset, not as evidence FX volatility drives (or is driven by) trade-imbalance size in general. Extending this to more of India's trading partners, or to a longer time series, would be needed before treating the +0.88 correlation as more than an artefact of Russia's dual extremity.
