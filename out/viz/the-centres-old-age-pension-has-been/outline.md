On 14 October 2026 the Union Cabinet extended the National Social Assistance Programme, the Centre’s pension scheme for poor elderly people, widows and disabled people, for five more years, to March 2031. It renamed the programme the Pradhan Mantri Samajik Sahayata Yojana, with the old-age, widow and disability pensions becoming the PM Vridhavastha, PM Vidhawa and PM Divyangjan Pension Yojanas, and the family benefit becoming the PM Parivar Labh Yojana. It approved ₹53,428 crore for the five years.

It raised one amount. Disability pensions go up by a quarter, and more people will qualify. The old-age pension stays at ₹200 a month for people aged 60 to 79, the amount set in 2007. At the prices farm labourers pay, that ₹200 now buys about what ₹63 did then. Most pensioners get more because their state adds a top-up, but those top-ups range from ₹50 in Assam to ₹3,800 in Andhra Pradesh.

## What the Cabinet changed, and what it left alone

The programme pays only households below the poverty line. The Centre sets a minimum amount and funds it; states administer it and may add their own money. The Cabinet note says the old-age, widow and family benefit schemes “continue unchanged”.

Centre’s share, per month unless stated. Old-age amounts: ₹75 when the scheme began in 1995, ₹200 from 2007, ₹500 for those 80 and over from 2011; widow and disability pensions rose from ₹200 to ₹300 in 2012 (Lok Sabha USQ 1159, 11 February 2025).

The disability change does three things. The pension rises from ₹300 to ₹375 below age 80 and from ₹500 to ₹625 above it. The qualifying level of disability falls from 80% to 40%, the “benchmark disability” of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, and the pension is now payable from birth rather than from age 18. The government’s backgrounder expects disability beneficiaries to rise from 8.33 lakh to 27.48 lakh. The 25% rise is the margin the 2016 Act requires: disability assistance must be at least a quarter higher than similar schemes. Guidelines are due within three months of the decision.

## What ₹200 bought in 2007, and buys now

The Consumer Price Index for Agricultural Labourers, the index the government uses to revise rural job-guarantee wages, averaged 409 in 2007-08 and 1,299 in 2024-25 (1986-87 = 100). Prices for farm labourers’ households rose 3.18 times in those 17 years. To buy what it bought when it was set, the ₹200 would have had to be ₹635 in 2024-25. Read the other way, ₹200 in 2024-25 bought what ₹63 did in 2007-08.

The new disability pension of ₹375 is 65% of what the 2012 amount of ₹300 would need to be in 2024-25. The increase recovers part of the loss and is not a real rise.

## Against the World Bank’s poverty line

The World Bank’s international extreme-poverty line is $3.00 a day at 2021 purchasing-power prices. Converted at the 2021 rate for India it is about ₹58 a day, or ₹1,777 a month for one person. The Centre’s ₹200 is about ₹6.6 a day, 11% of that line. The government told the Lok Sabha in March 2025 that, with state top-ups, the average pension in most states is about ₹1,000, roughly 56% of the line. These comparisons flatter the pension, because the line is in 2021 prices and the pension in 2026 rupees.

## Where you live decides the pension

In July 2026 the government told the Lok Sabha that about 3 crore people get the central pensions and about 5.9 crore more are on state-funded schemes, roughly 9 crore in all. State top-ups on the central amount range from ₹50 to ₹5,700 a month, the highest being Andhra Pradesh’s disability pension. The states’ own announcements put some totals higher than the annex. Andhra Pradesh pays ₹4,000 under NTR Bharosa (₹6,000 for disabled people) to about 62 lakh pensioners, of whom 9.34 lakh are on the central scheme, at a cost of ₹33,485 crore in 2026-27. That one state’s pension bill is about three times the Centre’s yearly NSAP outlay for the whole country. Haryana raised its old-age allowance to ₹3,200 from 1 November 2025; it was ₹100 when it began in 1987. Bihar raised its pension from ₹400 to ₹1,100 from June 2025 for about 1.11 crore people.

At the other end, the Comptroller and Auditor General found in 2023 that pensioners in Goa, Nagaland, Manipur, Punjab and Assam received only ₹200 to ₹250, because those states added nothing or almost nothing to the central amount for NSAP beneficiaries.

## The budget has grown slowly

The Centre set aside ₹47,808.31 crore for NSAP for 2021-26 and allocated ₹9,636.32 crore in 2023-24. The new ₹53,428 crore is ₹10,686 crore a year, 12% more a year than the 2021-26 outlay, in nominal terms, with more disabled people to cover. Spread across 3.20 crore beneficiaries, it works out to about ₹278 per person a month, consistent with amounts that stay mostly at ₹200 to ₹500.

A revision of the central amounts was considered for 2021-26 and not approved; in August 2024 the government told the Lok Sabha this was “considering the available financial space”. Parliament’s Standing Committee on Rural Development, in its April 2025 report on the programme, called the amounts inadequate and asked for a periodic reassessment to reflect inflation and a larger central contribution. As a rough upper bound, paying all 216.6 lakh old-age pensioners the 2007 value of ₹635 instead of ₹200 would cost about ₹11,310 crore a year more, slightly more than the whole programme costs now.

## Late, short and sometimes to the dead

The CAG’s performance audit of NSAP (Report No. 10 of 2023) found that only 11 States and UTs paid pensions monthly, as the guidelines require. States were slow to claim the Centre’s second instalment, which delayed payments down the line. Pensions not paid from the date they became due left 92,602 beneficiaries in 11 States and UTs short by ₹61.71 crore. In 26 States and UTs, ₹2 crore was paid for 2,103 beneficiaries after they had died, and 2,243 people drew more than one pension. The CAG also found eligible people left out because no one went looking for them.
