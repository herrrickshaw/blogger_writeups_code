India expects roughly 11.2 million tonnes of solar-module waste by 2047, and there is real chemistry for pulling the silicon, silver, copper and aluminium back out of a dead panel. What doesn't exist yet is a way to do that recycling without losing money on every tonne processed — and the rule that is supposed to eventually force the issue currently tells module owners mostly to keep the waste in a shed until the 2034-35 financial year, not to recycle it.

Solar panel recycling in India: both recycling pathways lose money per tonne

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 68% of P1's recurring cost is buying the waste module: ₹27,300 a tonne, the same in both pathways.
  • ₹10,230 a tonne is the net loss on mechanical recycling at today's input prices; chemical recycling loses ₹12,341.
Updated 7 November 2026. Two things have moved since this was written. India’s installed solar capacity reached about 171 GW at the end of September 2026, after 35.2 GW was added in January–September (MNRE data, as reported). And in March 2026 the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) issued its first technical guidelines for handling end-of-life solar equipment under the 2022 E-Waste Rules; they set out how waste should be handled, but solar PV is still exempt from numeric EPR recycling targets, so the economics below are unchanged. A CPCB reply to the National Green Tribunal (date unclear) says 248 of 322 registered e-waste recyclers had applied to recycle solar modules. Sources: SolarQuarter, JMK Research, Outlook Business, CPCB reply to the NGT (snippet-only).

What it means: Waste procurement is 68% of P1's recurring cost and identical in P2, so the input price drives the loss.

What to do: Treat the waste-acquisition price as the policy target, through mandated hand-over from developers and insurers rather than open bidding.

Both pathways lose money on every tonne of solar waste they process

What it means: P2 recovers 23% more revenue than P1, but its processing cost is nearly double, so it still loses ₹12,341 a tonne.

What to do: Start the EPR collection-and-recycling targets from 2027, as CEEW recommends, rather than waiting for the 2034-35 storage window.

Both pathways break even only at waste-module prices well below market

What it means: P1 breaks even at ₹375 a module and P2 at ₹330, both well below the market rate of about ₹600.

What to do: Use the mechanisms CEEW models, such as extending EPR-certificate trading to copper, then silver and silicon, to close that gap.

India's installed solar fleet has grown more than fourfold since 2019

What it means: Modules on 25–30-year hardware turn today's small waste stream into a much larger one, from a 2021 high scenario of 34 kt by 2030.

What to do: Set EPR collection and recycling targets now, before the fleet reaches end of life, as CEEW proposes.

What would fix this

  • CPCB should publish each year, from the E-Waste Management System portal, the number of registered solar PV producers, the tonnage of modules they have placed on the market and the tonnage of waste held in storage, by state, so the inventories Chapter V already requires become a public measure of the waste waiting for 2034–35.
  • CPCB should publish a list of recyclers registered to process solar PV modules, with each one’s authorised capacity in tonnes a day and the process used, so national capacity can be checked against the 150 tonnes-a-day ambition and the 2.5 tonnes a day of a pilot like Gummidipoondi.
  • MNRE should publish installed solar capacity by module technology — conventional crystalline silicon, TOPCon and CdTe thin-film — and by year of commissioning, so waste projections like 11,221 kt by 2047, and the silver content that decides recycling economics, rest on official fleet data.
  • MoEFCC should require solar PV producers to state in their annual returns the financial provision they hold for recycling the modules they have placed on the market, so a storage mandate that defers disposal also shows whether anyone is setting money aside for it.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. The two reports' waste-volume figures — a 34 kt "high scenario" by 2030 in Report 2, versus CEEW's 11,221 kt cumulative figure by 2047 — use different time horizons, base-fleet assumptions and (presumably) methodologies, and should not be read as competing estimates of the same number. Separately, CEEW's own website appears to title this same underlying work in at least two different ways across its own pages ("How Much Does it Cost to Recycle a Solar Module in India?" on the report itself, "Is Solar Module Recycling in India Financially Feasible?" on at least one ceew.in publication listing) — a minor inconsistency this piece is flagging rather than resolving.
