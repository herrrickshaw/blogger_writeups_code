Joining small molecules into a plastic or an oil chain releases heat; it does not store it. Breaking the chain back absorbs heat, and happens only because, at high enough temperature, the gain in entropy wins. A catalyst cannot change that balance, but it lets a refinery or a pyrolysis plant crack at a lower, controlled temperature and steer what comes out. Refineries discard tonnes of used cracking catalyst every day; put first in a staged process, ahead of newer research catalysts, it could take the dirtiest part of turning waste plastic and tyres back into fuel and chemicals. Indian refineries now pay to have it taken away, while India’s tyre pyrolysis plants recover about 41% of their feed as an oil that has no national standard.

Updated 28 October 2026. The plastic section has been checked against the Gazette of India. The Plastic Waste Management (Amendment) Rules, 2026 (G.S.R. 237(E), 31 March 2026, CG-DL-E-31032026-271465) now count conversion of plastic waste into feedstock chemicals or new plastic as recycling, not end-of-life disposal. This piece had said the rules had no category for pyrolysis that returns feedstock; that was wrong, and the paragraph and the related proposal have been corrected. The 19 January 2026 instrument is a Central Pollution Control Board notice, not an amendment to the Rules.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Polyethylene starts to break down 142°C lower over a ZSM-5 zeolite

What it means: Acid sites in the zeolite let polyethylene start to crack at a lower temperature, without changing the energy the reaction needs.

What to do: Refinery and pyrolysis operators should trial zeolite-loaded beds to hold their reactors at a lower, controlled temperature.

Oil is the largest output of India's tyre pyrolysis, at 41% by weight

What it means: Gas and losses take about 18% of the feed, which the reactor can burn to supply its own heat.

What to do: Sell the cleaned oil to refiners for hydrotreating, the route NITI Aayog points to for tyre oil.

In-reactor additives cut tyre-oil sulphur from 1.16% to as low as 0.38%

What it means: Basic oxides such as lime and red mud hold sulphur in the solid char, so the oil leaves the reactor cleaner.

What to do: Test red mud and lime beds at a plant, then hydrotreat the oil, because in-reactor capture alone does not reach diesel quality.

What would fix this

  • The Central Pollution Control Board should publish, state by state, how many tyre pyrolysis units have been verified as compliant with the revised procedure and how many have been closed, with each unit’s batch capacity, so the 2025 enforcement drive can be checked.
  • The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change should decide on its Technical Review Committee’s recommended crumb-rubber mandate for bitumen producers, stating whether 3–5% means the tonnage of rubber modifier or the share of bitumen sold as CRMB and how much waste tyre it implies, and the Central Pollution Control Board should set out how pyrolysis oil that goes to a refinery or cracker as feedstock is verified and credited as recycling under the March 2026 definition, distinct from pyrolysis to fuel, so that both routes are rewarded for what they return.
  • Indian Oil and the other public-sector refiners should open their spent-FCC-catalyst disposal tenders to authorised pyrolysis and catalyst-reuse units as well as cement co-processors, and publish the quantity and the awarded price, so that catalyst the refinery now pays about ₹6,000 a tonne to dispose of can be tested as a cheap first-stage pyrolysis catalyst, and the market for it becomes visible.
  • Indian Oil’s R&D centre at Faridabad and CSIR–Indian Institute of Petroleum, Dehradun, should run and publish a staged-catalyst pilot on Indian mixed plastic waste and tyre crumb, with refinery equilibrium catalyst, mixed with lime or red mud for tyres, as the first stage and a tuned zeolite as the second, reporting oil, olefin and aromatic yields, oil sulphur, catalyst life and heat input per tonne.
  • The Bureau of Indian Standards should act on NITI Aayog’s January 2026 recommendation to notify a standard for tyre pyrolysis oil, and extend it to plastic pyrolysis oil taken by refineries for co-processing, with limits on sulphur, chlorine, metals and solids of the kind refinery buyers already apply, so that the 1.1 million tonnes of tyre oil India made in FY2024 can be sold as a specified feedstock rather than a fuel of unknown quality.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

The rules partly explain why. Per tonne of tyre, CRMB earns about 1.1 EPR certificates under CPCB’s weightage and conversion factors, against about 0.5 for batch pyrolysis, by this blog’s calculation from the factors NITI Aayog reproduces. That should favour roads. But a pyrolysis plant sells its oil and char every day, while CRMB has to be made under high-shear mixing at a refinery or licensed plant, kept hot and agitated, and laid within six to eight hours. At the March 2025 committee meeting Indian Oil, which makes most of India’s CRMB, said its CRMB capacity was underused and that sales were below production capacity; at the July meeting Bharat Petroleum said it made none and argued that PMB performs better in the field. Road builders are choosing between the two, and the committee recorded that it could not establish why contractors and engineers prefer one over the other.
