Global crude storage exists for exactly the situation the market is in right now: since a US-Israel military campaign against Iran opened in late February 2026, the Strait of Hormuz — the transit point for roughly a quarter of the world's seaborne crude — has been under sustained attack, and the world's storage and shipping system has been absorbing the shock in real time. This piece lays out where crude actually sits when it isn't moving — onshore tanks and caverns, strategic reserves, and floating storage at sea — and what the 2026 disruption has actually done to each of those pools, as distinct from the steady-state numbers usually quoted for them.

Top line

  • 284.6M bbl left in the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve in the week to 18 September 2026, 39.9% of its 714M bbl authorised capacity.
  • 135M bbl of Iranian crude sat in floating storage in early August, more than the 80-120M bbl global pool usually seen in calm markets.
  • 85 to 1 daily Strait of Hormuz transits, pre-crisis baseline against the PortWatch count for 20 September 2026.

What it means: The US reserve was drawn down by a 172M bbl emergency release, and it fell to its lowest level since 1983 in early August.

What to do: ISPRL should publish fill levels for India's three strategic sites, so the 39M bbl designed capacity can be read against barrels actually held.

Iran's floating crude alone is larger than the usual global pool

What it means: The US port blockade stops empty tankers returning for the next loading, so the pile-up grows even without new demand for the oil.

What to do: The IEA should publish a dated global floating-storage total, with its definition, in each Oil Market Report while the disruption lasts.

Strait transits fell from about 85 a day to 1 on 20 September

What it means: The two readings come from different counting methods, and the post does not reconcile them.

What to do: IMF PortWatch should publish its Strait of Hormuz transit counting method alongside its daily figures, so the two readings can be reconciled.

What would fix this

  • ISPRL should publish the fill level of each strategic reserve site — Mangaluru, Visakhapatnam and Padur — at regular intervals, as the US does weekly for its SPR, so India's row in a comparison like this shows barrels held, not only the 39 million barrel designed capacity.
  • PPAC should publish monthly crude and product stocks held by Indian refiners and marketers, expressed in days of net import cover, so India's total buffer can be read against the IEA's 90-day benchmark during a disruption like the 2026 Hormuz crisis.
  • The IEA should include a dated global floating-storage total, with its definition, in each Oil Market Report while the Hormuz disruption lasts, since this post could find only an Iran-specific figure and warns readers against undated global totals circulating elsewhere.
  • IMF PortWatch should publish its counting method for Strait of Hormuz transits alongside its daily figures, so a reading of one transit on 20 September 2026 can be reconciled with official claims of roughly 60 ships a day in the same week.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish
What this article does not establish. The precise current throughput of the Strait as a share of its pre-crisis ~25%-of-world-seaborne-crude baseline; the exact volume of crude production that Gulf producers have had to curtail as a direct result of the disruption, a figure reported in some analyses but not corroborated here against a primary source; and a reliable, dated, single figure for total global floating storage in August 2026, as opposed to the Iran-specific Vortexa figure this piece does have reasonably confidence in. Readers should treat any precise global floating-storage total quoted elsewhere for this period with the same caution this piece is applying to its own figures.

This piece deliberately separates figures this article has reasonable confidence in (the US SPR numbers, the Iran-specific floating-storage figure, the IEA demand-trajectory revisions, all tied to a specific named source and date) from figures that are widely repeated but harder to independently verify (China's total reserves, the precise current Hormuz throughput, and any single global floating-storage total for the current period). Where a figure could not be traced to a clear primary source, or where different outlets reported materially different numbers, that is flagged in the text rather than resolved by picking one. This is a genuinely fast-moving situation; figures here should be treated as dated to the sources cited, not as a live picture.
