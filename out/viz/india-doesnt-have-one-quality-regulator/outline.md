## The four-layer stack nobody explains up front

India doesn't regulate quality through one law or one authority. Parliament and the ministries write the Acts — the BIS Act, the Food Safety and Standards Act, the Drugs & Cosmetics Act, the Energy Conservation Act, the Legal Metrology Act, the Geographical Indications Act, and others. Each Act creates or empowers a statutory regulator that owns one slice of risk and issues its own licences or marks: BIS for industrial goods, FSSAI for food, CDSCO for drugs and medical devices, the WPC Wing and TEC for telecom equipment, PESO for explosives, BEE for energy labels, Legal Metrology for weighing and measuring instruments. Sitting apart from all of them is a fourth thing entirely: an accreditation apex, the Quality Council of India (QCI) and its constituent boards — NABCB, NABL, NABH, NABET — that certifies no product at all. It accredits the certifiers, auditors and testing labs that the statutory regulators and the market both rely on. Below that sit the actual certification and testing bodies — some public, most private but accredited — who do the work and put the mark on the product a shopper actually sees: ISI, the FSSAI logo, Agmark, a GI tag, a star label, an ISO 9001 certificate, a Silk Mark.

Two things trip almost everyone up. A mark can be voluntary for most products and mandatory the moment a Quality Control Order names a specific one — the ISI mark works exactly this way. And an ISO 9001 certificate and a government mark are different systems sharing the same infrastructure: ISO 9001 comes from a private certification body, credible only because NABCB accredited it; BIS itself also happens to sell ISO certification as one commercial certifying body among many, which is a separate voluntary business line, not a regulatory requirement.

## The layer nobody's heard of, holding up every mark they have

The Quality Council of India was set up in 1997 as a registered society, anchored by the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT) alongside industry bodies ASSOCHAM, CII and FICCI — a direct response to India needing a WTO/TBT-compliant accreditation infrastructure rather than every ministry inventing its own. Ratan Tata was its first chairman. QCI itself certifies nothing; it runs four accreditation boards that certify the certifiers — NABCB for certification, inspection and verification bodies, NABL for testing and calibration labs, NABH for hospitals, NABET for education, training and environmental-audit consultants. Every ISO 9001 stamp, every NABH plaque on a hospital wall, every accredited lab report used to win an ISI licence traces its credibility back through one of these four boards to QCI, and from QCI to DPIIT. Nobody outside the standards world names QCI the way they name BIS, but structurally it sits a level above BIS, FSSAI and CDSCO all at once — accrediting the labs and certifiers each of them depends on to actually test anything.

## The mark that's voluntary until a notification makes it illegal not to have

The ISI mark is India's oldest product mark, predating BIS itself by four decades — it traces to the Indian Standards Institution, BIS's 1947-founded predecessor, with BIS itself constituted in 1987 and re-constituted under the current BIS Act, 2016. By default, certification is voluntary: a manufacturer can apply for an ISI licence on any product an Indian Standard covers, through BIS's testing and factory-audit process. It becomes compulsory, not merely encouraged, the moment a product category is named in a Quality Control Order under the 2016 Act — at that point selling the product without the mark is illegal. DPIIT told the Lok Sabha on 10 March 2026 that 142 Quality Control Orders covering 721 products were in force, spanning steel, cement, toys and electrical appliances. Furniture’s order was notified in February 2025 and took effect on 13 February 2026. The list keeps moving, which is exactly where the next section's correction comes in.

## Three "recent changes," and one of them isn't real anymore

The source repository names three specific, dated examples of this system actually moving, each checked independently for this piece.

Confirmed, and still current: BEE's January 2026 expansion. The Bureau of Energy Efficiency's Standards & Labelling programme made star ratings mandatory for a fresh batch of appliances — frost-free and direct-cool refrigerators, deep freezers, certain room air-conditioner types, colour and UHD televisions, LPG gas stoves, cooling towers, chillers, distribution transformers and grid-connected solar inverters — from 1 January 2026, under Gazette notification No. 5506/BEE/S&L/AL/2025-26 (dated 26 December 2025). Independent trade coverage describes it as the most sweeping expansion of the mandatory list in the 20-year history of the programme, corroborating the source repository's framing. Solar-inverter manufacturers separately faced a 28 February 2026 deadline to resubmit registrations or have existing models deactivated.

Confirmed, and narrower than headlines suggest: FSSAI's BIS/AGMARK carve-out. The Food Safety and Standards (Prohibition and Restrictions on Sales) First Amendment Regulations, 2024, gazetted 17 October 2024, removed the requirement for a separate BIS certification mark on packaged drinking/mineral water and certain milk products, and removed the requirement for a separate AGMARK mark on multi-sourced edible vegetable oil. A valid FSSAI licence now suffices on its own for those specific categories. Trade press often dates this to "February 2024," which is when FSSAI's governing body approved the change; the operative legal date is the October gazette notification. The change doesn't touch BIS or AGMARK requirements anywhere else — a narrow carve-out, not a general rollback.

Not confirmed — actively wrong as of this piece's research: "Scheme-X," due September 2026. The source repository's BIS entry, fetched 24 September 2026, describes Scheme-X — a lighter-touch compulsory certification scheme for machinery and electrical equipment — as "due to take effect in September 2026." That's no longer true. Scheme-X's implementation date was pushed from an original August 2025 target to 1 September 2026 by the Ministry of Heavy Industries, via a Quality Control Order published 12 June 2025 — and then, before that pushed-back date ever arrived, the Ministry deferred it again on 13 November 2025, this time to a date still to be notified (S.O. 5179(E)), and then repealed the underlying Machinery and Electrical Equipment Safety (Omnibus Technical Regulation) Order, 2024 outright by an order dated 14 January 2026 (S.O. 239(E), published in the Gazette on 16 January). Scheme-X launched in August 2024, was postponed, suspended, and is now fully scrapped; goods that would have needed its certification can be imported without it, and the wider BIS/QCO architecture around it is untouched. This is the exact failure mode the source repository's own README warns readers about — "check the fetch date... a scheme notified after that date won't be reflected yet" — playing out in one of its own two headline examples of recent movement.

A field guide built specifically to track how fast India's quality-regulation list moves already has one entry overtaken by events. That isn't a flaw in the source; it's the whole point it's trying to make, demonstrated on itself.

## Since this piece was first written, the list has shrunk

The list now moves in both directions. From July 2025, ministries withdrew compulsory certification for a long run of industrial inputs, mostly through orders that give no reason beyond “the public interest”. The Lok Sabha answer of March 2026 marks each withdrawal in its annexe:

- Chemicals and petrochemicals. Acetic acid, aniline and methanol on 23 July 2025; six fatty-acid and acid-oil standards on 22 October; styrene, maleic anhydride and acrylonitrile on 24 October. On 12 November 2025 the department rescinded 14 orders at once (S.O. 5129(E) to 5142(E)), covering terephthalic acid, ethylene glycol, five polyester yarns and fibres, polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC, ABS, EVA, polyurethanes and polycarbonate. Six more followed on 20 November (including pyridine, H-acid and vinyl sulphone) and seven standards on 28 November (including p-xylene, toluene, vinyl chloride monomer and ethylene dichloride).

- Metals. The Ministry of Mines withdrew its orders on aluminium, copper, nickel, lead, tin and zinc on 13 November 2025.

- Fibres. The Ministry of Textiles rescinded the viscose staple fibre order on 18 November 2025, citing supply constraints and higher costs, and the cotton bales order on 9 June 2026.

- Steel. On 20 November 2025 the Ministry of Steel deferred the implementation of dozens of its standards by three years, and in 2026 it suspended or exempted further grades, including 162 semi-finished stainless grades until 31 March 2027.

- Suspensions. The linear alkyl benzene order has been suspended in steps, most recently to 31 December 2026, and the n-butyl acrylate order was suspended in July 2026 on account of “supply chain disruption”.

The fall in DPIIT’s count, from 190 orders and 773 products in August 2025 to 142 orders and 721 products in March 2026, is about the size of those withdrawals. Business Standard reported that they followed a review by a committee chaired by Rajiv Gauba, a member of NITI Aayog, which proposed cancelling, suspending or deferring orders covering more than 200 products; this blog found no published report from the committee. The two departments that count orders also disagree: the Department of Consumer Affairs’ year-end review still gave 191 orders covering 773 goods.

New orders kept arriving for finished goods even as inputs left the list. The household electrical appliances safety order came into force on 1 October 2026. A Hand Tools order of 9 October 2026 (S.O. 5561(E)) applies from 1 April 2027, and a second cookware order of 13 October covers aluminium utensils, with beverage cans from April 2027. The pattern, for now, is fewer mandatory standards on the materials factories buy and more on finished products.

The other two examples above have moved too. The Bureau of Energy Efficiency’s mandatory label for induction hobs, due from 1 July 2026, was pushed to 1 January 2027; BusinessLine linked the delay to LPG supply concerns. And FSSAI added a testing scheme for packaged drinking water and mineral water from 1 January 2026, so the 2024 carve-out removed a second mark, not the checking.

## What this means for anyone actually trying to sell something

The practical upshot of the four-layer structure is that "is my product BIS-certified" is rarely the only or even the right question. A business needs to know: which statutory regulator actually claims jurisdiction over its product category (sometimes more than one — a packaged food product answers to FSSAI for safety and, depending on the product, AGMARK or BIS for specific grading or quality marks layered on top); whether that regulator's relevant mark is currently voluntary or has been pulled into a Quality Control Order naming that exact category; and, if a private certification body or lab is involved anywhere in the chain, whether it's actually accredited by the right NABCB or NABL scheme rather than just self-describing as "certified." None of that is static. The BEE, FSSAI and Scheme-X examples above are not exceptions — the source repository's own tracking suggests this is closer to the normal rate of change for a system with three dozen independent moving parts.
