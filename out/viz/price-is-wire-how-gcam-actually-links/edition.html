GCAM — the Global Change Analysis Model, maintained at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory — is usually described as a model that “links energy, water, land and the economy.” That phrase hides the actual mechanism. GCAM does not run one unified optimisation across all four; it solves five separate markets independently, five years at a time, with no subsystem allowed to see the future, and lets a single price signal carry information between them. The version CEEW and IIM Ahmedabad have run for India since 2007 uses exactly this machinery to work out what a net-zero target implies sector by sector. This piece works through how the wiring is actually built.

What it means: In one of the model's 32 regions, the scenario population peaks mid-century and then declines, a demographic input that shapes every downstream energy-service demand number in the model.

What to do: Check the population path against the latest UN or Census projection before treating any single-decade output as a forecast.

The urban share of India's population rises from 32.7% to 74.4% by 2100

What it means: In the India build set up in 2007–09, faster growth pulls people to cities sooner, and the medium-growth case reaches about 50% urban by 2050, so the urban share is an assumption rather than an output.

What to do: Test the urbanisation path against recent census urban-share data before relying on its link to economic growth.

Income per head is a scenario input that climbs from 1,617 to 35,811 dollars

What it means: GDP and population are scenario inputs in GCAM, so the income path is a chosen assumption rather than something the model derives from first principles.

What to do: Read each GCAM output alongside its GDP and population inputs, since runs with identical technology but different growth inputs will diverge.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change should publish, with any modelled pathway it releases for the 2070 net-zero target, the model used, whether it has foresight, and the GDP and population inputs, so readers can tell which modelling camp a sector-level number comes from.
  • NITI Aayog should commission and publish a comparison that runs one India 2070 scenario through a recursive-dynamic model such as GCAM and an intertemporal one such as REMIND or MESSAGEix, so the effect of foresight on India's estimated mitigation cost is measured rather than borrowed from MIT's EPPA work.
  • MoSPI should publish an annual series of the urban–rural gap in per-capita consumption from its household surveys, so modellers' assumptions that the gap narrows with growth can be tested against data, as CEEW itself notes the last three decades ran the other way.
  • The Bureau of Energy Efficiency should publish annual sales and installed stock of lamps by type — LED, CFL and incandescent, so energy models can calibrate lighting transitions to what actually happened, rather than assuming LEDs replaced incandescent bulbs when adoption data shows they mostly replaced CFLs.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
