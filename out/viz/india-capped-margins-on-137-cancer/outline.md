On 17 October 2026 the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA) capped the trade margin on 137 cancer medicines at 30%, from 9 November. Prices were expected to fall by up to 70%, and the government first put the saving at about Rs 2,500 crore a year; by 7 November, with revised prices reported for 121 of the 137 drugs, its estimate was Rs 3,502 crore a year. The list grew from the 110 drugs first proposed. Two months earlier, the Lok Sabha’s Standing Committee on Chemicals and Fertilizers reported that hospitals and pharmacies routinely sell medicines at 20–60% below their printed maximum retail price (MRP), which it read as evidence of large margins built into the price, and asked for a permanent trade-margin framework.

This piece explains how India controls medicine prices, why most medicines fall outside those controls, what earlier margin caps did, and why the regulator recovers so little of what it says companies overcharged.

Updated 9 November 2026. The revised prices took effect today. NPPA’s 7 November release says 76 companies had reported revised MRPs for 1,225 formulations covering 121 of the 137 drugs; 1,031 of them show a cut, 600 brands by more than half, the largest by 96.45%, and 10–15 of the listed drugs are not yet sold in India. Its savings estimate rose from Rs 2,500 crore to Rs 3,502 crore a year (a later count reported on 9 November, seen only in a secondary digest, gives 85 companies, 1,364 formulations and Rs 3,540 crore). Two developments bear on the argument here: the Supreme Court, hearing petitions on medicine mark-ups, said on 27 October that “economic policy” cannot take precedence over lives, while the Centre argued for “calibrated” controls; and an India Today analysis (3 November) found mark-ups on some drugs capped in 2019 back as high as 700%, the one-time-cap problem described below.

Updated 28 October 2026. Checked against the Gazette of India: NPPA’s order S.O. 5690(E) of 17 October 2026 (CG-DL-E-17102026-277271) caps the trade margin on 137 non-scheduled cancer drugs at 30% of the MRP, not 30% over the stockist price: its formula is MRP = price to stockist × {1 + 30/(100 − 30)} plus GST, about 1.43 times the March 2026 stockist price. The piece had shown both readings; the illustration table and chart now use the order’s formula. The order also covers patented drugs, treats hospitals that buy directly from the maker as stockists, excludes free patient-assistance supplies from the calculation, lets products already priced below the cap keep their MRP, and takes effect on 9 November 2026.

Most of the market is untouched by price control. India’s medicine sales were about Rs 2.41 lakh crore in 2025, and the committee puts the non-scheduled share at 82%, about Rs 1.97 lakh crore. For these, the only rule is that the MRP cannot rise by more than 10% a year. The launch price, and the margin in it, are left to the company. A 2023 Delhi High Court ruling (Bharat Serums) narrowed even that rule: the regulator can monitor non-scheduled prices and recover overcharges, but a breach does not take away a company’s right to raise prices later.

The committee’s December 2025 report showed what this allows. It cited a cetirizine pack with an MRP of Rs 21.06 against a stockist price of Rs 2, a markup of 953%, and pantoprazole at Rs 102 against Rs 10, a markup of 920%. Other common medicines were marked up by 600% to 1,800%. The public cannot see stockist prices, so it cannot see these margins.

In a usual branded-medicine chain, a wholesaler takes about 10% and a pharmacy about 20%, so the MRP ends up about 1.32 times the price to stockist. The order’s own formula (Table A of S.O. 5690(E)) sets the MRP at the price to stockist × {1 + 30/(100 − 30)}, plus GST: the 30% is a share of the MRP, so the MRP before GST can be at most about 1.43 times the March 2026 price to stockist. The order’s worked example takes a stockist price of Rs 70 to a maximum MRP of Rs 100 plus GST. That is close to, and slightly above, what an ordinary branded chain already earns. It bites only where margins are far above normal: on “trade generics”, sold to pharmacies with very high margins so that they push them, and on expensive specialty drugs like cancer medicines. The Competition Commission’s 2021 market study found that companies compete for pharmacy shelf space mainly by offering higher margins, especially on trade generics.

Every margin cap so far has cut prices sharply because the margins it removed were large. In 2019, 526 cancer-drug brands lowered their MRPs, by up to 90%. In 2021, 91% of reported brands of five home medical devices cut prices after the regulator found margins of up to 709%. But each cap was issued under the order’s emergency powers for a named list. The committee noted that there is still no timeline for turning trade-margin rationalisation into a standing part of the price-control order, and the 2026 list covers only cancer drugs.

When a company charges more than a ceiling price or raises a non-scheduled price by more than 10%, the regulator demands the excess back with interest. The demands have grown and the recoveries have not kept up. By March 2026 the regulator had demanded about Rs 10,030 crore and recovered about Rs 1,582 crore, 16%. The committee put the unrecovered sum at Rs 8,526 crore, and found 70% of it, about Rs 5,968 crore, tied up in court, some cases for more than 20 years. It recommended a fast-track mechanism for cases not in court and a legal cell inside the regulator for the rest.

## The cheaper alternative already on sale

Jan Aushadhi Kendras, the government’s generic-medicine pharmacies, price each medicine at no more than half the average of the top three brands, and often much less. One study found telmisartan 40 mg at a median Rs 7.19 a tablet in private pharmacies against Rs 1.1 at Jan Aushadhi, 85% less. That gap is roughly the size of the margins the committee found, which suggests the margin, more than the cost of making the medicine, is what separates the two prices. This blog’s [piece on Jan Aushadhi’s 2,439 products](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/09/jan-aushadhi-sells-2439-products-heres.html) looks at where their ingredients come from.
