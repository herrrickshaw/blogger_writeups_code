In 1975-76, India's public canal systems recovered 96% of what it cost to run them — not build them, just keep them operating — from the fees charged to farmers. By 1997-98 that had collapsed to 5.8%. It recovered to roughly 20% by 2013-14, then averaged only about 7% across 2000-01 to 2016-17 once the full run is counted. Nobody has published a national figure past 2017-18. This is the public, canal-and-dam half of India's irrigation story; the private half — the 96.6% of India's 23 million irrigation schemes that are borewells and tubewells farmers paid for themselves — this blog has already covered. This piece is the other half: what the public money actually gets back.

Top line

  • 96% of running costs covered by irrigation fees in 1975-76; the 2000-01 to 2016-17 average was 7.37%.
  • 22 million hectares of created irrigation potential was not being used in 2024-25.
  • 57.72% of AIBP dam and canal projects completed (71 of 123) as of 8 May 2026.

What it means: The CWC series shows recovery collapsing after 1975-76 and only partly recovering by 2013-14.

What to do: Track recovery with current data, since the CWC's latest known series stops at 2017-18.

About 22 million hectares of created irrigation potential goes unused

What it means: The gap between potential created and potential utilised is a persistent, named problem in India's irrigation planning.

What to do: Measure use of created potential each year, not only its creation, so the gap can be seen as it changes.

Under 60% of AIBP dam and canal projects were complete in May 2026

What it means: The flagship PMKSY component is well short of completion a decade into its spending cycle.

What to do: Report completion alongside each new project added to AIBP, since additions keep stretching the backlog.

What would fix this

  • Publish the recovery-rate series on a fixed annual schedule, not a three-to-five-year lag. A number that's structurally always years stale can't inform a current budget decision, only describe a past one.
  • Report recovery net of, not excluding, fixed capital cost, alongside the working-expenses-only figure. The current methodology is the most forgiving version of the question by design; publishing the stricter number next to it would show how much of the gap the generous framing is currently hiding.
  • Publish the state-by-state breakdown behind the "less-developed states recover more" finding. If that pattern holds up to scrutiny, it complicates a lot of assumptions about which states manage irrigation well; if it doesn't, the aggregate claim shouldn't keep circulating unexamined.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

None of the above shows that Indian irrigation delivers no value for the money spent on it — a canal system can generate large, diffuse agricultural and food-security benefits that never show up as a fee collected from a farmer, and a recovery rate measured against water charges alone was never designed to capture that. Nor does the historical 96%-to-7% arc mean recovery has fallen in a straight line; the 2013-14 partial rebound to roughly 20% shows the trend isn't monotonic, and a multi-year average can sit well below even a recent good year. This piece also does not establish why less-developed states recover a higher share than more-developed ones, only that research on the data has found this pattern; the mechanism is not stated in anything this piece could access. And the "22 million hectare" utilisation gap is a national aggregate; this piece does not have a state-by-state breakdown of where that gap actually sits.
