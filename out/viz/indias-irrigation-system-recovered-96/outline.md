## A generous measure, and it's still failing

"Financial recovery" for public irrigation in India has a specific, narrow meaning, and it's worth stating before the number, because the number looks bad even under the most forgiving version of the question. The Central Water Commission (CWC) defines it as gross receipts — water-fee collections and other charges — divided by working expenses, meaning operation and maintenance costs alone. It deliberately excludes the original fixed capital investment: the canal, the dam, the distributary network the state already spent to build. This is the recovery rate on running the system, not on having built it. Even by that generous definition, gross receipts covered only about 7.37% of working expenses on average between 2000-01 and 2016-17 — split between 1.59% recovered from actual water-sale fees and 5.79% from other charges. The system, on its own terms, doesn't cover the electricity and staff bill to keep the canals flowing, let alone anything toward what it cost to dig them.

## The collapse, and the partial recovery, in one series

The CWC's own historical figures trace a specific arc, not a flat failure. In 1975-76, gross receipts covered roughly 96% of working expenses — irrigation fees were doing close to the job they were designed for. By 1997-98 that had fallen to a mere 5.8%. It partially recovered to about 20% by 2013-14, before the fuller multi-year average (2000-01 to 2016-17) settles at the much lower 7.37% figure above — a reminder that a single good year sitting inside a longer bad stretch can flatter a headline if quoted alone. One counterintuitive detail worth keeping: research on this data has found the recovery rate tends to run higher in India's less economically developed states than in its more developed ones — the opposite of what a simple "richer states manage their utilities better" prior would predict.

Why there's no 2020s number. The CWC's own irrigation-finance data is reported to run three to five years behind, and its most recent known publication, from 2020, carried figures only through 2017-18. This isn't a case of the number existing and this piece failing to find it — independent commentary on the sector specifically notes that "detailed studies focusing on issues such as why the financial recovery of the irrigation sector is poor in India are seldom available." The lag is structural, not a research gap this piece can close by searching harder.

## What the spending side adds — and what this piece can't add to it

[This blog's own reporting](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/09/india-irrigates-itself-966-of-its.html) on India's Minor Irrigation Census already introduced a 2024 peer-reviewed paper relevant here — Bathla, Kannan & Das, "Public investment in irrigation across the Indian states: Financial recovery and governance" (Agricultural Economics Research Review 37(1): 93–112) — in the context of that piece's private-irrigation argument. This piece is the public-investment counterpart the earlier one flagged but didn't develop: the paper examines public capital formation in major, medium and minor irrigation across 20 states from the triennium ending 1983-84 to the triennium ending 2019-20, alongside a governance index built from state-level indicators for 2001-02 to 2015-16. Its own published abstract states that a sizeable increase in public expenditure on irrigation during the 2000s "has barely corresponded" with net irrigated area, potential utilised, or the rate of financial recovery, and that recovery itself is positively associated with a state's agricultural income, capital stock, and area under rice and wheat — meaning the states that recover the most are the ones already concentrated on the two crops this blog's own urea and cropping-pattern coverage keeps returning to.

What this piece does not claim from that paper. The full text sits behind a free-registration wall on ICAR's own repository, and this piece's research environment could not reach it, ResearchGate's mirror, or several of the secondary sources checked while trying — an unusually broad set of blocked domains today, not the narrower government-site gaps this blog has flagged before. Every number in the table above comes from the CWC-sourced recovery-rate series, not from Bathla, Kannan & Das; this piece states their finding only in the words of their own public abstract, and does not attribute the specific recovery percentages above to their paper.

## The gap persists into the current year

The spending-versus-outcome mismatch the 2024 paper describes is still visible in the latest available national figures. As of 2024-25, India has created irrigation potential of roughly 112 million hectares against an ultimate irrigation potential estimated at about 139.9 million hectares — but actually utilises only about 90 million hectares of what's been created, a gap of roughly 22 million hectares that is created, funded, built, and not being used to irrigate anything. This is a persistent, named structural problem in India's own irrigation planning literature, not a one-year anomaly; the same gap between potential created and potential utilised has been flagged as a "major challenge" across successive irrigation reviews.

The current flagship spending vehicle shows the same shape of gap on its own numbers. Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY) was continued for 2021-26 with an overall outlay of ₹93,068.56 crore; as of August 2025, ₹64,407 crore had been released, reported as benefiting more than 27 million farmers through irrigation, micro-irrigation, watershed and groundwater components since the scheme's 2016-17 launch, with a further ₹6,587 crore budgeted for FY2026-27. Within that, the Accelerated Irrigation Benefits Programme (AIBP) component — the part that funds specific named dam and canal projects — had completed 71 of 123 projects (57.72%) as of 8 May 2026, and created 2,940.97 thousand hectares of irrigation potential against a target of 4,531.86 thousand hectares (64.90%). Potential-created is running ahead of project-completion, which is normal (a project can create partial potential before its last component is finished), but both figures sit meaningfully under 100% on a scheme now a decade old. The programme keeps adding to its own project list even as the existing backlog works through: the Cabinet approved adding Uttarakhand's Jamrani Dam and Bihar's Kosi-Mechi link to AIBP's coverage in 2026, each a multi-year addition to a completion rate that hasn't yet cleared 60%.
