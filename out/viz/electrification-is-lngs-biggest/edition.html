Global LNG runs at roughly 0.35 tonnes of CO2-equivalent per tonne produced on average, and the handful of plants built to beat that number by a wide margin lean on the same short list of levers — better heat recovery, smarter cooling, carbon capture for what's left, and increasingly, replacing the gas turbine with an electric motor. Public data from built and announced projects shows which levers actually move the number, and which one keeps running into the same wall: the power grid.

Top line

  • 0.35 t CO2e per tonne of LNG for the global average, against 0.15 t announced by LNG Canada Phase 1.
  • ~95% motor efficiency for electric drive, against 25–42% for gas turbines.
  • 13.4% of new liquefaction trains ordered in 2025 used electric-motor drive.

Careful conventional design already closes most of the gap to best-in-class

What it means: Careful design of a conventional gas-turbine plant gets close to the best reported figure before any electrification.

What to do: Judge each new LNG project against the 0.15 t target and the 0.16 t BC benchmark, the baselines the post uses.

Electric drive reaches about 95% efficiency, against 25–42% for gas turbines

What it means: Once transformer, drive and transmission losses are counted, the drivetrain gap narrows to about 40% against 34–36%.

What to do: Compare full drivetrain efficiency, not the motor rating alone, when judging an electric-drive plant.

Electric-drive LNG plants move from feasibility to construction and operation

  1. 2025Electric-motor drive takes 13.4% of new liquefaction train orders
  2. OperatingFreeport LNG, Texas: reportedly the largest all-electric LNG plant, with 675 MW built in
  3. July 2026Woodfibre LNG about 70% complete, completion scheduled for 2027
  4. 2028Ruwais LNG targets commercial operations, reported ahead of schedule

What it means: Freeport LNG is the only one of the three named electric-drive projects reported as operating.

What to do: Check where Woodfibre and Ruwais draw their power as they start up, since the post calls grid supply the binding question.

What would fix this

  • The British Columbia government should publish each operating LNG facility's verified annual emissions intensity against its 0.16 t CO2e per tonne benchmark, stating what is included (flaring, fugitive methane, feed-gas CO2), so LNG Canada's announced 0.15 target can be compared with an audited outcome.
  • The International Energy Agency should publish its LNG supply emissions estimates with a stated, standard system boundary and, where data allow, a plant-level breakdown, so the global 0.35 t CO2e average and single-project figures can be compared like for like.
  • BC Hydro should publish a dated schedule for the transmission capacity needed to serve the Kitimat site, so readers can track whether LNG Canada Phase 2 runs on electric drive or falls back to more gas turbines, which the post says would narrow or erase the emissions advantage.
  • British Columbia's Environmental Assessment Office should require new LNG applications to report modelled emissions for air, water and hybrid cooling at matched capacity, so the magnitude of the cooling trade-off, which the post found no public plant-specific figures for, becomes documented.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Sources and caveats

Global LNG emissions intensity (~20 g CO2e/MJ average, ~350 Mt CO2e/year, ~70% CO2 and 30% unburnt methane) is from the International Energy Agency's report on emissions from LNG supply. The 0.35 t CO2e/t LNG average across several international facilities, and LNG Canada Phase 1's announced ~0.15 t CO2e/t LNG target, are cited via the International Institute for Sustainable Development's analysis of Canadian LNG's climate impact. British Columbia's 0.16 t CO2e/t LNG regulatory benchmark for new facilities, LNG Canada's gas-turbine-driven Phase 1 design, and reporting on Phase 2's dependence on BC Hydro transmission buildout are from CBC News and the Globe and Mail's coverage of the project. Electric-motor vs. gas-turbine efficiency figures (25–30% single-shaft industrial, 38–42% aeroderivative, >95% electric motor, ~40% vs. 34–36% full-drivetrain) and the RasGas/Toshiba Starter-Helper Motor case studies are as sourced in this site's earlier piece on helper motors, linked above, which in turn drew them from Siemens Energy's 2023 Gastech, Hydrogen & Climatetech conference paper and turbomachinery trade coverage. LNG liquefaction market share and growth figures for electric-motor-driven trains (13.4% in 2025, ~15.4% CAGR to 2035) are from Global Market Insights' LNG Liquefaction Train Market report. Freeport LNG's all-electric configuration, Woodfibre LNG's reported ~14-fold emissions reduction, and ADNOC Ruwais LNG's positioning as the first all-electric LNG export site in the Middle East/Africa region are from Oil & Gas Journal, LNG Industry and GE Vernova trade coverage; none of the three has been independently verified against the operator's own technical filings for this piece. The Hammerfest LNG grid-disturbance and under-voltage ride-through findings are from the 2024 PCIC Energy conference paper by Innomotics and Equinor engineers, as previously sourced on this site. Waste-heat-recovery and turbo-expander figures are from a GPPS (Global Power and Propulsion Society) viewpoint on decarbonization design options for gas-turbine-driven LNG plants. Carbon-capture project status (Venture Global, ExxonMobil Gulf Coast CCS, Woodside's Tellurian/Louisiana LNG acquisition) is from company disclosures and 2025–2026 industry trade coverage; capture volumes and timelines for projects still under construction should be treated as company-stated targets, not verified outcomes. Nothing in this piece is engineering, investment or procurement advice; any figure that matters to a decision should be checked against the primary source cited.
