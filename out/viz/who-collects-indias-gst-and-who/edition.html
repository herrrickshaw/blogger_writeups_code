Five states — Maharashtra, Karnataka, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu and Haryana — generate more than 40% of India's domestic GST. When the Centre redistributes tax revenue back to states through Finance Commission devolution, that same group receives a small fraction of what they contribute, while less industrialised, higher-population states receive several multiples of their GST share. None of this is new in principle — it is how India's Constitution designed fiscal federalism to work — but the actual 2025-26 numbers, pulled directly from GSTN's own statistics portal and a primary PIB release, put real figures on a debate that usually stays qualitative. This piece also finds something the debate doesn't usually mention: FY2025-26 was the first year in at least five that India's domestic GST collection actually fell, not just grew more slowly.

Updated 7 November 2026. October 2026 gross GST collections were about ₹2.31 lakh crore, up 22.9% on October 2025: domestic revenue rose 15.8% to about ₹1.59 lakh crore and import revenue 42% to about ₹72,246 crore. April–October gross collections reached about ₹14.78 lakh crore, up 13.2% (provisional data released on 1 November 2026, as reported by Deccan Herald and Free Press Journal). Like the September figures in the note below, these national gross figures include tax on imports and are not comparable with the state-wise domestic tables.
Updated 18 October 2026. From April 2026 tax devolution follows the 16th Finance Commission’s award for 2026-31, not the 15th’s, so the August 2026 instalment analysed below falls under the new award. According to published summaries (Policy Circle, Bank of Baroda), it kept the states’ share of the divisible pool at 41%, set Uttar Pradesh’s share at about 17.62%, which is the figure in that instalment rather than a deviation from a 15th Commission share, and added a “contribution to GDP” criterion with a 10% weight alongside income distance, population, demographic performance, forest and area. The criterion weights differ slightly between summaries; the redistributive pattern described below is unchanged. The original text below is unchanged.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Maharashtra gives 19.97% of matched GST but gets back 6.44% of devolution

What it means: Maharashtra's devolution share (6.44%) is well below its GST share (19.97%), while Uttar Pradesh's (17.62%) is about double its GST share (8.72%).

What to do: Check the full-year devolution figures against these instalment shares before treating the gap as settled.

National domestic GST fell 2.6% in FY2025-26, after growth every year before

What it means: Growth had already slowed each year, from 20.7% to 10.0%, before the 2025-26 reversal.

What to do: Compare later years against the 2025-26 level of ₹16.32 lakh crore before calling the dip a trend.

Uttar Pradesh's domestic GST fell 11.0% in 2025-26, the sharpest drop listed

What it means: Uttar Pradesh is also the single largest devolution recipient, so it takes the biggest share of tax back while losing ground on its own GST base.

What to do: Check Uttar Pradesh's FY2026-27 GST collections to see whether the decline has continued.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Finance should publish a consistent state-wise series of actual tax devolution for every year from FY2021-22, so devolution can be compared with five years of GST data on audited actuals rather than Budget Estimates and single instalments.
  • GSTN should publish IGST settlement to each state as a downloadable monthly series covering all years since GST began, so each state’s effective GST take — SGST plus settlement — can be tracked over time, not only for FY2025-26.
  • The Ministry of Finance should publish one annual state-wise statement of fiscal flows, setting SGST collected, IGST settled, tax devolution and grants side by side, so the debate over what states contribute and receive rests on a single official table rather than competing calculations.
  • The GST Council secretariat should publish a state-wise estimate of the revenue effect of the September 2025 rate rationalisation, so the 2.6 per cent fall in domestic collections in FY2025-26 can be separated into rate effects and underlying activity, state by state.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
