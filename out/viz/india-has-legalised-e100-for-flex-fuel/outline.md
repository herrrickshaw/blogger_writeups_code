G.S.R. 911(E)notified 23 October 2026: flex-fuel vehicles may now be certified to run on E100

+6%more per kilometre for E85 at Rs 82.12 than E20 petrol at Rs 102.12, by energy content

Rs 72a litre is where E100 matches petrol per km; ethanol already costs oil companies Rs 71.2 delivered

8%of petrol demand is all today’s unsold ethanol could move to E100

On 23 October the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways notified an amendment to the Central Motor Vehicles Rules that lets manufacturers certify flex-fuel vehicles to run on E100, pure ethanol, as well as E85. The rules apply only to vehicles designed for these fuels, and the ministry said no owner of an existing vehicle has to switch or modify anything. E85 went on sale in Delhi in June, and the first flex-fuel cars and motorcycles are being readied for launch.

This blog has looked at [how far India’s ethanol surplus stretches](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/10/indias-ethanol-surplus-buys-about-five.html) and [how Brazil sells E30 and E100 side by side](https://blog.vertistratsolution.com/2026/09/brazil-pumps-e30-at-every-station.html). This piece asks the two questions the new rule raises: would a driver save money on E100, and is there enough ethanol for a flex-fuel fleet?

Updated 9 November 2026. The final rules, G.S.R. 911(E), are dated 23 October 2026 and were published in the Gazette on 26 October (SCC Online’s summary of the notification). Besides putting E100 next to E85 in rule 115, they send E100 vehicles to a new Annexure SA and update the E10 and B10 references to E10/E20 and B100. Brazil’s regular petrol has carried a temporary 32% ethanol (E32) since 1 August 2026, not 30%. Delhi E20 petrol is still Rs 102.12 (6 November), so the per-kilometre arithmetic stands.

Updated 28 October 2026. Checked against the Gazette of India: the April 2026 draft (CG-DL-E-28042026-272125) proposed changing “(E85)” to “(E85) or (E100)” in the emission and certification tables of the Central Motor Vehicles Rules, with a separate annexure for E100 vehicles, as described here. A second draft, G.S.R. 634(E) of 13 July 2026 (CG-DL-E-27072026-274834), added a reference fuel for E100 and would require makers to state, on a sticker on each vehicle, whether it is compatible with E85 and E100. The final G.S.R. 911(E) is not in the index this blog used, so it is still described from press reports. No figure changed.

A litre of ethanol holds about 21.3 megajoules of energy against 32.4 for petrol, so a litre of E85 carries about 76% of the energy of a litre of E20, and a litre of E100 about 71%. A car needs correspondingly more litres to go the same distance. At Rs 82.12 a litre, E85 is 20% cheaper at the pump than E20 but about 6% dearer per kilometre. It would have to sell at about Rs 77.7 to break even.

Engines built for high-ethanol fuel can recover some of the gap, because ethanol’s high octane allows a higher compression ratio. If a flex-fuel engine were 5% more efficient on ethanol, the break-even prices would rise to about Rs 81.6 for E85 and Rs 75.7 for E100. That is a favourable case, and E85 at Rs 82.12 is still just above it.

E100 has a harder problem. To match E20 per kilometre at the same efficiency it would have to sell at about Rs 72.1 a litre. Oil companies already pay about Rs 71.2 a litre for ethanol delivered to their depots, including GST and freight. That leaves less than Rs 1 a litre for excise, VAT, the dealer’s commission and the oil company’s own costs. Petrol in Delhi carries about Rs 36 of excise and VAT. If E100 bore the same excise and VAT rate, it would cost about Rs 109 a litre, about 51% more per kilometre than petrol. Industry body SIAM has said E100 works only at about 30% below the price of petrol, which matches the energy arithmetic.

India’s distilleries offered 1,776 crore litres for the 2025-26 supply year, and about 576 crore litres had no buyer even after the fourth-quarter top-up. Moving a share of petrol demand to E100 needs about 1.4 litres of ethanol for every litre of E20 it replaces, less the 20% ethanol those vehicles already burn. On the 2025-26 petrol pool, the unsold surplus would cover flex-fuel vehicles taking about 8% of petrol demand. A 10% share would need about 731 crore litres more.

The same litres are already spoken for. This blog’s central forecast puts the ethanol needed for E30 alone at 2,221 crore litres in 2029-30, more than everything offered this year. Every litre that goes into a flex-fuel tank is a litre that cannot raise the blend in ordinary petrol, which every car on the road uses.

## What Brazil’s flex-fuel fleet shows

  r2: Ethanol share of car-fuel energy | 14% at E20; 22% at E30 | About 40% in 2025 (24% from pure hydrous ethanol)
  r3: High-ethanol fuel price vs petrol | E85 at 80% of E20; needs about 76% to match per km | Hydrous at 68.57% of gasoline on average in 2025; drivers switch when it is above about 70%
  r4: Tax rule | No rule that ethanol fuels be taxed below petrol | Constitutional Amendment 123/2022 requires biofuels to be taxed below fossil fuels for 20 years


Brazil is the proof that flex-fuel cars work at scale: about 82% of its light vehicles can run on any mix of petrol and ethanol, and ethanol supplied about 40% of the energy its cars used in 2025, by this blog’s arithmetic from ANP sales data. But it also shows that a flex-fuel fleet does not guarantee ethanol is used. Drivers fill with hydrous ethanol when it costs less than about 70% of the petrol price, and switch back when it does not. The national ratio averaged 68.57% in 2025, and hydrous ethanol sales fell 2.3% that year to 21.24 billion litres; in the first three months of 2026 the ratio was above 72%.

What keeps Brazilian ethanol competitive is tax. Since a 2022 constitutional amendment, Brazil must tax biofuels less than the fossil fuels they replace, and keep that gap for 20 years; São Paulo, for example, charged 13.3% state tax on hydrous ethanol against 25% on petrol. India has no equivalent rule, and the E100 notification says nothing about price.
