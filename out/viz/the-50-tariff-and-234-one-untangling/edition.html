In August 2025 the US tariff on Indian goods jumped to 50% overnight, and Walmart, Amazon, Target and Gap all paused new India orders within days. Thirteen months and three separate legal mechanisms later, that number is gone: the Supreme Court ruled in February 2026 that the emergency law behind it never authorized tariffs at all, a replacement global tariff expired by statute in July, and what India's exports actually face today, as this piece is published, is a 10% duty under a different statute entirely — tied for the lowest rate among its main apparel-and-footwear rivals, not the highest. Running underneath all of that, mostly untouched by any of it, is a completely different and much older mechanism: antidumping and countervailing duties (AD/CVD), which have separately put a combined duty of roughly 234% on Indian solar cells while leaving Indian shrimp at under 10%. This piece traces both threads — why one blanket-tariff vehicle after another kept getting struck down while AD/CVD sailed on undisturbed, and how the whole sequence reached a retailer as large as Walmart. Several of the process explainers this piece drew on could not be fetched directly during its research, though KPMG's own report on the August 2025 tariff shift was read in full; every unreachable source is flagged where it matters, and summarised at the end.

What would fix this

  • The Department of Commerce should publish an HS-line list of Indian exports covered by and exempt from the US Section 301 duty, with FY25 export values, so that its statement that the duty applies to 60–65 per cent of exports while 45 per cent are exempt can be reconciled.
  • The Department of Commerce should publish monthly India-to-US export data by sector for August 2025 onward, marking which goods were exempt under each tariff regime, so that the analyst estimates of a 40–50 per cent fall in order volume can be tested against measured trade rather than left as projections.
  • The Directorate General of Foreign Trade should maintain a public tracker of every US antidumping and countervailing order on Indian products, with current rates by named exporter and next review date, so that exporters can see per-company margins such as shrimp's without assembling Federal Register notices themselves.
  • The Department of Commerce, with the export promotion councils, should publish guidance for Indian suppliers who absorbed US tariff costs through renegotiated FOB prices, explaining that refunds after the IEEPA ruling go to US importers of record, so that exporters can address this in contract terms for any future blanket tariff.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. This piece's research could not directly fetch six of the specific pages behind its own account of AD/CVD process, WTO framework and mitigation strategy — USITC's antidumping/countervailing overview (usitc.gov), CBP's AD/CVD priority-issue page (cbp.gov), Roanoke's importer's-guide whitepaper, Purolator International's customs explainer, the WTO's own Tariff Analysis Online help page (wto.org), and NetSuite's tariff-mitigation-strategies article — all returned blocked-egress errors on direct fetch; every account of their content above was reconstructed from search-engine summaries and other reporting that independently describes the same source pages, not a direct read of the documents themselves. KPMG's PDF report was the one exception: it was supplied directly and read in full, so the sector-exposure table, the transshipment-surcharge detail and the GDP-proportionality figure in Section 3 come from a direct reading of that document, not a reconstruction — though it's worth noting KPMG's own report is dated September 2025 and describes the picture as of 26 August 2025, so it is itself a snapshot of the tariff regime's opening weeks, not of anything after. A PIIE chart tracking the 2018–2019 US-China tariff timeline, offered as background during this piece's research, could not be fetched for the same reason and is not cited above. The 30–35% cost-increase and 40–50% order-volume-decline figures in Section 4 are analyst estimates reported in August 2025, not measured outcomes confirmed in subsequent trade data; this piece did not locate a later trade-data release that would confirm or disprove them, and it separately could not confirm whether Walmart resumed or expanded India orders after the rate fell to 10% in July 2026 — both are left as open questions rather than assumed answers. AD/CVD margins in Section 2, especially the solar figures, are described as preliminary or as the result of a specific administrative-review cycle; nearly all AD/CVD rates in this piece are subject to further revision in future annual reviews, and none should be read as a permanently fixed number. On the legal history in Section 3: this piece describes the Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling, the Section 122 tariff's subsequent Court of International Trade rejection and statutory expiry, and the Section 301 tariff that replaced it, based on contemporaneous legal-alert and trade-press coverage rather than a full independent reading of the underlying court opinions; readers relying on this for compliance decisions should confirm current status directly, since this is an area that has changed materially three times in thirteen months and could change again. The US-Canada trade dispute in Section 6 is similarly fluid: talks collapsed 21 August 2026, tariffs and counter-tariffs followed through early September, and President Trump said on 12 September 2026 — the day this piece was finalized — that a new deal could come "fairly soon," with no signed agreement or announced terms as of that date. Anything after 12 September 2026, on either the India or Canada threads, should be checked against current reporting rather than assumed to still hold. Finally, this piece does not take a position on whether any tariff or AD/CVD order discussed above is fair, proportionate, or good policy — it describes the mechanisms and their reported effects, not their merits.
