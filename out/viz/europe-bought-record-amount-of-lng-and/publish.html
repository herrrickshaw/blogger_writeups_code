The EU imported 146 bcm of LNG in 2025, overtaking China and Japan to become the world’s largest buyer. Nearly three-fifths of it came from the United States. That is not diversification — it is one dependency exchanged for another, on a market where Europe now buys a third of its volume at spot and a chokepoint 6,000 km away can move the price.

EU LNG imports: a record 146 bcm in 2025, nearly three-fifths from the US

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 146 bcm of LNG entered the EU in 2025, a record, and 58% of it came from the United States.
  • 95.1% utilisation at Dahej in FY2023-24, against a national average of 50.5% across eight terminals.
Updated 7 November 2026. The dependence described below deepened in 2026. The United States supplied 63% of European LNG imports in January–March 2026, up from 57% a year earlier (IEEFA), and IEEFA projects about two-thirds for the year as a whole. EU gas storage was about 75% full on 21 October 2026, low for the time of year, after the Hormuz disruption tightened global supply. Sources: IEEFA European LNG Tracker, May 2026, Euronews (two-thirds projection), Global LNG Hub (storage, search summary only).
Updated 26 October 2026. Petronet LNG commissioned the Dahej expansion from 17.5 to 22.5 Mtpa on 31 March 2026, as this blog’s LNG terminal piece records; the text below described it as under construction. The FY2023-24 table keeps Dahej at 17.5 Mtpa, its capacity in that year.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

France, the Netherlands and Spain took most of the EU's LNG imports in 2025

What it means: France was the largest EU importer in 2025, ahead of the Netherlands at 24 bcm and Spain at 23 bcm.

What to do: Track Belgium and Italy, which added the most volume in 2025, against the refill need.

One supplier, the United States, provided 58% of EU LNG imports in 2025

What it means: ACER reports the United States at 58% of EU LNG imports, roughly a quarter of all EU gas consumption.

What to do: Diversify supply before the end of 2026 Russian LNG cutoff, because new capacity only lands in 2029 to 2031.

India's LNG capacity is one terminal running flat out, Dahej at 95%

What it means: Across all eight terminals India ran at 50.5% in FY2023-24, with Dahej at 95.1% and most others far lower.

What to do: Tie new regas capacity to pipelines and demand centres, as PNGRB advises, before adding more terminals.

What would fix this

  • PNGRB should publish terminal-by-terminal LNG regasification capacity and utilisation in an annual statistical release, instead of only in section 4.2 of a demand-projection study two fiscal years behind, so that a Dahej at 95% and five terminals near a quarter of nameplate are visible every year.
  • The Petroleum Planning & Analysis Cell should publish monthly LNG import volumes and landed prices by terminal alongside its national totals, so that the price-against-domestic-gas constraint this post identifies can be read terminal by terminal rather than only as an all-India average.
  • PNGRB should make authorisation of new or expanded regasification capacity, such as Jafrabad and the planned Dabhol expansion, conditional on a published evacuation-pipeline and offtake plan, since Kochi and Ennore have never averaged above 13% utilisation without one.
  • The European Commission should publish a quarterly single-supplier concentration indicator for EU LNG imports, alongside a stated ceiling it intends to monitor against, so that the rise of the US share above 58% after Russian LNG ends in 2026 is tracked against a declared benchmark.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
