India's draft steel policy: 604 Mt by 2047, blast furnaces keep 43–57% of output

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 604 Mt of crude steel capacity targeted for 2047, up from 220 Mt in 2025-26, a pace close to recent growth.
  • 35% cut in blast-furnace emissions per tonne needed to reach the draft's 1.54 t CO2 goal.
  • 236 Mt of coking coal the draft expects to need in 2047, against 72 Mt now.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

What it means: Capacity rose by about 20 Mt a year over the last three years, and the draft's 2047 target needs about 18 Mt a year.

What to do: Watch capacity utilisation, which slipped to about 77% in 2025-26 as capacity grew faster than output.

What it means: The draft's 236 Mt of coking coal supports 229 to 303 Mt of blast-furnace steel, or 43 to 57% of the 529 Mt needed.

What to do: Plan for coking coal imports rising to about 3.3 times today's level, through long-term contracts and domestic mining.

Blast-furnace emissions must fall about 35% for the 1.54 t goal

What it means: Even with all other steel gas- or scrap-based, blast furnaces would have to emit 1.66 to 1.68 t against 2.5 to 2.7 t today.

What to do: Fund carbon capture, hydrogen or gas injection, or biomass for blast furnaces, none of which is yet in commercial use at scale in India.

What would fix this

  • The final policy should publish the route mix behind its coal, ore and scrap projections (blast furnace, coal DRI, gas DRI, scrap), so the forecasts can be checked against each other and against the emissions target.
  • It should set an emissions ceiling per tonne for new blast-furnace capacity approved after 2030, or require new furnaces to be built ready for carbon capture or hydrogen injection.
  • The Ministry should publish emissions per tonne by plant, verified to the standard the EU accepts, so exporters avoid default CBAM values.
  • The Viksit Bharat Ispat scheme should pay against measured emissions per tonne, verified independently, rather than on reported technology upgrades.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

The draft policy document could not be opened directly; its figures come from news reports and summaries, which give coking-coal demand as 236 Mt (most) or 214 Mt (one summary). The draft’s own assumptions about routes and utilisation were not found. Route shares for 2025-26 are not yet published; this piece uses BigMint’s 41% blast-furnace share for 2024-25. The emission factors by route are industry ranges, not plant data, and the coal per tonne is calibrated from the draft’s current-demand figure, which may include coal used outside steelmaking. Capacity figures for 2018-19 and 2019-20 could not be confirmed and are not shown.
