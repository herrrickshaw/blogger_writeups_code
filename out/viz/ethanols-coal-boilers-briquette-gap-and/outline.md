Two distillery types, two fuel stories. India's ~380 ethanol distilleries split into two feedstock lines: sugarcane/molasses-based, which are mostly co-located with sugar mills, and grain-based (maize and rice), the segment driving most of the recent capacity growth because it isn't tied to the cane season. That split maps almost directly onto what fuels their boilers — and it's the reason "are distilleries running on fossil fuels" doesn't have one answer.

## 1. The boiler-fuel split that answers the original question

Sugar-integrated distilleries generally don't need to buy fossil fuel for process heat at all: bagasse, the fibrous cane residue left over from crushing, is their own by-product, available on-site at low or no cost, and is standard boiler fuel across the industry. A growing number of these plants also run anaerobic digesters on their spent wash (the distillery's liquid effluent), producing biogas that further offsets coal or diesel use. For a cane-linked distillery, biomass isn't an aspiration — it's already the default fuel.

Standalone grain-based distilleries are the different case. They don't produce bagasse, so their boiler-fuel choice is a genuine, open decision between rice husk, purchased biomass, or coal — and coal is documented as one of the fuels actually in use, alongside furnace oil, at plants that haven't built out a biomass supply chain. This is the segment where the question in the original ask actually bites: it's real distilleries, running on fossil fuel, for lack of an on-site alternative rather than by design.

There's already a policy handle for this — just not a mandate. MNRE's National Bioenergy Programme (2021–2026) offers central financial assistance for non-bagasse biomass cogeneration projects, and explicitly clarifies that ethanol distilleries are eligible to claim it (up to ₹40 lakh per MW installed, capped at ₹5 crore per project). That's real money on the table for a grain distillery to switch its boiler from coal to biomass briquettes. What doesn't exist is a SAMARTH-style percentage mandate forcing that switch — the coal co-firing rule described below applies to thermal power plants, not distilleries.

## 2. The coal co-firing precedent — and its real, currently unmet supply gap

The direct analogue the original question points to is real and already running. The Ministry of Power's revised biomass policy, issued 16 June 2023, mandates 5% biomass co-firing (agri-residue pellets blended with coal) in coal-based thermal power plants from FY 2024-25, rising to 7% from FY 2025-26 — administered through the SAMARTH Mission. A comprehensive policy issued in November 2025 replaced that path: from FY 2025-26 plants must co-fire 5% biomass pellets, and plants in the National Capital Region 5% pellets plus 2% torrefied charcoal made from municipal solid waste. By 31 December 2025, 72 thermal plants (11 of them in the NCR) had co-fired about 40.2 lakh tonnes of biomass cumulatively, 24.76 lakh tonnes of it in calendar 2025, according to the Ministry of Power’s annual report for 2025-26. Farmers selling crop residue into this supply chain, instead of burning it in the field, can earn an estimated ₹3,000–6,000 per acre, a benefit government estimates put at potentially reaching 10–20 million farmers and injecting ₹6,000–24,000 crore a year into the rural economy if fully realised.

"Fully realised" is the operative phrase. As on 23 December 2025, the Commission for Air Quality Management (CAQM) imposed ₹61.85 crore in environmental compensation on six thermal power plants within 300 km of Delhi for missing their co-firing thresholds under the Environmental (Utilisation of Crop Residue by Thermal Power Plants) Rules, 2023 — the plants had until 15 April 2026 to pay up and prove compliance. The reason the shortfall is happening at scale, not just at six plants, is a straightforward supply-demand mismatch: national biomass pellet/briquette production runs around 2.5 million tonnes a year, against an estimated 15–20 million tonnes needed to meet the current mandated co-firing rate across all obligated plants. The official co-firing figure for 2025, about 2.5 million tonnes, is consistent with that production estimate.

That 12–17 million tonne gap between what's produced and what's mandated isn't a policy failure to route around — it's the opportunity the original question is asking about, already quantified, already enforced with real penalties, and still unmet.

Whether that same supply-side opportunity extends to grain distilleries' own boilers depends on whether MNRE's existing cogeneration subsidy (Section 1, above) gets taken up at scale, or whether a coal-co-firing-style mandate eventually gets extended to distillery boilers specifically. Neither this piece nor the sourcing behind it found evidence that such an extension is currently proposed.

## 3. The actual bottleneck is aggregation machinery, not raw biomass

The supply gap in Section 2 isn't a shortage of crop residue in the field — India's Ministry of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare frames the underlying problem the opposite way: "a large portion of the paddy straw/crop residues are burnt in the field," precisely because no one collects it before the next sowing window closes. Burning one tonne of paddy straw releases 3 kg of particulate matter, 60 kg of CO, 1,460 kg of CO₂, 199 kg of ash and 2 kg of SO₂ — and destroys 5.5 kg of nitrogen, 2.3 kg of P₂O₅, 25 kg of K₂O and 400 kg of carbon per tonne that would otherwise return to the soil. The residue exists; what's missing between the field and the pellet mill is machinery to bale it, transport it and store it before it either rots or gets burned.

India runs a real, current scheme for exactly this gap. The Ministry of Agriculture's Crop Residue Management (CRM) programme — under Rashtriya Krishi Vikas Yojana, currently covering Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and NCT of Delhi, with Centre:State funding at 60:40 (100:0 for Delhi) — funds three distinct layers of this problem under its 2026 operational guidelines.

The supply-chain component's own stated objectives include "assured procurement of paddy straw from farmers by the paddy straw aggregators" and to "ensure increase in income (both from the produce and later from the sale of paddy residue) for farmers" — the same farmer-income mechanism the coal co-firing mandate runs on, applied one layer upstream, at the collection stage rather than the power-plant gate.

A separate, SAMARTH-administered scheme — Biomass Aggregation Machinery (BAM) — funds the tractors, balers and rakers used specifically to supply co-firing pellet plants, with a three-way cost split: roughly 65% government, 25% industry (the pellet manufacturer or offtaker), and 10% from the farmer-producer organisation itself, with indicative aggregation-unit costs running ₹1-1.8 crore depending on seasonal paddy-straw throughput. That the government is funding the majority share of the machinery, rather than the pellets or the boilers, is itself the tell: the bottleneck long identified in this sector is logistics, not chemistry.

Punjab's own 2025 numbers show the model working at state scale, even if not yet closing the national gap: ₹395 crore disbursed for CRM machinery translated into a reported 53% drop in Kharif-season stubble-burning incidents, from 10,909 in 2024 to 5,114 in 2025, across 5,358 beneficiaries — a mix of individual farmers (4,181) and CHCs, panchayats and supply-chain entities. That's real, state-level progress on the collection side of the problem; it's also one state, and the CAQM enforcement action in Section 2 shows the national pellet shortfall persisting regardless.

## 4. The carbon-credit layer distilleries can already claim — and the one they mostly can't yet

Separate from the biomass-supply question, a distillery that does switch its boiler from coal to biomass has an existing route to monetise that switch as a carbon credit. The Bureau of Energy Efficiency's methodology BM IN02.001, "Energy efficiency and fuel switching measures," is built for exactly this: industrial facilities, including distilleries, that aren't obligated entities under India's Perform, Achieve and Trade scheme can claim credit for the fuel-switch abatement directly. Distillery-side estimates put biomass-fired process steam at roughly 50 gCO₂e/MJ against roughly 70 gCO₂e/MJ for coal-fired steam (figures reflect working sourcing, not an independently verified primary document) — call it a 20 gCO₂e/MJ spread that's the real, creditable abatement per unit of steam, on top of whatever farmer-income effect the briquette purchase itself has.

The BM IN02.001 methodology and its scope (non-obligated industrial facilities, including distilleries) is confirmed against the Bureau of Energy Efficiency's own published methodology document. The specific carbon-intensity figures (50 vs 70 gCO₂e/MJ), the CBG methodology reference, and the claim that fermentation-CO₂ and 2G-ethanol pathways currently lack an issued methodology reflect this piece's working sourcing rather than a document this piece independently retrieved and checked line by line; see the caveat below.

Of the two uncredited pathways, fermentation CO₂ capture is the more consequential gap. Ethanol fermentation produces a stream of CO₂ that's already 99.9% pure before any processing — the cheapest capture point of any major industrial CO₂ source, because there's no separation step to pay for. A peer-reviewed cost analysis of the pathway (corn ethanol, US context) puts capture-and-storage at a levelised cost of roughly $52 per tonne of CO₂e abated, delivering about a 58% reduction in the fuel's life-cycle carbon intensity. That the pathway isn't yet creditable in India isn't a technology problem — it's a missing methodology, which is a different, and in principle faster, thing to fix than the biomass-supply gap in Section 2.

## 5. What America's own ethanol-CCS record actually shows

The Global CCS Institute's Global Status of CCS 2025 report (published 9 October 2025, titled "Staying the Course") counts 77 CCS projects operating worldwide and 47 under construction, with capture capacity growing at a compound annual rate of over 30% since 2017 across all sectors. Within that, the report specifically flags seven new biofuel and bioenergy CCS projects that reached construction in the year covered — ethanol is the largest single contributor to that bioenergy category, but the Institute's own public reporting doesn't break out an ethanol-only project count or capacity figure from the broader bioenergy total.

The flagship named project is more informative than the aggregate count. ADM's Illinois Industrial Carbon Capture and Storage project at its Decatur ethanol plant — the first commercial-scale CCS deployment at any ethanol facility, storing CO₂ over 2 km deep in the Mount Simon Sandstone formation — was built and permitted against a stated target of storing over 1 million tonnes of CO₂ a year. Since operations began in 2017, EPA-reported figures show the project actually storing roughly 428,580 to 525,002 tonnes a year — consistently under half its stated target, cumulatively exceeding 3.5 million tonnes stored by 2022. That's a real, operating, independently-monitored project; it's also a documented case of "commercial-scale CCS" undershooting its own headline number for the better part of a decade.

The bigger, newer US project has run into something the technology can't fix: politics. Summit Carbon Solutions' pipeline — designed to gather CO₂ from ethanol plants across Iowa, Nebraska, Minnesota, South Dakota and North Dakota for underground sequestration in North Dakota — is the largest attempt yet to scale ethanol CCS as shared infrastructure rather than one plant at a time. In March 2025, South Dakota's legislature passed a law banning the use of eminent domain for CO₂ pipelines, a direct blow to the project's routing. By May 2026, Summit had dropped four ethanol plants and roughly 200 miles of route, while continuing to work with a remaining group of 27 Iowa plants, and announced a route shift sending captured CO₂ through Nebraska to Wyoming — for enhanced oil recovery or sequestration there, not the original North Dakota storage plan. A project conceived as ethanol decarbonisation infrastructure is now, in part, a CO₂ supply line for oil recovery.

The federal policy backdrop is mixed rather than uniformly supportive. Section 45Q, the US tax credit that underwrites most CCS project economics, survived intact when Congress passed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act in July 2025 — but the same period saw the Department of Energy cancel $3.7 billion in previously awarded decarbonisation grants, including CCS demonstration funding. Read together with ADM's decade of underperformance and Summit's routing collapse, the honest summary of "how is the US ethanol industry doing on CCS" is: the tax incentive held, the project pipeline is still growing in aggregate, and the two most prominent named projects have each run into a different kind of real-world limit — one operational, one political — well short of their original billing.
