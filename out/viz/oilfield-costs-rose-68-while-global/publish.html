S&P Global's oilfield cost indexes say building and running an oil or gas project got roughly 6–8% more expensive over the past year. The same year, the industry spent less money doing it — global upstream capital spending is falling for a second straight year running. India's own oil and gas public-sector companies just posted their first first-quarter capex decline in at least five years. None of this is a coincidence; it's the same cost squeeze showing up in three different data sets.

Top line

  • 6.5% to 8% rise in upstream capital costs in S&P Global's latest reports, while global spending falls.
  • $420 billion of global upstream spending in 2025, roughly 30% below the $600 billion needed each year.
  • 3.8% fall in combined Q1 FY27 capex across 12 Indian oil and gas PSUs, the first Q1 drop in at least five years.
Updated 7 November 2026. Two things have changed since this piece was written. First, oil is no longer cheap: the US Energy Information Administration says Brent averaged $114 a barrel in September 2026, after disruption to Middle East supply, and its October outlook puts the 2026 average at $96 (it was $69 in 2025). The “sub-$60 oil” and “Brent around $60 in 2026” framing below reflects forecasts made before that, so the contrast this piece draws between rising costs and falling prices no longer holds for the second half of 2026. Second, Baker Hughes’ September 2026 count puts the Middle East at 508 rigs and Asia-Pacific at 205 (June: 488 and 206); the chart below still shows June. No newer S&P Global cost-index reading was found.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

Global upstream spending fell to $420 billion, roughly 30% below need

What it means: Spending fell for a second year running, leaving about $180 billion a year short of the $600 billion S&P Global says is needed.

What to do: Track whether 2026 spending falls the further 2–3% forecast, which would deepen the gap.

Indian Oil, ONGC and Oil India each cut Q1 capex by 16% or more

What it means: The 12-PSU total fell only 3.8%, which hides steeper company-level cuts behind the first Q1 decline in at least five years.

What to do: Track the next quarter's PSU capex to test the experts' view that this dip is a one-off.

Middle East onshore breaks even at $27 a barrel, the cheapest tracked

What it means: A separate report puts India's domestic breakeven at roughly $35–45 a barrel, close to deepwater and shale, though it uses a different method.

What to do: Read any 2026 Brent forecast as one analyst's view, since the post's own sources disagree on it.

Asia-Pacific has 206 active rigs against 488 in the Middle East

What it means: A thinner rig fleet leaves Asia-Pacific fewer projects across which to spread the same cost inflation.

What to do: Compare future Asia-Pacific and Middle East rig counts to see whether regional drilling activity recovers.

What would fix this

  • The Directorate General of Hydrocarbons should publish a monthly count of active drilling rigs in India, split onshore and offshore and by operator, so India’s share of Asia-Pacific’s 206 rigs is an official figure rather than a gap in regional totals from Baker Hughes.
  • PPAC should publish its quarterly capital-expenditure figures for the twelve oil and gas PSUs on its own website, company by company and split across exploration and production, gas transport, and refining and marketing, so a fall like Q1 FY27’s 3.8% can be read from the source rather than from press reports of provisional data.
  • The Directorate General of Hydrocarbons should publish an annual estimate of average development and operating cost per barrel for domestic fields, by onshore, shallow-water and deepwater, so India’s position on the cost curve rests on an official series rather than a single uncorroborated $35–45 range.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. The UCCI/UOCI percentage increases cited here (roughly 6.5% to 8%) come from two overlapping but not identical S&P Global reporting windows, and this piece was unable to directly access S&P Global's own primary report pages (spglobal.com returned an access-denied response from this environment) to confirm a single, precise, current figure — both numbers are reported via independent secondary summaries of S&P Global's research, graded moderate confidence. The North American Cost Index (NACI) is named in S&P Global's own product materials but this piece could not independently confirm a detailed, current methodology for it specifically. The India domestic breakeven figure ($35–45/barrel) and its accompanying $75–85/barrel Brent assumption come from a single source not independently cross-checked, and are flagged above as inconsistent with S&P Global's separate $60/barrel 2026 Brent outlook. Baker Hughes' rig-count figures are regional totals only; a country-level Asia-Pacific breakdown (India, China, Indonesia, Malaysia) was not available in the sources reviewed for this piece. The $431bn (2024) and $407–410bn (2026) capex figures are this piece's own derived arithmetic from S&P Global's stated percentage changes, not directly cited index numbers.
