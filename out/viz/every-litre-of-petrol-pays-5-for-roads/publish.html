Every litre of petrol sold in India carries ₹5 of Road and Infrastructure Cess, and every litre of diesel ₹2 — the part of the Centre's fuel tax earmarked by law for roads and infrastructure. When the Centre cut excise by ₹10 a litre in March 2026, it left that cess untouched, so it is now 42% of what the Centre takes on a litre of petrol. Electric vehicles pay none of it, and none of the rest of the fuel tax either. Today that costs the road fund less than one per cent of its income. The question is what replaces it before it costs much more.

Road and Infrastructure Cess: ₹5 a litre on petrol, ₹2 on diesel, none on EVs

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • ₹5 a litre of petrol goes to the road cess, fixed since May 2022; an electric car pays none of it.
  • ₹70,278 cr of national highway tolls in 2025–26, more than the road cess; electric cars pay them, two-wheelers do not.
  • 1,700 km of highway a year is what the budgeted cess buys at average project cost.

Updated 15 November 2026: rewritten as charts; figures unchanged.

The cess buys about 1,700 km of highway a year at average project cost

What it means: Costlier projects buy far fewer kilometres from the same cess, so the average figure hides a tenfold spread.

What to do: Audit the cess transfers the CAG has flagged, since the cess is earmarked in law but loosely in practice.

Each electric vehicle avoids the road cess its fuel-burning twin pays

What it means: The post estimates each electric vehicle avoids the road cess that its petrol or diesel twin pays every year.

What to do: Price electric vehicles' road use against what petrol and diesel vehicles pay, while meeting the privacy objection that stopped satellite tolling.

National highway tolls more than doubled in four years, now above the road cess

What it means: Tolls charge per plaza crossing, so electric cars already pay them while two-wheelers do not, and tolls now outweigh the road cess.

What to do: Revisit satellite tolling with a privacy answer, since per-crossing tolls still charge for a point on the road, not for distance.

What would fix this

  • The Finance Ministry should publish road-cess collections and actual transfers to the Central Road and Infrastructure Fund side by side every year, and transfer the full amount — the step CAG has asked for since 2010–11 — so the cess either funds roads in full or is honestly treated as general revenue.
  • The Budget's statement of revenue foregone should include an annual estimate of fuel excise and road cess not collected from electric vehicles, by segment, so the size of the gap is measured by the government before it becomes large, rather than by think tanks after.
  • The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways should pilot a per-kilometre road charge for heavy electric vehicles — buses and trucks — on access-controlled expressways, which already toll by entry and exit, set at roughly the road cess a diesel vehicle pays per kilometre (about 57 paise for a bus at 3.5 km a litre), while light EVs stay exempt during the adoption phase. Publishing the data-retention and access rules its own committees asked about would let satellite tolling be reconsidered for the wider network.
  • States should replace open-ended EV road-tax waivers with published phase-downs tied to EV share of new registrations, so owners and manufacturers know when concessions end, rather than facing an abrupt reversal like Karnataka's.
  • The road transport ministry should publish national highway toll collections by fuel type each year, which linking FASTag accounts to Vahan registration records already makes possible, so that what electric vehicles pay in tolls can be set against what they do not pay in fuel tax.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
