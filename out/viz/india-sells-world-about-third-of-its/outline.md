## Why an extract is worth ten times the spice

An oleoresin is made by soaking ground spice in a solvent — hexane, acetone or ethanol, or supercritical carbon dioxide — and then stripping the solvent off, leaving the oils, resins and pigments that give the spice its colour, pungency and smell. Ten kilos of black pepper yield roughly one to 1.3 kilos of oleoresin; turmeric gives 8–10%, ginger 3–8%. What the buyer gets in exchange is standardisation: a sausage maker or snack company can dose colour or heat to a specified strength every time, without dust, microbial load or batch-to-batch variation in the raw spice. Food and drink take just over half of world demand; animal feed, pharmaceuticals and cosmetics take the rest.

India's export statistics show what that concentration is worth. The Spices Board's item-wise table, compiled from DGCI&S data, has carried "spice oils and oleoresins" as a single line for years. It is one of the smallest lines by weight and one of the largest by value.

## How big the market is, and India's share of it

There is no official count of world oleoresin trade, so the size of the market comes from market-research firms. Fortune Business Insights puts it at US$1.64 billion in 2025, rising to US$1.72 billion in 2026 and US$2.6 billion by 2034, a 5.3% annual growth rate. It gives Europe the largest regional share (30.6% in 2025) and Asia-Pacific 26.3%, and ranks paprika the biggest product type at 34.2% of the market in 2026. IMARC (US$1.6 billion in 2024) and Mordor Intelligence (US$1.44 billion in 2025) are in the same range. These are commercial estimates with undisclosed methods, not measured data.

Set against those estimates, India's roughly US$536 million of exports is about a third of the world market — consistent with the Dutch government's export-promotion agency CBI, which says India "controls more than 30% of the global market" and was the largest supplier of oleoresins to Europe by volume, providing 41% of Germany's imports, 47% of the Netherlands' and a quarter of Spain's in 2021. The figure often repeated in trade media, that India makes 70% of the world's spice oleoresins, has no primary source this piece could find and appears to confuse oleoresins with spice oils; it is not used here.

## Who makes it

The industry is concentrated in Kerala and led by a handful of firms. CRISIL's December 2024 rating rationale puts Synthite Industries of Kochi at 45–50% of the domestic market and 30–40% of the global one. That is close to the one-third share estimated above for India as a whole, which suggests either that Synthite's plants outside India count toward its figure or that the two estimates don't line up; treat both as approximate. Two of the five biggest players are now controlled by European groups.

## An export built partly on imported spice

A good share of the raw material is not Indian. Under the Directorate General of Foreign Trade's advance authorisation scheme, extractors can import pepper, cardamom and chillies duty-free to make oils and oleoresins for export, provided they add at least 15% in value (a June 2024 public notice set 25% only for spice-to-spice cases). Oleoresin makers prefer immature Sri Lankan and Vietnamese pepper, which yields more extract. Plant Lipids' credit rating says imports are 40–50% of its purchases. UN Comtrade data compiled by a trade-data provider show India importing about 52,000 tonnes of pepper in 2024, 55% of it from Sri Lanka — more than twice the 20,830 tonnes of pepper it exported in 2024–25. The different periods and the secondary source make that a rough comparison, but the direction is clear: part of India's oleoresin export is value added in Kerala to spice grown elsewhere.

## The US case

On 25 June 2025, Rezolex, a paprika-extract maker in Las Cruces, New Mexico, petitioned for anti-dumping and countervailing (anti-subsidy) duties on oleoresin paprika from India alone — covering extract of at least 500 ASTA colour units. US imports of the product from India were about US$56 million in 2024, roughly a tenth of India's total oleoresin exports. The Commerce Department issued preliminary subsidy findings in February 2026 and preliminary dumping findings in April, and published its final determinations on 21 August 2026.

It is the third time this has been tried: a 1979 case against Indian oleoresins ended with no injury found, and a 2001 petition from the same company, Rezolex, failed 6–0 at the preliminary stage. What changed this time is visible in the table. The dumping margins are modest — about 4–6%. Most of the duty is the subsidy rate, which means the case turned mainly on Indian government support to exporters rather than on below-cost pricing. Which Indian schemes Commerce counted, and at what rate, is set out in its issues-and-decision memorandum, which this piece was not able to read; that matters beyond paprika, because the same export-support schemes back many other Indian exports.

The dumping margins were 4–6%. The subsidy rates were 19–25%. The US case against Indian oleoresin was mostly a case against Indian export support.

## Europe: an easing, and a standing problem

Europe, the biggest regional market, has just relaxed one control. Since January 2022 each consignment of Indian pepper and dried capsicum needed a pre-export laboratory certificate because of ethylene oxide contamination; an EU implementing regulation of 28 January 2026 moved both to the lighter regime of random border checks at a 20% frequency. The standing problem is the record. A peer-reviewed study of the EU's food-safety alert system from 2000 to 2022 found India the most-notified origin for herbs and spices, at 23.6% of 3,741 alerts, with Sudan dyes and ethylene oxide among the leading hazards. Since 2022 the EU's 0.1 mg/kg ethylene oxide limit applies to all food additives, which covers paprika extract (E160c) and curcumin (E100) directly, and EU and US rules cap residues of extraction solvents such as hexane and acetone at tens of parts per million.
