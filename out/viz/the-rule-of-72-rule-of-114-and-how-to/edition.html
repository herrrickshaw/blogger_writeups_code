Every fixed-deposit ad, PPF brochure and mutual-fund pitch in India comes with a number attached — 7.1%, 12% CAGR, “doubles in 5 years.” Most people either trust the number blindly or can't do anything with it beyond that. The Rule of 72 (and its less-famous siblings, the Rule of 114 and the Rule of 144) turn any stated interest rate into a doubling, tripling or quadrupling time in your head, in seconds — and knowing where these shortcuts come from is what lets you catch the difference between an honest number and a marketing one.

Rule of 72 and Rule of 114: turning an interest rate into doubling and tripling times

Skip to article content

Top line

  • 10.1 yrs for a PPF deposit at 7.1% to double, by the Rule of 72.
  • 34 yrs to double in real terms once roughly 5% inflation is taken off, not 10.
  • 0.04 year error at PPF's 7.1%, inside the band where the rule is most accurate.

What it means: The rule overstates doubling time at low rates and understates it above about 8%, so it is a rough check rather than an exact figure.

What to do: Use the exact formula n = ln(2) / ln(1 + r) whenever a projection will be relied on.

What it means: Quadrupling is simply doubling twice, so the 144 rule is exactly twice the 72 rule at every rate.

What to do: Read the 114 and 144 columns as rough guides, since both constants are rounded up from their exact values.

After inflation, the PPF's real return of about 2.1% takes 34 years to double

What it means: Once inflation is subtracted, the PPF's growth in purchasing power is far slower than the headline rate suggests.

What to do: Test any projection against the real rate, not only the headline return.

What would fix this

  • SEBI should require any investment communication by a registered intermediary that quotes a doubling or tripling time to state the implied annual compounded rate alongside it, so that a claim like “doubles in 4 years” is always shown as the 18% a year it actually promises.
  • AMFI should require that SIP return illustrations state they use an annuity-based calculation and never quote a lump-sum doubling time for recurring instalments, since the Rule of 72 overstates how fast a SIP doubles and that is the commonest misuse of these shortcuts.
  • The Department of Economic Affairs should keep a single dated web page carrying every small-savings interest-rate notification, with gazette text and the full rate history for each scheme, so that a figure like PPF’s 7.1% since April 2020 can be checked against the notification rather than press reports.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish
What this article does not establish. How SIP doubling time compares numerically to lump-sum doubling time at the same rate — that requires the annuity future-value formula and a chosen instalment schedule, which is outside this article's scope; the point made here is only that the Rule of 72 does not apply directly to SIPs, not what the correct SIP-specific shortcut would be. Historical realised CAGR for Indian equity or debt mutual fund categories — this article uses PPF's administered, government-set rate as its only real-world example because it is a single fixed number with no estimation involved; it does not claim any specific equity or fund return figure. The current level of Indian CPI inflation used in the real-rate illustration (~5%) is a round illustrative figure for the arithmetic, not a claimed current official inflation reading, and readers should substitute the actual current CPI figure for their own real-rate calculation.
