India is the world's largest lime and lemon producer — roughly 3-3.5 million tonnes a year off about 3.05 lakh hectares, per market-data aggregators (not a single government census figure, so treat the precise total as directional). Andhra Pradesh leads by volume; Gujarat, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Bihar and the northeastern states round out the rest.

## 1. Why lemon shows up in every "diversify your farm" pitch

The pitch has three real legs. First, the fruiting pattern: mature lemon trees crop near-continuously rather than in one seasonal window, so a grower isn't waiting on a single harvest date the way a cereal or oilseed farmer is. Second, post-harvest resilience: a fallen lemon holds up for days without rotting, where a fallen guava starts decaying within hours — one Dindigul grower put a number on the difference, estimating a guava farmer can lose 40-60 boxes a year (25 kg each) to spoilage that a lemon crop simply doesn't suffer in the same way. Third, market breadth: lemons move through households, juice stalls, restaurants, and pickle/food-processing buyers simultaneously, so a grower isn't dependent on one buyer type.

None of that makes lemon low-risk. It makes lemon differently risky — input and establishment costs are real, trees take several years to reach full bearing, and (as Section 3 shows) prices swing hard with weather, which cuts both ways for growers already in the ground.

## 3. The price swing is the real story, not a footnote

Two independently sourced, dated snapshots of the same phenomenon: in April 2025, Dindigul-district lemons were fetching a steady ₹90/kg from processors under normal conditions. A year later, in April 2026, drought-hit Tenkasi-district lemons hit ₹250/kg in the open market — nearly triple — because Puliyangudi's daily market influx had collapsed from 150-200 tonnes to 75. Both figures are real, both are dated, and the gap between them is the risk profile: a lemon grower's upside in a bad year for everyone else's supply can be substantial, but so is the downside of being the grower whose own wells ran dry alongside everyone else's.

A fallen lemon survives three days on the ground. A fallen guava starts rotting the same day. That single fact is doing more work in farmers' actual switching decisions than any marketing pitch about superfoods or export markets.

The broader pattern, as reported by growers and trade associations rather than derived here: wholesale rates in normal conditions run roughly ₹30-90/kg depending on market, size and grade; scarred or lower-grade fruit destined for juice or pickle factories sells for less than fresh-market grade; and prices spike toward ₹130-250/kg specifically in drought or peak-summer conditions when supply falls fastest — heat stress and irrigation shortfalls hit lemon yields before they hit demand, which is largely inelastic (households, restaurants and food processors don't stop buying lemons because they're expensive, they just pay more).

The two government arrival-price platforms give a live, national picture that's worth checking directly rather than trusting news snapshots alone. As of 18 August 2026, Agmarknet (India's official mandi price-reporting system, sourced via data.gov.in) put the national average lemon price at ₹8,071/quintal — ₹80.7/kg — averaged across 267 APMC markets in 21 states, with Andhra Pradesh the highest-priced state on average (₹9,750/quintal) and Odisha the lowest (₹3,500/quintal), a ₹6,250/quintal spread between them. eNAM (the national electronic trading platform, a separate government system covering a different, overlapping set of mandis) showed a materially higher national average from an early-August 2026 snapshot, about two weeks before the Agmarknet figure above — roughly ₹13,536/quintal (₹135/kg) across 247 markets in 17 states, with Jammu & Kashmir highest (₹18,000/quintal) and Delhi lowest (₹7,000/quintal). The two official platforms disagreeing by nearly ₹5,500/quintal for what should be the same underlying commodity is itself informative: mandi-level price reporting varies by which markets each system samples and how modal price is calculated, so a single "the lemon price is X" claim from either platform alone should be read as one system's snapshot, not a settled national figure. Lemon carries no Minimum Support Price — it is fully market-driven, which is exactly why the swings in this section happen at all.

## 4. Two GI tags, one real export shipment

India's lime/lemon sector has two geographical-indication tags: Assam lime was the first, and Indi Lime — a Kagzi-lime variety exclusive to Vijayapura district, Karnataka, which alone accounts for roughly 58% of Karnataka's lime production (Karnataka ranks fourth nationally in the crop) — became the second, in 2025. The concrete result: in October 2025, India sent its first-ever air shipment of GI-tagged limes to the United Kingdom, combining both "Indi" (Vijayapura) and "Puliyankudi" (the same Tenkasi-district market discussed in Section 3) limes in a consignment that included 350 kg of Indi Lime. It's a genuinely new export channel, not yet a large one.

Set against that, India's existing citrus export base is strikingly concentrated: Bangladesh and Nepal together absorb roughly 99.45% of India's citrus exports by volume, per available trade-data coverage. The UK shipment is a diversification story precisely because almost none of India's citrus trade currently looks like it.
