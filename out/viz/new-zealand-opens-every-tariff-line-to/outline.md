India’s free trade agreement with New Zealand comes into force on 20 October 2026, ten months after negotiations ended and eighteen months after they began. From that day every Indian product enters New Zealand duty-free. India in turn cuts tariffs on about 70% of its tariff lines, some at once and some over ten years, and keeps dairy, the product New Zealand most wanted to sell, almost entirely out.

The headline symmetry hides an asymmetry. New Zealand’s tariffs were already low: an average of 2.3%, with more than half of its lines already free. India’s average is 17.8%. So most of the tariff cutting in this agreement is India’s, concentrated in a short list of New Zealand products, chiefly apples, kiwifruit, wine, wool and timber, while what India gets is mostly a guarantee, visas and an investment promise.

India–New Zealand goods trade is small: about $1.2 billion in 2025-26. It grew by half in 2024-25 and then eased, and India’s surplus became a small deficit as exports fell. New Zealand’s largest export to India is forestry products, NZ$399 million in the year to December 2025 according to its trade ministry.

For India’s exporters, the gain is mostly certainty: most lines were already free, and the remaining New Zealand duties, which are low, go to zero and cannot be raised again. The larger gains claimed for India are outside goods: 5,000 temporary employment visas a year for professionals, 1,000 working-holiday visas, and New Zealand’s stated aim of steering $20 billion of investment into India over fifteen years, which is a facilitation target rather than a commitment by any investor.

## Where India’s concessions land

New Zealand’s gains are concentrated and dated. Wool, coal, sheep meat and hides go duty-free on day one. Kiwifruit gets a duty-free quota of 6,250 tonnes, rising to 15,000 in six years. Wine duties fall by two-thirds or more over ten years, and mānuka honey’s by three-quarters over five.

Apples are the line Indian growers will watch. New Zealand may ship 32,500 tonnes in the first year at 25% duty instead of 50%, rising to 45,000 tonnes, subject to a minimum import price and a seasonal window. New Zealand harvests from February to May, when Indian orchards in Himachal Pradesh and Jammu and Kashmir are selling from cold storage rather than picking, so the fruit arrives off-season rather than head to head with the Indian crop. The quota is also small against India’s apple crop of over two million tonnes a year, though it matters more against the premium imported segment it competes in.

Dairy, one of the main reasons India gave for leaving the RCEP talks in 2019, stays largely closed: milk, cream, whey, yoghurt and cheese are excluded. The openings are at the edges: bulk infant formula and some dairy preparations over seven years, a half-duty quota for milk albumins, and duty-free entry for New Zealand dairy that is processed in India and re-exported. The last is the one to watch: it brings New Zealand milk solids into Indian plants without letting them into the Indian market.
