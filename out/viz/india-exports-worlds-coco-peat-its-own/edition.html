India is the world's largest exporter of coco peat — a coconut-husk byproduct that happens to be one of the best growing substrates for vertical and hydroponic farming anywhere. Almost all of it leaves the country as a raw input for someone else's controlled-environment farm. Meanwhile, Kashmir's saffron harvest — India's own highest-value crop, worth more per kilogram than gold — has fallen 68% in two decades to climate change, and a scattered handful of private entrepreneurs, not any government programme, are the ones proving it can be grown indoors, in coco-peat-based substrate, anywhere in the country.

Top line

  • 68% fall in Kashmir saffron output, from 8 tonnes in FY2010-11 to 2.6 tonnes in FY2023-24 on one accounting.
  • $455 million in coir and coir-product exports in FY25, up from $360 million in FY24.
  • 600 grams from Abdul Majeed Wani's indoor saffron plot, against 1.2 kilograms from the same quantity of corms in the field.
Updated 7 November 2026. Two newer sets of figures. Coir and coir-product exports reached ₹6,614.40 crore in FY2025-26 (MSME ministry), with coir pith the main driver. And the J&K government’s own saffron production figures, given to the Assembly in 2026, do not match the collapse described below: 17.33 tonnes (2020-21), 14.87 (2021-22), 14.94 (2022-23), 23.53 (2023-24) and 19.58 (2024-25), with 3,715 hectares under the crop. On that series, output in recent years is not down to 2.6 tonnes, and the 68% fall in the chart (which uses press-reported figures) is contested. The government also rejected growers’ claims that the 2025 crop fell to a small fraction of normal; no official 2025-26 figure was found.

Kashmir's saffron output has fallen 68% in about two decades

What it means: The post attributes the fall mainly to erratic rainfall and lost insulating snowfall, not to any failure of demand or policy.

What to do: Reconcile the two tonnage series before using either figure in a policy case.

Indoor saffron packs more yield into each square metre of floor

What it means: Reported densities run 20-30 corms per square metre in the field against 200-300 indoors, from one Kerala operation graded moderate-to-strong rather than audited.

What to do: Measure yield in a Kashmir-sourced trial, such as the 10×10 foot NABARD-farmer room, before scaling the claim.

Coir exports climbed to $455 million in FY25 from $360 million

What it means: Coir pith alone carried 59.6% of FY25 export value, making coco peat the largest coir category.

What to do: Check whether the 62.9% rise in April-July FY26 holds for the full year before planning around it.

What would fix this

  • The Jammu & Kashmir Agriculture Production Department should publish one official annual series of saffron area, production and yield, stating how it relates to the 8-to-2.6-tonne and 16-to-5–6-tonne series now in circulation, so the size of the collapse is not left to two irreconcilable accounts.
  • The National Horticulture Board should state in its MIDH protected-cultivation guidelines whether indoor aeroponic rooms, hydroponic racks and container farms are eligible components, and on what cost norms, so growers like those in Hisar, Pune and Wayanad know whether the 50% assistance applies to them.
  • The Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare should publish a completion report on the National Saffron Mission, setting each component’s original deadline against its actual completion, so the missed deadlines now known only from trade press are on the official record.
  • CSIR-IIIM and SKUAST-Kashmir should publish a tested package of practices for indoor saffron, with measured yields against field planting from the same corm quantity, so growers can weigh Wani’s 600 grams indoors against 1.2 kilograms in the field rather than an unattributed “3x yield” claim.
  • The Coir Board should publish monthly export value and volume by product, including coir pith, by destination, on its own website, so figures like coir pith’s 59.6% share of $455 million in FY25 can be read from the primary source rather than secondary summaries.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does not establish. This article does not establish that coco peat specifically (as opposed to other substrates such as rockwool, perlite, or the wooden trays and mist systems the named cases above actually describe) is what any of the named growers are using — none of the sourced reporting on Wani, Mohammad, the Sindhu brothers, or Sivakumar specifically names coco peat as their growing medium; the connection between coco peat and vertical/aeroponic saffron farming made earlier in this piece is a general agronomic argument about the substrate's suitability for controlled-environment agriculture, not a confirmed material choice at any of these specific operations. It does not establish that CSIR-IIIM's own institutional technology has been formally transferred to the Sindhu brothers or Sivakumar; each appears to have independently researched and built their own operation, including by visiting Kashmir directly, rather than working from an official technology-transfer agreement. The "3x yield" figure that circulates in lighter trade coverage is not supported by the one directly reported, named yield comparison this piece found (Wani's indoor plot underperformed the traditional planting), and readers should weight that named case over the unattributed multiplier. The "95% less water" figure fares better: it traces to Shailesh Modak's own reported claim about his specific hydroponic system, not an industry-wide average, so it should be read as one operator's figure for one system, not a general property of all indoor saffron cultivation. This piece does not recommend saffron cultivation, coco peat trading, or vertical farming as an investment or business decision for any reader; nothing here is investment or agricultural advice.
