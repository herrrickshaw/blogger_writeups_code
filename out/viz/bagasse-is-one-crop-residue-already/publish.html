Bagasse: about 75% liquefied in 9 minutes in one lab study, no cost study found

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 75% of raw bagasse liquefied in 9 minutes at 150°C, in one microwave study with a lab polyol.
  • 0 cost or life-cycle studies of a bagasse polyol or foam process were found.
  • 27–55 g of sugar per 100 g dry bagasse across 115 cane varieties, after pretreatment and enzymes.
Updated 7 November 2026. The 2025-26 sugar season has closed. ISMA’s final figure for net sugar production after ethanol diversion was about 279 lakh tonnes, below the February estimate of about 293 lakh tonnes quoted in the note below, with about 36 lakh tonnes carried over (Free Press Journal). The 2026-27 season began on schedule with about 107 mills crushing by 25 October (Business Today). Bagasse comes from crushing whatever the cane is turned into, so the 2026-27 ethanol tender’s exclusion of cane-juice and B-heavy routes does not change how much is generated. Still no cost or life-cycle study of a bagasse polyol was found. The nearest benchmark is a 2025 Green Chemistry paper on a polyurethane gel made from sugarcane molasses and castor oil, which put its selling price at about $15,000 a tonne, roughly five times a fossil equivalent, and found about four times the global-warming impact of fossil polyurethane (Royal Society of Chemistry, read via search snippets only). It is a different feedstock and product, so it does not settle the bagasse question.
Updated 12 October 2026. Newer sugar-season data: ISMA’s third advance estimate (February 2026) put 2025-26 gross sugar output at about 324 lakh tonnes, with about 31 lakh tonnes diverted to ethanol and net output about 293 lakh tonnes, 12% above last season’s net. About 456 mills were crushing in mid-February, against 460 a year earlier. The 80 million tonne bagasse figure below predates this and was not recomputed; by inference it is probably higher this season. Three 2026 laboratory papers on bagasse polyol and liquefaction are added in the liquefaction section. None is a cost, life-cycle or pilot study, so that gap remains.

What it means: Pretreatment and variety set how much sugar comes out, and none of these lab yields has been costed.

What to do: Test the highest-yielding varieties in a pilot pretreatment and enzyme run before any plant is built.

Lab foams from bagasse polyol reached 0.32–0.48 MPa at the hotter cook

What it means: The foams are small-batch lab results from one study, with no independent replication found.

What to do: Make foams in a larger batch, with thermal and replicate tests, before any commercial polyol is replaced.

Mills already burn bagasse, so a polyol plant competes with the boiler

What it means: Most bagasse is burned for steam and power, so a polyol plant would have to replace that fuel and power.

What to do: Publish the price a polyol plant would need to pay to cover the lost fuel and export power.

What would fix this

  • The Department of Food and Public Distribution should require sugar mills to report bagasse generated and where it goes: own boiler, power export, paper, other. Without it, no one can say how much is free to divert.
  • The Department of Science and Technology should fund an independent bagasse-polyol pilot with a published mass balance: bagasse in, liquid out, residue, catalyst and solvent losses, and the foam’s measured strength and conductivity at a stated replacement ratio.
  • The National Sugar Institute, Kanpur, should publish composition and sugar yield by variety and region, since the 115-variety study found a twofold spread and that decides which mills could supply any process.
  • The Bureau of Indian Standards should set how bio-based polyol content in polyurethane foam is measured and declared, so a 30% bagasse polyol claim means one thing across makers.
  • The Ministry of New and Renewable Energy should report bagasse cogeneration separately from other biomass in its state table, so the power that a diverted tonne would cost is visible.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Corrected 25 October 2026. The dilute-acid row in the hydrolysis table was mislabelled: Timung et al. (Journal of Energy 2016, abstract) report 452.27 mg/g for bagasse after acid alone and 282.85 mg/g after acid then enzymes; 487.50 mg/g is the citronella acid-only figure, not bagasse. And bagasse-polyol foam numbers do exist: Xie et al. (Materials 2015, full text) report foam density 0.031–0.043 g/cm³ and compressive strength 0.19–0.48 MPa depending on liquefaction temperature and solvent ratio, so the earlier claim that none were recoverable has been amended.

Sources and caveats

Composition: a regional-journal paper on raw sugarcane bagasse (40.4% cellulose, 23.8% hemicellulose, 27.2% lignin, 3.0% ash, 4.4% extractives; search summary, one sample). Hydrolysis: a study of 115 cane varieties (Industrial Crops and Products, abstract); Timung et al., Journal of Energy 2016 (doi:10.1155/2016/8506214, abstract; dilute acid then enzymes; the paper's abstract gives 452.27 mg/g for bagasse after acid alone and 282.85 mg/g after acid then enzymes; 487.50 mg/g is the acid-only figure for spent citronella, not bagasse); a potassium hydroxide study (PubMed 40126807); a sodium hydroxide study; a ferric chloride and surfactant study (PubMed 28110130); a lime and hydrothermal study (Grimaldi et al., Biotechnology for Biofuels 2015, read in full, PMC4667421); a ten-solvent deep eutectic study and a liquid hot water plus deep eutectic study; a protic ionic liquid study (Biotechnology for Biofuels); a subcritical water conference paper (no yield seen); a Brazilian study of acid inhibitors and detoxification. Platform chemicals: a CICEQ 2023 xylitol study, a Frontiers in Nutrition 2022 xylitol study, RSC Advances 2016 on levulinic acid and furfural, an IJOGCT 2017 study. Liquefaction: Xie et al., Materials 8, 2015 (read in full via Europe PMC, PMC5458812; DOI 10.3390/ma8125472); a 2020 Springer study of bagasse liquefaction kinetics in PEG 400 and glycerol (search summary); a ScienceDirect study of rigid foam from bagasse polyol and one of foam from hydrothermal bagasse oil (2022); an RSC Advances 2024 study of bagasse in flexible foam; a BioResources 2020 study of several biomass sources; a cassava-bagasse study (a different feedstock). Hydrothermal liquefaction: an IIT Madras record (36.3 wt% with KOH and methanol), a study of sugarcane bagasse and straw, and work on sorghum bagasse and soybean-oil co-liquefaction. Adhesives: a 2013 Industrial Crops and Products study of bagasse lignin in phenolic resin and a bagasse pyrolysis-oil study. India: Waste Management World on sugar-mill cogeneration (527 mills; about 80 million tonnes of wet bagasse); ISMA’s third advance estimate for 2025-26 (February 2026) and trade-press counts of mills crushing; a Composites Part B paper (March 2026) on bagasse polyol in geopolymer, a Physchem 2026 paper on nanobubble pretreatment and a BMC Chemistry paper (March 2026) on hydrothermal bio-oil extraction; Powerline, March 2025 (9,806 MW); this blog’s earlier posts on TNPL, biomass power and the 2G viability-gap scheme; Business India and trade sources on Praj. Apart from Xie et al. 2015 and the lime and hydrothermal study, which were read in full on 25 October 2026, figures were seen in abstracts or search summaries, not in the papers themselves.
