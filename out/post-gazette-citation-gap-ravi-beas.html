Read any single Indian gazette notification on its own and you cannot tell whether it is the first version of a rule or the seventh, whether an earlier version was quietly withdrawn as a mistake, or whether the deadline it extends has already been extended thirty-five times before. A small independent research project set out to build a citation index for these notifications — what amends what, ministry by ministry — and in doing so surfaced a tribunal whose report deadline has been pushed back 36 times since 1986, still unresolved 40 years later, tracked nowhere as a single chain until now.

The short version.

  • India's central gazette publishes tens of thousands of notifications (G.S.R./S.O. rules) with no official cross-reference index — nothing on egazette.gov.in tells you that a notification you're reading amends an earlier one, or what later superseded it. An independent research project (Gazette Trail, source on GitHub) set out to build that index by hand, ministry by ministry, and this piece reports what it found.
  • The headline finding, independently corroborated: the Ravi and Beas Waters Tribunal, constituted in April 1986 to settle a Punjab–Haryana–Rajasthan river-water dispute, has had its report deadline extended by gazette notification 36 times — the project's own database traces every single one, 1986 to 2026. A Business Standard report independently confirms the pattern (deadline pushed to August 2026, cited to "exigencies of the work involved") though its date doesn't quite match the project's own latest entry — an open discrepancy this piece reports rather than resolves (see §2).
  • A real duplicate-publication story turned up too: a November 2025 Department of Telecommunications amendment was accidentally gazetted twice under two different G.S.R. numbers a week apart; a third notification a month later quietly rescinded the duplicate. Nothing marks this on either individual notification's own page.
  • This piece independently rebuilt the project's database from its own published source files rather than trusting its summary claims. The real, working number is 52 ministries, 400 notifications, 285 cross-references — not quite the 53/412/291 the project's own final status file claims, because 2 of 53 seed files have a real load-order bug (traced and explained in §6). Everything that does load passes a foreign-key integrity check cleanly.
  • Coverage is a bounded sample, not a survey: 3 ministries (Law and Justice, Tourism, Panchayati Raj) were checked and found to have nothing linkable in the citation shapes this project models — a real finding about how those ministries draft, not a gap in the research.

The invisible link: what a single gazette notification can't tell you

A Government of India gazette notification — a G.S.R. (General Statutory Rule) or S.O. (Statutory Order), the everyday instrument by which a ministry amends a rule, extends a deadline, or reconstitutes a committee — is published as a standalone document. Read one on egazette.gov.in and you get exactly what's on the page: a date, a rule text, sometimes a "Note" reciting its own history. What you don't get, anywhere in the official record, is a queryable answer to "what did this amend, and what amended it before that." Gazette Trail, the project this piece is built on, set out to construct that missing index from scratch: a small SQLite database (gazette_notification and cross_reference tables, with an amends | supersedes | corrigendum | rescinds | repeals | cites relation type on every edge) populated one real, dated, individually-researched notification pair at a time, across every central ministry and department.

The method, as documented in the project's own files, is bounded and disclosed rather than exhaustive: real notification text was researched via gazettetracker.com's Full Text search (a third-party aggregator, not the official egazette.gov.in archive itself), each finding transcribed by an AI research pass and tagged verified_by: research-agent-quoted rather than independently re-checked against the primary PDF by a human. A citation is only modeled when it cites a G.S.R./S.O./S.R.O. number on both ends; notifications citing a bare Act, Regulation, or file number — a real and common citation form in Indian drafting — are deliberately left unlinked rather than forced into a shape the schema doesn't fit. Every one of those choices matters for how much weight the numbers below can bear, and this piece flags them again in §7 rather than letting the topline findings imply more certainty than the underlying research supports.

36 extensions, 40 years, one tribunal that has never finished

The single longest chain in the project's database, by a wide margin, belongs to the Ministry of Jal Shakti: the Ravi and Beas Waters Tribunal, constituted by a notification dated 2 April 1986 under the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956, to adjudicate how much Ravi and Beas river water Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan each get. The tribunal submitted an initial report in 1987, but a further reference back to it — on the volume of water Punjab has "surplus" to share — has never been resolved. Every time the tribunal's deadline to report back approaches, the government gazettes a fresh S.O. extending it. The project's database traces this as one continuous 36-node chain: notification after notification, from 1986 through a notification dated 11 June 2026, each one naming the extension before it.

1986-04-02 (constitution, S.O. 169(E)) → 1996-11-23 → 2003-06-10 → 2003-08-05 → 2004-08-05 → 2005-02-04 → 2005-08-04 → 2006-02-03 → 2006-07-28 → 2007-02-02 → 2007-07-06 → 2008-01-30 → 2008-07-16 → 2009-02-04 → 2009-07-23 → 2010-01-29 → 2010-08-05 → 2011-02-04 → 2011-08-05 → 2012-02-03 → 2012-08-03 → 2013-02-04 → 2013-07-29 → 2014-08-06 → 2015-08-10 → 2016-08-01 → 2017-08-03 → 2018-08-09 → 2019-08-01 → 2020-07-27 → 2021-09-03 → 2022-08-04 → 2023-06-26 → 2024-08-05 → 2025-07-09 → 2026-06-11 (36th and latest, S.O. 3024(E))

This piece independently searched for the tribunal rather than taking the project's chain on faith, and found real, independent corroboration: a Business Standard report confirms the tribunal, its 1986 constitution, its 1987 initial report, and a pattern of repeated deadline extensions "attributed to the exigencies of the work involved" — consistent, in shape, with what the project's database shows. One detail doesn't quite reconcile: that report describes the deadline being pushed "to August 5, 2026," while the project's own latest-dated entry is 11 June 2026. This could be two different notifications close together (a not-infrequent pattern in the rest of this dataset, discussed in §4), a reporting date versus effective date, or a genuine discrepancy in one source or the other — this piece could not resolve it and reports it as open rather than picking a side. What's not in doubt: a real tribunal, real 40-year unresolved history, and a genuinely long, genuinely undocumented-until-now chain of individually unremarkable-looking notifications that only becomes a story once someone actually links them.

A notification published twice, then quietly withdrawn

A smaller but sharper example, from the Department of Telecommunications side of the Ministry of Communications: on 21 November 2024, the ministry gazetted the Telecom Cyber Security Rules, 2024 (G.S.R. 720(E)). Amendment Rules followed on 22 October 2025 (G.S.R. 771(E)). Then, per the project's own research notes, the same amendment appears to have been gazetted a second time under a different number, G.S.R. 796(E), published a week later on 29 October 2025. A third notification, G.S.R. 863(E) dated 25 November 2025, then rescinded the duplicate — formally withdrawing 796(E) and leaving 771(E) as the version actually in force. None of this is visible from reading any one of these four notifications in isolation; a reader who found G.S.R. 796(E) on its own, without knowing it had been rescinded a month later, would have no way to know it no longer applies.

This example is explicitly flagged in the project's own source comments as "precisely the invisible link this index exists for" and as only partly modeled — the 720(E)→771(E) principal-amendment edge and the 796(E)-rescinded-by-863(E) edge are both in the database; a further citation inside 771(E)'s own text, to specific numbered rules within the Indian Telegraph Rules, 1951 rather than to another gazette notification, is a citation shape this schema doesn't represent and is left unmodeled rather than forced.

The Rural Development chain is worth a second look because of what the project's own notes say it deliberately did not do: the most recent notification's own "Note" clause names its immediate predecessor, which names its own predecessor in turn, reaching back to a 2022 notification — and separately, both notes name a 2007 notification (S.O. 323(E)) as Schedule I's very first amendment. But no single quoted text bridges the 15 years between 2007 and 2022, even though the project's own documentation says real intervening amendments plainly exist (MGNREGA's wage Schedule is amended often). Rather than guess at the missing links, the project recorded 2007 as a real, dated fact and simply left no edge connecting it to 2022. That's a useful model for how to read every chain in this piece: a documented edge means a specific quoted citation was found; an undocumented gap means real links likely exist but weren't individually captured, not that nothing happened in between.

When a committee's gazette trail crosses a ministry reorganization

One more finding worth isolating: what happens to a citation chain when the government itself reorganizes. The National Cooperative Development Corporation's General Council membership was last set by a notification issued in July 2020 — under the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare, because the Ministry of Cooperation did not yet exist as a separate ministry. It was created in 2021. When the General Council's membership was next revised, in February 2024, the amending notification came from the new Ministry of Cooperation — a different ministry, on paper, superseding a notification from a ministry that, organizationally, no longer has anything to do with this subject. The project modeled this correctly (the 2020 notification stays recorded under Agriculture, its real ministry_id at the time; the 2024 one is Cooperation's own), rather than either inventing a Cooperation-era ministry for 2020 or silently dropping the cross-ministry link. It's a small, specific illustration of a structural feature of India's gazette record: a machinery-of-government change orphans the citation trail for anyone not already tracking it across the boundary.

What this piece verified itself, rather than took on faith

The project's own final status file (GATES.md) claims, as its closing gate, a clean rebuild: "53 ministries, 412 notifications, 291 cross-references" with a foreign-key integrity check passing cleanly. This piece did not take that claim at face value — it cloned the repository and rebuilt the database itself, in Python, from the project's own schema.sql plus every db/seed_*.sql file, in the same order the project's own documented process specifies.

The rebuild does not fully match the claim. Two of the 53 seed files — Agriculture and Cooperation — fail partway through when loaded in the documented order, because db/seed_agriculture.sql inserts two gazette_notification rows referencing a ministry ("agriculture-and-farmers-welfare") in its first two statement blocks, before the statement that actually creates that ministry row three blocks later in the same file — a straightforward internal ordering bug, not a data-correctness problem. db/seed_cooperation.sql then fails too, but for a different reason: one of its own notification rows deliberately reuses the same "agriculture-and-farmers-welfare" ministry_id for the pre-2021 NCDC notification discussed in §5, and because Agriculture's file aborted before ever creating that row, Cooperation's otherwise-correct reference to it fails as a knock-on effect. The real, working numbers from a from-scratch rebuild are 52 ministries, 400 notifications, 285 cross-references — not 53/412/291. Once those two files' internal ordering is looked past, everything else loads cleanly and passes SQLite's own foreign-key check with zero violations: nothing that did load is internally broken, and the two-file gap is a real, narrow, explainable bug rather than a sign the modeled data itself is unreliable.

One more real finding, also independently checked rather than assumed: the project's coverage plan documents 3 ministries — Law and Justice, Tourism, and Panchayati Raj — as deliberately abandoned after a genuine research pass turned up nothing linkable. Law and Justice's real amendment pairs all cite Act or Regulation numbers, never a G.S.R./S.O./S.R.O.; Tourism's tracker coverage runs to only 4 notifications total, each amendment citing a G.S.R. number on one side only; Panchayati Raj has just 2 tracked notifications, both standalone Resolutions with no relationship between them. That's a genuine finding about how those ministries' delegated legislation is drafted and numbered, not a gap in the research — and the project's own documentation treats it with the same weight as a successfully modeled ministry rather than hiding it.

What doesn't follow from any of this

Every number and chain in this piece describes what one bounded research pass, using one third-party aggregator, found and could transcribe into the citation shapes this project's schema models — not a complete or official record of India's gazette history. A ministry "done" in the project's own coverage plan means "modeled what a bounded pass turned up," explicitly not "modeled everything that exists"; a longer or shorter real chain may well exist for any subject covered here that this research simply didn't surface. Every fact in the underlying database is tagged research-agent-quoted — an AI research pass's own transcription of text found via a secondary aggregator, not independently re-verified against the primary PDF by a human, by this blog, or (with the one exception of the Ravi-Beas story checked in §2) by any source this piece could independently confirm. The 36-node Ravi-Beas chain itself, while independently corroborated in its shape and broad history, has one specific unresolved date discrepancy against outside press coverage, reported rather than resolved. And the database-rebuild discrepancy in §6 is a narrow, explained bug in exactly two files' statement ordering — it says nothing about the correctness of the roughly 400 real notification records that do load and check out cleanly.
