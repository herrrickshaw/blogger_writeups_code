Put a piece of thermocol in orange-peel oil and it collapses in seconds, leaving a small puddle of syrup. Videos of this are common, and Indian press coverage of an IIT Hyderabad project has presented it as a way to recycle foam waste. The chemistry is real. What the videos do not show is what happens to the syrup: whether it can be turned back into polystyrene, how often the solvent can be used again, and who would pay for the plant.

This piece reads the published record, from abstracts, patents and project pages, because the full texts were not available to this blog. The finding is that the first half of the story is well documented and old, and the second half has hardly been published at all.

What dissolves, and what you get back

Thermocol is expanded polystyrene, a plastic that is about 98% air by volume. Limonene, the main compound in citrus peel oil, is a solvent for polystyrene, and so the foam dissolves and the air escapes. What is left is not the “chemicals” of the foam taken out one by one but a solution of polystyrene chains. That solution has three possible uses: precipitate the polymer with an alcohol and pelletise it again; use the solution directly, for fibres, coatings or adhesives; or treat the dissolving step as a way to compact foam before something else. It does not give back styrene, the building block, which needs heat or a catalyst.

The dissolving is also quick and cold. In the Noguchi series of 1998, the first to describe a recycling system built on limonene, foam shrank to about one-twentieth of its volume and the recovered polystyrene showed almost no loss of molecular weight, which the authors credited to limonene protecting the polymer when heated. A later paper on extruded polystyrene reported solubility of about 0.26 g per mL of limonene at 25°C, which on this blog’s arithmetic is a solution of about a quarter polymer by weight. The Colombian study put the fall in volume at about 96%.

Limonene is not the best terpene

At 50°C, p-cymene, another compound found in plant oils, dissolved more polystyrene than limonene did, about 212 g per 100 g of solvent against 182 to 197 g. At that strength the solution is about two-thirds polymer. p-Cymene and the polymer could also be recovered almost completely by steam distillation. The two sets of figures are for different temperatures and polystyrene, so they cannot be set side by side. The point is that limonene is one of several workable solvents, and the industrial solvent processes that exist, CreaSolv and Polystyvert, use others. The European demonstration of CreaSolv did not reach its target of 3,000 tonnes a year.

The loop, and where the data stop

  1. Dissolving is documented. The limit of the three-part mixture is not. The amount of polystyrene that a limonene and alcohol mixture can hold, which sets the size of the plant, was reported as missing from the literature until a 2024 paper and preprint.
  2. Residual limonene in the recovered polymer. Limonene boils at 176°C, which makes it hard to strip, and it has a strong smell. No source gave how much stays in pellets or whether it can be sold into food-contact or indoor uses.
  3. Solvent ageing. The claim that limonene can be reused at least ten times comes from a review. In air limonene oxidises to carvone and limonene oxide, and oxidised limonene is a skin sensitiser. No source measured the solvent after repeated cycles.

Two further gaps do not fit in the figure. Distilling a liquid with a flash point near 45°C is a fire risk that a plant has to be licensed for. And an independent cost or life-cycle study of a limonene foam process was not found; the two recent studies on foam recycling cover a mechanical compaction route in Colombia (viable over twelve years, with a 68% lower carbon footprint) and a chemical route to styrene.

What actually gets used

Where limonene-dissolved foam has been put to work, it has been used as a solution, not as a source of pellets. Shin and Chase spun waste foam in limonene into fibres of 300 to 900 nanometres in 2005, but they were coarse and of poor quality, and later work mixes in a second solvent. The IIT Hyderabad group of Chandra Shekhar Sharma, working with a grant of about ₹35.7 lakh under the Department of Science and Technology’s waste management programme, made a non-woven fabric from foam and citrus peel extract that soaks up oil. DST says a pilot machine is ready and technology transfer is under way through a company in the IIT Hyderabad incubator. These are peel extracts, not purified limonene, and the published descriptions do not say how the solvent is recovered.

The additives matter for what is recovered. Foam waste typically carries 0.1% to 1.5% of the pentane used to expand it. The flame retardant HBCD, a persistent pollutant in insulation foam, stays in the liquid when polystyrene is precipitated, which is the principle behind CreaSolv, with a target of under 100 parts per million in the polymer. Typical packaging foam is not the HBCD-treated kind. Nothing found covered food contamination or dye removal.

The Indian setting

Thermocol has been on the regulatory map for some years. Maharashtra banned thermocol products in March 2018 and has since relaxed parts of its plastics rules. The national ban on identified single-use plastic items that took effect on 1 July 2022 names polystyrene and expanded polystyrene, including thermocol for decoration. Packaging foam is not covered by the ban, and falls under extended producer responsibility for producers and brand owners; whether and how foam is counted separately in the EPR data is not clear from what was found.

Foam recycling already exists in India by mechanical routes. A Pune recycler has processed it since the early 1970s, according to a profile, and a Ghaziabad firm lists about 720 tonnes a year, though the only throughput figure found is self-reported in a directory. Small compaction machines are advertised at about ₹2.65 lakh to ₹3.5 lakh. No resale price per kilogram or national collection volume turned up. Against that, a solvent route would have to beat compaction on cost, and there is no cost data to say whether it can.

The solvent side is better placed than it looks but is unmeasured. India grows about ten million tonnes of oranges a year, and a preprint on Nagpur orange waste found 91.9% limonene in the extracted oil. But there were no figures for how much peel oil is produced, no trade data for citrus oils, and world supply of technical-grade limonene is about 30,000 tonnes a year according to one supplier, with other estimates up to 70,000 tonnes. A foam-recycling industry would be competing for a by-product that cleaning, fragrance and coatings makers already buy.

What would fix this

  • The Department of Science and Technology should fund an independent pilot of a limonene or peel-oil foam loop with a published mass balance: polystyrene recovered, solvent lost per cycle, residual solvent in the polymer, and solvent quality after ten cycles.
  • The Central Pollution Control Board should publish expanded polystyrene collection and recycling tonnage separately from the EPR filings, so the size of the problem, and the share handled by compaction recyclers, can be seen.
  • The Bureau of Indian Standards should set a specification for recycled polystyrene from solvent routes, with a limit for residual solvent and a statement of which uses, such as food contact, are excluded.
  • The Ministry of Food Processing Industries and APEDA should publish citrus peel oil output by processor and region, and the Directorate General of Commercial Intelligence and Statistics should report citrus oils at a finer tariff line, so supply can be judged.
  • The Petroleum and Explosives Safety Organisation should say how a plant that distils limonene would be licensed, given its flash point of 45–48°C, so that safety requirements are known before a pilot is built.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
