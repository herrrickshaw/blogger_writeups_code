India’s Biggest Mandi Crops Have the Steadiest Prices. Its Vegetables Swing Two to Eight Times as Much.

Updated 12 November 2026. Refreshed with Agmarknet data through 31 October 2026 (previously to 23 October). Arrivals now cover November 2025 to October 2026, and the method and 23 crops are unchanged. Paddy and wheat now take 45.1% of the 23 crops’ arrivals, 71.9 of 159 million tonnes, against 44.7% (68.6 of 153) before. Rankings: maize overtakes potato as the fourth-largest crop, groundnut moves past garlic to 12th, and Madhya Pradesh (34.3 million tonnes) overtakes Uttar Pradesh (33.7) as the largest state. Wheat’s typical monthly move rises from 2.5% to 2.7%, which takes it from 18th to 17th on volatility, swapping places with chana. Tomato’s stays at 20.4% and onion’s at 13.5%. One earlier slip is also corrected: Chhattisgarh, at 10.2%, edges out Uttar Pradesh (10.3%) as onion’s steadiest state. MoSPI’s October consumer price index put onion up 116% on a year earlier (Zee Business, 12 November 2026). In the mandi data, the all-India arrival-weighted onion price in October 2026 was Rs 3,440 a quintal. That is 3.2 times October 2025’s Rs 1,059 and 4% below September’s Rs 3,590, after five monthly rises from Rs 1,077 in May. October 2025 was itself unusually cheap: in October 2024 the price was Rs 3,507. October’s onion arrivals were 1.30 million tonnes against 1.34 million a year earlier. The CPI is a retail index and the mandi figure is a wholesale price, so the two are not expected to match.

Two earlier pieces on this site looked at maize trading for ethanol plants and at how maize and rice prices vary by state and season. This one widens the lens to 23 crops: seven cereals, five pulses, three oilseeds and cotton, four vegetables and three spices. For each crop and state it asks two questions. How much reaches the mandis? And how much does the price move from one month to the next?

How much to trust it. All figures come from Agmarknet’s public dashboard API: monthly weighted-average prices in rupees per quintal and arrivals in tonnes, by state, January 2019 to October 2026. Arrivals are totals over the past 12 months. Volatility is measured as the typical monthly price move, the median of the absolute month-on-month price change from January 2023, so a 3% figure means that in a typical month the price rose or fell by about 3%. The median is used deliberately: Agmarknet contains occasional misreported months, and a standard deviation let a single bad month in Tamil Nadu’s tomato series swamp everything else. State-months with under 50 tonnes of arrivals are dropped, and a crop is shown for a state only with at least 24 monthly price changes and 5,000 tonnes of arrivals in the past year. Agmarknet’s coverage widened when its new portal went live in November 2025, which raises recent arrivals for every crop. Prices mix varieties and grades within a crop. Arrivals measure what is reported at regulated markets, not production: crops sold mostly outside mandis, or procured directly by government agencies, are undercounted, while states that procure through mandis show large volumes.

Volume and volatility, crop by crop

The chart splits cleanly. The crops that fill India’s mandis by weight, paddy, wheat and maize, are also among the steadiest, moving 2.7–3.1% in a typical month. Pulses, oilseeds and cotton sit in the same band, with cotton the steadiest of all at 1.8%. The four vegetables sit far to the right. Onion and potato each draw more than 16 million tonnes a year, as much as maize, but onion moves 13.5% a month and potato 6.1%. Tomato and garlic move 20.4% and 16.0%. Spices fall in between, at 3.8–5.5%.

The price-range column, comparing a crop’s dearest months with its cheapest after trimming the extremes, tells the same story at a longer horizon. Wheat’s dearest months since 2023 ran 27% above its cheapest. Tomato’s and onion’s ran about four times higher, and garlic’s nearly five times higher. Some steady crops still had large ranges because their prices trended. Soybean’s 66% range and mustard’s 54% mostly reflect prices drifting over the period rather than month-to-month churn.

Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh dominate, with about 34 million tonnes each across these crops. Madhya Pradesh’s biggest flows are wheat, maize and soybean; Uttar Pradesh’s are potato, wheat and paddy. Chhattisgarh’s 15 million tonnes of paddy likely reflect the state’s large-scale paddy purchases at the support price being recorded as arrivals. Maharashtra is the onion state, with 8 million tonnes. Delhi appears because Azadpur and the city’s other terminal markets receive vegetables from across the north, not because it grows them.

Volatility state by state

The crop matters far more than the state. Tomato moved at least 18.8% a month in every state shown, and 40.7% in Telangana, 34.4% in Andhra Pradesh and 32.3% in West Bengal. Onion was steadiest in Chhattisgarh and Uttar Pradesh at about 10% and most volatile in Madhya Pradesh at 21.6%. Maharashtra, which supplies the largest share of the country’s onion arrivals, ran at 17.6%. At the other end, wheat in Uttar Pradesh moved 1.4% in a typical month, paddy in West Bengal 0.5% and rice in West Bengal 0.9%. These are crops that the government buys at minimum support prices and holds in public stocks, which helps anchor their price.

Within the steady crops a few state readings stand out. Maize in Haryana moved 8.4% a month against 2.5–5.4% in the main producing states, a small market with patchy reporting. Paddy moved 6.8% in Madhya Pradesh and 5.9% in Rajasthan, against about 2% in Chhattisgarh, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh, states that procure large volumes of paddy. Tur moved 8.7% in Telangana against 1.7% in Uttar Pradesh.

Why the two groups differ

Part of the explanation is visible in the crops themselves. Those with steady prices share two traits: they store for months or years, and most are bought by the government at a support price or held in public stocks. Wheat and paddy have both. Pulses and oilseeds store well, and their prices are steered by import duties and periodic government purchases. The volatile crops cannot be held for long. Tomato lasts days, and onion and potato a few months at best, even in cold or ventilated storage. A late monsoon, a heatwave or a glut in one producing region therefore reaches the price within weeks, with no stock to absorb it.

What it means

For a processor or trader, the ranking sorts crops into two kinds of business. High-volume, low-volatility crops such as wheat, paddy, maize, soybean and mustard suit models built on buying in season and storing, provided the seasonal spread beats the cost of carry. The earlier state-wise maize and rice analysis found that for those two grains it usually did not. High-volume, high-volatility crops such as onion, potato and tomato reward speed instead: sourcing from whichever state is harvesting, moving produce quickly, and contracting ahead with growers or buyers to share the price risk. For policy, the gap between the two groups suggests where price support has worked as intended, and where storage and processing capacity, rather than procurement, would do more to steady prices.
