Every year, India's downstream oil-and-gas regulator publishes an Annual Plan, and every year it follows up with an Action Taken Report (ATR) describing what it did against that plan. The ATR for 2025-26, published around PNGRB's 19th Foundation Day in September 2026, runs 18 pages across 13 divisions and reads, division by division, like a list of accomplishments. It is also, read carefully, one of the more useful documents PNGRB has published this year for a different reason: it discloses its own numerator and denominator often enough that its "achieved" claims can be checked against its own arithmetic. This piece does that checking, division by division, using nothing but the ATR's own figures.

Common Carrier conversion: 2 pipelines converted, 25 waiting on a court, 16 unaccounted for

Under the Ease of Doing Business heading, the ATR states that PNGRB has a "Regulatory Process initiated for converting captive pipelines to Common Carrier pipeline. Out of 43, 2 had been declared Common Carrier, and the order are reserved for 25 pipelines, awaiting the outcome of Hon'ble high court on subject." Declaring a pipeline a "common carrier" forces its owner to offer spare capacity to third parties on non-discriminatory terms — the mechanism that is supposed to stop a single company from sitting on unused pipeline capacity and blocking competitors. Two conversions out of 43 identified pipelines is a 4.7% completion rate on the ATR's own numbers, with more than half the remaining 41 (25 of them) stalled on litigation rather than regulatory action, and the ATR does not account for the other 16.

Billing transparency: "achieved," for under half the sector

The Consumer Affairs Division reports that "Full & Mandatory Billing Compliance by 10 entities across 102 GAs, covering approximately 49% of consumers under the CGD sector has been achieved. Compliance by remaining entities is under progress." The word "achieved" sits directly next to the number 49%, describing a mandate that, on the regulator's own account, does not yet cover a majority of the sector it governs. Ten entities meeting a standard is a real result; it is also, on the same page, evidence that an unspecified larger number of entities have not met it, more than a year into the initiative it belongs to.

A consumer right to an Ombudsman that has no Ombudsman

The PNGRB (Consumer Protection) Regulations, 2025 were notified on 19 March 2026 and took effect on 24 March 2026, consolidating consumer rights across PNG, CNG, LPG and retail-outlet segments into one framework. The regulations create a four-tier escalation path: an entity's own grievance mechanism, an Appellate Authority, the centralised IGMS Portal (launched 20 March 2026), and finally an Ombudsman for complaints that survive all three earlier tiers. The ATR's own Consumer Affairs section, however, reports that the Ombudsman itself is not yet built: "An Expert Advisor Committee was constituted to examine the feasibility, scope and framework of an Ombudsman mechanism... the final report of the Committee is expected shortly." Five months after the regulation granting consumers the right to escalate to an Ombudsman took legal effect, the office they would escalate to is still at the "expected shortly" stage of an advisory committee's own report.

Why this matters more than a missing office. A four-tier grievance system is only as strong as its final tier. Until the Ombudsman mechanism exists, PNGRB's own regulations grant consumers a right of last-resort escalation that currently terminates at the IGMS Portal — a real improvement over no centralised portal at all, but not yet the independent final-appeal body the regulation itself describes.

None of this means the underlying initiatives are badly designed. A captive-to-common-carrier conversion genuinely can get stuck in a High Court; consumer protection regulations genuinely do take time to staff an Ombudsman's office after the regulation itself is notified; a CBG plant genuinely cannot inject gas into a network that has no pipe connecting it. The pattern worth naming is narrower: the ATR's own section headers ("Ease of Doing Business," "achieved," "operationalised") consistently describe progress in stronger terms than the numbers two sentences later support.

What would fix this

  • Report completion rates, not just completed counts. "2 of 43" tells a reader something "2 pipelines converted" does not. The ATR already has the denominator for most of its claims; publishing the ratio, not just the numerator, would let a reader judge pace without doing the division themselves.
  • Give every "expected shortly" a date. The Ombudsman committee, the Scientific Committee for hydrogen pilots, and the Cadre proposal are all open-ended in this report. A next Action Taken Report with no named date attached to any of them would be a second report in a row without accountability for the delay.
  • Separate "notified" from "operational" in the report's own language. A regulation taking legal effect and a grievance mechanism actually being staffed are different facts; the ATR currently uses "achieved" and "operationalised" for both.
  • Publish the 16 unaccounted captive pipelines' status explicitly. If 2 are converted and 25 await a court, the remaining 16 of the original 43 are simply missing from this report; naming their status would close the gap this piece could not.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does and doesn't cover

  • This piece works entirely from PNGRB's own Action Taken Report and does not independently verify the underlying figures (43 captive pipelines, 102 GAs, ~130 CBG plants) against a separate primary dataset; where this blog has separately verified an adjacent figure (the Unified Tariff and Pipeline Development Reserve mechanism, and the CBG connectivity figures), those pieces are linked below rather than re-derived here.
  • It does not allege intent to mislead. The gap between headline language and disclosed numbers is consistent with normal institutional self-reporting incentives, not evidence of a specific decision to overstate progress.
  • It does not cover every division in the ATR — the Authorisation Division's pipeline-identification work, the Statistics Division's Excellence Awards, and the IT Division's NHIMS platform are all reported as delivered with less ambiguity and are not re-litigated here.
