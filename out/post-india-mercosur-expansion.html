On 14 September India and MERCOSUR, the trade bloc of Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay and Uruguay, launched negotiations to widen the preferential trade agreement that has been in force since 2009. The same day they signed a protocol so that electronic certificates of origin are accepted in place of paper. The 2009 pact is narrow: it gives preferences on about 450 Indian and 452 MERCOSUR tariff lines. The launch statements say the talks will cover “new areas of mutual interest”. They do not say which products, and the terms of reference that would define the scope were still being finalised.

The trade the new terms would sit on has changed fast. Taking the four members together, India’s imports rose from about $9.4 billion in 2024-25 to about $13.3 billion in 2025-26, while its exports rose from $8.1 billion to $8.5 billion. The deficit went from about $1.2 billion to about $4.9 billion, nearly four times as large, and four-fifths of it is with one country.

Brazil is the larger partner in both directions, and India’s balance with it is close to even: $7.0 billion of exports against $8.1 billion of imports. India’s largest exports to Brazil in 2025-26 were organic chemicals ($0.92 billion), vehicles ($0.83 billion) and pharmaceuticals ($0.60 billion), together about a third of India’s sales there, according to the commerce ministry’s trade data. Argentina is the imbalance. It sold India about $5.0 billion and bought about $1.0 billion.

The composition of Argentina’s sales was not found in the official data available for this piece, but reported figures fit the total. Argentina’s ambassador has said India was the main destination for Argentine soybean oil in 2025, worth about $3.7 billion, with the sunflower complex adding $0.8 billion; trade press reports Argentina overtaking Ukraine as India’s sunflower oil supplier in the 2025-26 marketing year, with a record 530,000 tonnes shipped between September and April. Those figures use different periods, but together they come to most of the $5.0 billion. From Brazil, reported items include crude oil and soybean oil. This is the same trade covered in this blog’s piece on India’s cooking-oil duties.

What an expansion would put on the table

An agreement that broadens preferences does so on both sides of the schedule. For India, the products it sells to the bloc (chemicals, vehicles and parts, pharmaceuticals) are the obvious asks. For MERCOSUR the products at the centre of its sales to India are farm goods, above all edible oils. Cooking-oil duties are a lever India has pulled three times since 2024, raising them to support farmers and then cutting them twice to calm prices. A preference on edible oils for four countries that already supply a large share of the imports would sit on top of that lever, and the question of how it would affect domestic oilseed prices is one that the launch statements do not raise.

Argentina has been pressing for the broader deal, according to trade press, and its sales to India are what a wider pact would most readily grow. Whether India’s own exports to the bloc are wider than the 450 lines now covered, and how much of the current trade uses the preferences at all, are things the published data does not show.

What would fix this

  • The Department of Commerce, with Customs, should publish each year how much of India’s trade with MERCOSUR claimed the preference: shipments, value and duty foregone, by line, for each direction, so the existing 450 lines can be judged before more are added.
  • The Directorate General of Commercial Intelligence and Statistics should publish India–MERCOSUR trade at the eight-digit line, flagged as covered or not covered by the pact, so the share of trade the agreement reaches is visible.
  • The Department of Commerce should publish the terms of reference and a summary of the request lists by sector once agreed, and run a public consultation, especially with edible oil, pulses, sugar and ethanol producers and the chemicals, auto and pharma exporters who would gain.
  • The Department of Agriculture and the Department of Food and Public Distribution should publish an impact note on domestic oilseed and edible oil prices before any preference on vegetable oils is offered.
  • The Department of Commerce should publish the negotiating calendar against the one-year aim reported by Brazil, with a note after each round on what was discussed.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
