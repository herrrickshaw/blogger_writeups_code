India's Draft Seeds Bill Cuts States' Committee Seats From 22 to 5. That's the Provision Farmer Groups Are Actually Fighting.

Skip to article content
Text Size

On 13 November 2025, the Department of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare put out a routine-sounding press release: a draft Seeds Bill, 2025 was ready, comments were invited by 11 December, and the bill would replace the Seeds Act of 1966 and the Seeds (Control) Order of 1983. Ten months later, on 9 September 2026, government messaging around the same bill sounded different — "stringent," a crackdown on "seed mafias," a farmer consultation called with one day's notice ahead of a meeting the next morning. Both descriptions are accurate. What changed between them is emphasis, not substance — and the emphasis shift has obscured the one provision farmer organisations and civil-society groups have actually organised against: a rewritten Central Seed Committee that gives states a fraction of the seats they held before.

The short version.

India's draft Seeds Bill, 2025 would replace the 1966 Seeds Act and 1983 Seed Control Order. Its stated goals, per the government's own November 2025 announcement: seed-quality regulation, affordable access for farmers, curbing spurious seed sales, liberalising seed imports, and decriminalising minor offences while keeping strong penalties for serious violations. Multiple secondary reports (not independently verified against the draft text) describe mandatory registration for seed varieties and dealers, QR-code traceability, and fines up to ₹30 lakh with up to 3 years' imprisonment for selling spurious or unregistered seed. Farmer groups and civil-society organisations have focused their criticism less on the penalties and more on the bill's proposed Central Seed Committee, which reportedly cuts state government seats from 22 to 5, and on a stated conflict with the 2001 law that recognises farmers as plant-variety rights-holders in their own right. A high-level consultation with farmer-organisation leaders was held in Delhi on 10 September 2026 to fold their input into the draft before introduction.

What the government's own announcement actually said

The Ministry's own framing, read directly from its 13 November 2025 release (PIB Release ID 2189600), lists six goals for the draft bill: regulate the quality of seeds and planting material in the market; ensure farmers can access high-quality seed at affordable rates; curb the sale of spurious and poor-quality seed; protect farmers from the losses that causes; liberalise seed imports to widen access to global varieties and encourage innovation; and safeguard farmers' rights while building transparency and accountability into the seed supply chain. On enforcement specifically, the release states the bill "proposes to decriminalise minor offences, thereby promoting Ease of Doing Business and reducing compliance burden, while maintaining strong provisions to penalise serious violations effectively."

That is a genuinely tiered design, not a contradiction of the "stringent" framing that followed it in September — paperwork lapses and minor non-compliance are the offences being decriminalised or lightened; selling fake or spurious seed is what the "strong provisions" and, per later reporting, the heavier fines and imprisonment are aimed at. The two descriptions of the same bill, ten months apart, are consistent with each other once read together; the shift is which half of the bill each moment's messaging chose to lead with, not a change in the bill's actual design.

The fight is over representation, not fines

The criticism farmer organisations and agrarian civil-society groups have raised publicly is not primarily about the size of the penalties — it is about who sits on the committee that will administer the law and what legal framework the bill sits alongside. Two specific objections recur: first, that cutting state governments' seats on the Central Seed Committee from 22 to 5 concentrates seed-regulatory authority away from the states that actually administer agriculture on the ground; second, that the bill's registration-and-liability architecture is built around corporate and institutional seed varieties, treating farmers' own traditional varieties as an exemption carved out of the system rather than a category the law is designed to protect — which critics argue puts it in tension with the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights (PPV&FR) Act, 2001, a law that explicitly recognises farmers as breeders and rights-holders over the varieties they develop and conserve, not merely as an exempted class under someone else's registration regime.

Neither objection appears in the government's own November announcement, which frames the bill entirely in terms of quality, affordability, and ease of doing business. That gap — between what the bill's own advocates emphasise and what its critics have organised around — is the real story here, more than any single number in the penalty table above.

Today's consultation, and who's in the room

On 10 September 2026, Union Agriculture and Farmers Welfare Minister Shivraj Singh Chouhan held a high-level consultation in New Delhi with senior leaders of major farmer organisations, called with the stated aim of incorporating farmers' "ground-level experiences, suggestions and concerns" directly into the draft legislation before it advances further. Per the Ministry's own account, the organisations represented included the Bharatiya Kisan Sangh, several factions of the Bharatiya Kisan Union (BKU), the All India Kisan Coordination Committee (AIKCC), Kisan Mahapanchayat, Bharatiya Krishak Samaj, Gaon Kisan Unnayan, and the Society for Independent Farmers Association (SIFA), alongside ground-level farmer representatives from several states. The Ministry framed the exercise as advancing Prime Minister Narendra Modi's stated vision of a prosperous, self-reliant farming community, and described the proposed Act's enforcement aim as building "a robust and effective regulatory framework to curb the activities of seed mafias and protect farmers from fraudulent and poor-quality seeds."
