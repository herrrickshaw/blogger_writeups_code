Roughly two-thirds of everything the Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas gazettes isn't fuel-pricing policy — it's land. Every kilometre of pipeline crossing private land needs a specific, dated notification acquiring the "right of user" under a 1962 law, and those notifications, not the more visible tender and pricing orders, are the bulk of this ministry's paper trail. This piece maps what that actually looks like: the two separate laws doing two separate jobs, the national pipeline build-out those notifications are quietly enabling, and a state-by-state accounting of the specific projects this piece could verify — with an explicit, prominent flag on where that accounting is solid and where it isn't.

The short version.

  • Two different laws do two different jobs, and conflating them misreads the whole system. The Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board (PNGRB), under a 2006 Act, authorises a company to build and operate a specific pipeline. The Petroleum and Minerals Pipelines (Acquisition of Right of User in Land) Act, 1962 — a much older, separate law — is what MoPNG then uses to actually acquire the right to lay that authorised pipeline across private land, village by village, as construction proceeds. The gazette notifications this piece is about are almost all the second kind.
  • This blog's own gazette-indexing project estimates roughly 700 of this ministry's 1,081 tracked gazette items are these land-acquisition ("ARUL Act") notices — the large majority of everything MoPNG gazettes, by volume.
  • The pipelines these notices are chasing are real and large: PNGRB's own data puts India's authorised natural gas pipeline network at 34,803 km as of 31 March 2026, of which 27,905 km, about four-fifths, is operational and the rest under construction or partly commissioned, against a stated ~35,000 km ambition; a further ~18,900 km of petroleum-product pipeline and, as of August 2026, a newly-expanded ~9,500 km LPG pipeline network.
  • Several flagship projects are running behind their own most recent schedule: the Kochi-Koottanad-Bengaluru-Mangaluru pipeline (KKBMPL) has had its completion date revised at least twice since December 2025, most recently to September 2026; the Srikakulam-Angul spur (June 2026), the last Maharashtra stretch of Mumbai-Nagpur-Jharsuguda (June 2026) and the last two sections of JHBDPL (September 2026) have also been rescheduled. All of those dates have now passed without a completion report this piece could find.
  • The state-by-state table in this piece (§5) should be read as a research trail, not a verified register. Every primary and tracker source for the underlying notifications — egazette.gov.in, PNGRB's own data-bank PDFs, gazettetracker.com, and the compliance-tracker sites that write these up — was blocked from this piece's research environment. This piece also caught its own search tooling reusing the same two notification numbers for two unrelated pipelines in different searches; a check against the Gazette’s own text on 28 October 2026 resolved both to the Reliance ethane line (see the note above).

Two laws, two notifications, easy to conflate

"MoPNG issued a pipeline gazette notification" undersells how much is packed into that one sentence, because it could mean either of two structurally different actions under two different laws passed 44 years apart.

The first is authorisation, under the Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board Act, 2006. PNGRB runs a bidding process and then authorises a specific entity — GAIL, a state gas company, a joint venture like Petronet or IHB Limited — to lay, build, operate and expand a named pipeline of a defined length, for a defined fluid, along a defined route. This is the regulatory and commercial approval: it fixes who gets to build the pipeline and, eventually, who can charge what tariff to move fuel through it. PNGRB publishes its own running data-bank of every pipeline it has authorised, by entity, length and status.

The second is land acquisition, under the much older Petroleum and Minerals Pipelines (Acquisition of Right of User in Land) Act, 1962 (sometimes abbreviated ARUL, PMP, or P&MP Act in the notifications themselves). Once a pipeline is authorised, the company still has to actually get onto the land it crosses — and rather than buying every parcel outright, the Act lets the Central Government declare a narrower "right of user": the right to lay, maintain and access a pipeline underground, while ownership and (mostly) surface use stay with the landowner. This runs in two notified stages: a Section 3(1) notice declaring the intention to acquire the right of user over specifically described land (triggering a 21-day objection window), followed, once objections are heard, by a Section 6(1) notice making the acquisition final. For a single national pipeline hundreds of kilometres long, this generates dozens of separate notifications as the acquisition proceeds village by village, district by district, sometimes years apart.

Almost everything in the rest of this piece is the second kind. PNGRB's authorisation records are themselves genuinely useful public data (see §3–4) and this piece draws on them directly; but the "pipeline gazette notifications" that dominate MoPNG's own gazette output, by sheer count, are ARUL Act right-of-user notices, not PNGRB authorisations.

This distinction is drawn from secondary legal-explainer sources (Lexology, Bhatt & Joshi Associates) rather than a primary reading of both Acts side by side; the Acts' own texts are consistent with this framing but this piece did not cross-check every procedural detail (e.g. exact objection-hearing timelines) against the bare statute.

The scale: this blog's own prior finding, extended

This blog's earlier deep dive into India's gazette-notification record (see "Related on this blog" below) built an independent, from-scratch database of ministry-by-ministry gazette activity. Its own research notes on the Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas, cross-checked again for this piece, put the ministry's total tracked gazette items (per the gazettetracker.com aggregator) at 1,081, of which the project's own research estimates roughly 700 — about two-thirds — are ARUL Act pipeline right-of-user notices specifically, rather than fuel-pricing orders, rules amendments, or anything else this ministry gazettes.

That earlier project deliberately modeled only a bounded sample of this ARUL Act traffic in its own database — 12 notifications, covering one corrigendum and four citation pairs between 2021 and 2026 — rather than the full ~700, and none of those 12 sample rows carry pipeline name, state, district, or fluid-type metadata; that level of detail was never transcribed into the database. This piece's own research (§5) is an attempt to reconstruct some of that missing state-and-project detail independently, for a different, small sample of notifications, using open web search rather than the database.

The 1,081/~700 figures are this project's own research estimates, based on reading gazettetracker.com's aggregator listing and the underlying notifications' full text (not just their titles, which the project's own notes say are sometimes misleading — several are titled "Erratum" but are actually declarations). gazettetracker.com itself was blocked for direct fetch by this piece's own research environment, so this figure is inherited from the earlier project's work rather than independently re-verified here.

What all this notification traffic is actually building: the national gas grid

The pipelines generating these notifications are not abstract. PNGRB's own published data-bank puts India's total authorised natural gas pipeline network at 34,803 km as of 31 March 2026: 33,027 km of common-carrier lines, 997 km of tie-in connectivity and 779 km of dedicated lines. Of that, 27,905 km was operational and 8,551 km under construction or partly commissioned, both figures including sub-transmission lines owned by city gas companies. A year earlier the picture was different: snapshots from 2024 and 2025 gave 33,500 to 34,233 km authorised and roughly 25,430 km operational, so about 2,500 km came into operation in nine months. Treat the total as a moving target that grows with each new authorisation. The government's own stated ambition is a ~35,000 km network, part of a broader target to raise natural gas's share of India's energy mix from roughly 6.3% to 15% by 2030.

Alongside natural gas, India's petroleum product pipeline network runs to roughly 18,858 km operational plus about 1,401 km under construction, according to industry-press coverage of PNGRB's own figures. And as of August 2026, PNGRB authorised three new common-carrier LPG pipeline projects — all to be built by GAIL, together worth roughly ₹7,000 crore and covering about 1,800 km — which the regulator says will take the country's authorised LPG pipeline network from about 7,700 km to nearly 9,500 km, a jump of roughly 23.5%. The three new LPG lines: Cherlapally (Telangana) to Nagpur (Maharashtra), 556 km; Jhansi (Uttar Pradesh) to Sitarganj (Uttarakhand), 611 km; and Shikrapur (Maharashtra) to Goa and Hubli (Karnataka), 633 km. PNGRB frames the LPG push explicitly as shifting fuel movement off road tankers and onto pipe, citing lower logistics cost, less road congestion, and reduced emissions.

Five pipelines under construction right now, and where they actually stand

Behind the aggregate numbers in §3 are specific, named projects, several of them generating the ARUL Act notification traffic in §5 as they proceed. Their own reported schedules are worth stating plainly, because more than one has already slipped.

  • Kochi–Koottanad–Bengaluru–Mangaluru Pipeline (KKBMPL), GAIL, natural gas — Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Puducherry. Authorised length 1,104 km; by GAIL’s 13 April 2026 disclosure, 919 km laid and 657 km commissioned, up from 884 km and 628 km in January. Completion has moved from December 2025 to March 2026 (3 January 2026) and then to September 2026 (April 2026). GAIL's stated reason both times is delay in getting "hindrance-free" work fronts, i.e. exactly the kind of land-access friction the ARUL Act process exists to resolve. September 2026 has passed; this piece found no report that the pipeline was completed.
  • Srikakulam–Angul Gas Pipeline (SAGPL), GAIL, natural gas — Andhra Pradesh, Odisha. Total 744 km (422 km main line, 322 km spur). The main line was commissioned 26 September 2025. On the spur, 280 of 322 km were complete in January 2026, with the rest held up by forest working permission for a 56 km stretch; completion was revised from December 2025 to June 2026. GAIL’s 2025-26 annual report says about 470 km of the 744 km was commissioned during that year. This piece found no report that the spur was finished by June.
  • Jagdishpur–Haldia–Bokaro–Dhamra Pipeline (JHBDPL) / Pradhan Mantri Urja Ganga, GAIL, natural gas — Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Odisha. Together with the Barauni–Guwahati line, an authorised 3,306 km network; GAIL reported 97.6% of it laid and 96.6% operational in April 2025. The two unfinished sections, Durgapur–Haldia and Dhamra–Haldia, have been rescheduled three times: from March 2025 to December 2025, to March 2026, and on 25 March 2026 to September 2026, each time citing right-of-use and “fisheries front” availability. This piece found no report that they were completed by September.
  • Mumbai–Nagpur–Jharsuguda Pipeline (MNJPL), GAIL, natural gas — Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Odisha. About 1,707 km, a PM GatiShakti priority project, first targeted for full commissioning by 31 December 2025. By GAIL’s 31 January 2026 disclosure, the Mumbai–Nagpur section and the Chhattisgarh and Odisha stretches of Nagpur–Jharsuguda were operational, and work on the Nagpur–Jabalpur spur was reported finished in March 2026. The remaining Maharashtra stretch of Nagpur–Jharsuguda was rescheduled on 25 March 2026 for progressive commissioning by 30 June 2026, with PNGRB’s approval for the extension sought. PNGRB has separately mapped 17 Geographical Areas across roughly 60 districts along this route for future city gas distribution.
  • Mehsana–Bhatinda Natural Gas Pipeline Network (including the HRRL Refinery Spurline), GAIL, natural gas — Gujarat, Rajasthan, Punjab. The core network is long-established; current ARUL Act activity (a Section 3(1) notice dated 25 November 2025) concerns a specific spurline extension to the HPCL-Rajasthan Refinery, not the whole network.

Individual pipeline lengths, commissioning percentages, and revised completion dates are drawn from trade press (Indian Infrastructure, PSU Connect, Indian Masterminds, InvestyWise, Machine Maker, PSU Watch) reporting GAIL's own investor and project-update disclosures; this piece did not independently verify these against GAIL's own filings. The KKBMPL September 2026 date, which the first version of this piece reported as unconfirmed, is now the date in GAIL’s April 2026 disclosure as reported. Every completion date in this section falls before this update; none has a completion report this piece could find as of 9 October 2026.

What doesn't follow from any of this

None of this shows the ARUL Act process itself is being misused, delayed unfairly, or applied inconsistently — the Act's basic design (a narrower right-of-user rather than outright acquisition, a notified 21-day objection window) is a real, longstanding legal accommodation, and nothing in this piece's research found evidence it isn't being followed as written. The pipeline schedule slippages in §4 are reported by the companies themselves in their own investor and project disclosures, not uncovered by this piece — they are a normal feature of large linear infrastructure, not a specific finding of mismanagement. And the state-by-state table in §5 should not be read as a complete or authoritative register of pipeline right-of-user activity in any of the 21 states/UTs listed: it is a lower bound, built from what open web search happened to surface, and several genuinely active projects (any pipeline whose land-acquisition notices simply weren't written up by a secondary source this piece's search could find) are certainly missing from it entirely.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should publish a searchable register of every Section 3(1) and Section 6(1) notice under the 1962 Right of User Act, with S.O. number, pipeline, operator, fluid, state, district and village, so the roughly 700 notices can be traced to projects without reconstructing them from secondary write-ups.
  • PNGRB should publish its pipeline data-bank as a dated, machine-readable series with authorised, operational and under-construction lengths for each pipeline, so totals such as 33,500, 33,764, 34,233 and 34,803 km can be reconciled snapshot by snapshot.
  • GAIL should publish, for each pipeline under construction, the original and every revised completion date with the stated reason, so slippage on KKBMPL, the Srikakulam–Angul spur, JHBDPL and the last stretch of Mumbai–Nagpur–Jharsuguda can be tracked from the company rather than from conflicting trade reports, and so that a target date that passes is followed by either a commissioning notice or a new date.
  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should publish, for each Section 3(1) notice, the number of objections received in the 21-day window and how they were disposed of, so the right-of-user process can be assessed on its record and not only on its design.
  • The Ministry of Environment should show on PARIVESH the current stage and outstanding conditions of the forest clearance for the 56 km stretch of the Srikakulam–Angul spur line, so the cause of its revised completion date can be followed from the clearance record itself.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Sources and caveats

This piece's research was conducted entirely via WebSearch; every primary or aggregator source for the underlying gazette notifications — egazette.gov.in (including specific PDF paths supplied directly for this research), pngrb.gov.in (including its natural-gas-pipeline data-bank PDFs), gazettetracker.com, teamleaseregtech.com, and advocategandhi.com (a legal-explainer site that carries the fullest write-ups of individual ARUL Act notices found in this research) — returned a network-level block (EGRESS_BLOCKED) on every direct-fetch attempt, including a retry via the Wayback Machine, which is itself unreachable from this environment. §5 was first built from WebSearch’s summaries of how secondary sources describe these notifications. On 28 October 2026 its S.O. numbers, operators and fluids were checked against the Gazette’s own text through the SansadSaar index of the archive.org Gazette of India collection: the reused numbers S.O. 1303(E) and S.O. 4224(E) both belong to the Reliance ethane line, the Cauvery Asset row was corrected to natural gas, the Krishna district row was identified, and the Mehsana–Bhatinda operator was corrected to GSPL India Gasnet. Rows without a number were not all checked. The §3–4 pipeline-network and project-status figures rest on industry trade press (Indian Infrastructure, PSU Connect, Indian Masterminds, Business Standard, Free Press Journal, and others) citing PNGRB's and GAIL's own releases, which this piece also could not read directly. The single highest-confidence claim in this piece is the distinction in §1 between PNGRB authorisation and ARUL Act land acquisition, corroborated by independent legal-explainer sources describing the same two-Act structure consistently. The single lowest-confidence claims are any specific S.O./G.S.R. number or exact date in §5, several of which could not be corroborated twice and one of which (the unresolved "MSAE to MSNAA" entry) could not be resolved to a real pipeline name at all. A reader who needs a verified, complete register of ARUL Act pipeline notifications for a real purpose (legal, land-planning, or investment) should search egazette.gov.in's Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas archive directly, or PNGRB's own data-bank PDFs, rather than rely on this piece.
