The Two Biggest Parties in Parliament Ask About Everything. Everyone Else Doesn't.

Rank India's 51 parliamentary parties by how many questions their MPs asked in the 18th Lok Sabha and current Rajya Sabha sessions, and BJP wins, then INC — by margins that just track how many MPs each party has. That ranking answers a boring question. A different cut of the same 59,381 questions — each party's focus on a specific ministry, divided by what its overall size would predict — answers a more interesting one: which parties are actually specialists. The two biggest are not. Nearly everyone smaller is, sharply, on one or two ministries tied to exactly what you'd guess from their base.

That is nearly the whole story if you stop here — and stopping here is exactly what a raw ranking invites you to do. BJP holds roughly 240 of 543 Lok Sabha seats and a large Rajya Sabha bloc; asking twice as many questions as the next party is not a finding, it is arithmetic.

A better question: not how much, but how disproportionate

Divide each party's share of questions on a given ministry by that party's overall share of all questions, and you get lift: a value of 1.0 means the party asks about that ministry exactly as often as its overall size would predict; a value of 3.0 means three times more than predicted. Lift strips out size and isolates focus. To keep the numbers meaningful, the cut below is restricted to parties with at least 100 total questions and at least 10 questions on the specific ministry — a party with two total questions can post a mathematically enormous but meaningless lift score on whichever ministry those two happen to fall under.

None of this needs a political-science theory to explain. A party built around India's Scheduled Tribes asks disproportionately about Tribal Affairs. A Muslim League asks disproportionately about Minority Affairs. A coastal Andhra Pradesh party with a long shipping and ports policy history asks disproportionately about Ports, Shipping and Waterways. The pattern is legible because it is exactly what each party's own base would predict — which is precisely what makes it a real signal and not noise.

The confirming contrast: BJP and INC specialise in nothing

The two largest parties' highest lift anywhere in the entire ministry list is 1.9× and 1.4× respectively — nowhere near the 2.4–7.6× range the parties above hit on their signature issue. BJP's single highest-lift ministry is Parliamentary Affairs (1.9×, 49 questions); INC's is Road Transport & Highways (1.4×, 347 questions). Both are modest deviations from perfectly proportional, not specialisations.

That is the actual finding, and it is structural rather than partisan: a party large enough to need MPs covering every portfolio ends up asking about everything roughly in proportion to its size, because covering the floor is the job. A smaller or regional party, with fewer MPs and a narrower base, concentrates its limited number of questions on the two or three ministries that matter most to the people it represents. Size doesn't just win the raw count — it flattens the profile. Specialisation is what's left over for everyone who isn't trying to cover everything.

Grouping by what parties emphasise, not by size

Clustering all 51 parties by their relative ministry emphasis (not raw volume, which is dominated by every party's shared interest in Health, Railways and Education) sorts them into four groups: a 24-party mainstream bloc spanning both the ruling party and the principal national opposition, whose ministry profiles are broad and unremarkable by construction; a 13-party bloc with a shared lean toward minority-affairs- and left-coded ministries; an 8-party bloc of smaller regional and first-time parties with less-defined profiles; and BRS standing alone, its lone standout being a 200× lift on Prime Minister's Office questions — a single-question artefact of the size filter above, included here only to show why that filter exists.

What this dataset does and doesn't cover

  • One term, not history. This covers the 18th Lok Sabha (since June 2024) and Rajya Sabha sessions 265–271 over the same calendar window, through August 2026. It says nothing about whether these specialisation patterns hold in earlier Lok Sabhas.
  • 138 of 34,720 Lok Sabha questions (0.4%) are excluded from every party breakdown above — they couldn't be matched to a roster member by name (two MPs' name-spellings didn't reconcile against the roster used).
  • A jointly-tabled question is credited to its first-listed MP's party only, to avoid one question inflating several parties' counts at once. Real cross-party co-tabling happens more than this framing shows: the underlying data logs 7,428 distinct pairs of MPs who have jointly asked a question together across party lines.
  • Ministry, not full question text, is the primary unit here. The Lok Sabha's own listing API exposes each question's subject line and ministry but not the full question text (that sits in a per-question PDF); the Rajya Sabha's does give full question text, but not answer text either way — both chambers publish the actual answer only as a PDF.
  • Lift on a small base is noise, not signal — which is why every party/ministry pair shown above passes a ≥100-question, ≥10-question floor, and why party-level counts for very small parties (a handful of nominated MPs, independents) are shown for completeness but not treated as specialisation claims.

What a Parliament Question actually is

Per Rules 32–54 of the Lok Sabha's Rules of Procedure (Rules 47–50 in the Rajya Sabha), an MP files a notice — capped at 150 words, barring policy-level or sub-judice matters, at most five notices a day — at least 15 days ahead of the sitting (10 for a short-notice question). A starred question gets an oral answer and floor follow-up, capped at one per MP per sitting day; an unstarred question gets only a written reply on the record. Across this dataset, unstarred questions dominate heavily — 31,944 of 34,720 in the Lok Sabha (92%), 22,552 of 24,661 in the Rajya Sabha (91%) — which matches the procedural cap on oral questions, not a lack of MP interest.
