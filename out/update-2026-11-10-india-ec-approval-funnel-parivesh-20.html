The rest are activity on land that already holds a clearance: 22% are re-appraisals (mostly minor-mineral mining leases expiring and re-cleared on the same plots), plus transfers of EC to new owners, expansions of existing plants, and amendments. So the 54,406 grants below overstate the number of distinct new projects cleared. The EC approval funnel: where it actually loses proposals

Two of the funnel's stages account for almost the entire gap between 113,970 filed and 54,406 granted, and neither one is the applicant getting turned down. Auto-delisting removes 25,061 proposals (22% of everything filed) before they ever reach a substantive review — PARIVESH's own system flags an application as inactive, typically because the proponent stopped responding to a query or let a procedural deadline lapse, and closes it administratively. That is a process failure on the applicant's side, not an environmental judgement on the project. Pipeline backlog is the second big loss: of the 88,909 live applications, 27,548 (31%) are still sitting in one of the three review stages — Terms of Reference (15,869), screening (7,197) or appraisal (4,482) — simply waiting their turn, neither approved nor rejected. Add auto-delisting and backlog together and they account for roughly 46% of everything filed; genuine rejection, by contrast, is a rounding error on the total — just 2,553 of 113,970 proposals (2.2%).

That pattern reframes what "88.7% grant rate" actually measures. It is not the odds a random new proposal has of being approved — it's the odds a proposal has of being approved once it survives long enough to reach a decision at all. The binding constraint on India's EC pipeline, on this data, is administrative throughput and applicant follow-through, not environmental scrutiny turning projects away. A reader using EC data to judge how permissive or restrictive India's clearance regime is should weight the auto-delisting and backlog numbers at least as heavily as the 88.7% headline — a system that clears almost everything it finishes reviewing, but takes a long time to finish reviewing a third of what's in front of it and administratively drops a fifth of what's filed, is a different story than either "clearances are a rubber stamp" or "clearances are a bottleneck" told on its own.

Where Environmental Clearances land: the state × sub-sector view

The EC register is the earliest public, machine-readable signal of where physical investment is headed — before IEM implementation, before commissioning. Flagship-project absences (e.g. POSCO-JSW JV, RRPCL) are as informative as the grants. Split by state and EIA sub-sector, the 113,970 proposals are anything but evenly spread: mining of minerals alone accounts for 78,867 of them (69%), and in most sub-sectors a single state holds a large share of the grants.

Why the state pattern isn't random

The state × sub-sector split matters because it separates two very different kinds of clearance activity that a national grant-rate number blends together. States like Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Rajasthan carry disproportionately large EC registers because of minor-mineral mining — and mining clearances have a structural reason to recur on the same plots: a mining lease has a fixed term, and its renewal or re-appraisal shows up in the register as a new proposal even though no new land is being disturbed. That's a large share of what sits behind the 49% fresh-versus-reuse split. Gujarat and Maharashtra's registers, by contrast, skew toward industrial and infrastructure ECs — plants, corridors, ports, and in the table above 56.2% of chemicals grants and a third or more of building and township grants — which are more likely to represent genuinely new capacity landing rather than an existing lease renewing itself on paper.

That distinction is the reason to treat the 88.7% grant rate as a starting point rather than a conclusion. A state with a high grant rate built mostly from mining re-appraisals is not attracting more new investment than a state with a lower rate built mostly from first-time industrial ECs — the two numbers describe different things wearing the same label. Anyone using EC counts as a leading indicator of where capital is actually going needs the fresh-versus-reuse split and the sub-sector mix, not just the state total, or the read is backwards.

What the four funnels say

On the EC basis, all four registers grant most of what they decide: Wildlife 86.2%, CRZ 85.1%, Forest 69.7%. The funnel above holds across the board — the losses are delisting and waiting, not rejection. Wildlife is still the real bottleneck, but as a queue: 4,319 of 4,919 sit in process, most “Pending at Proponent” (the ball is with the project applicant, not the regulator) — projects near protected areas stall here for the National/State Board for Wildlife recommendation. Forest: 88% are fresh Form-A diversions, but 40% of the register is auto-delisted, so the live pool is much smaller than the headline count. CRZ is small and coastal (17 states/UTs) — the gate for ports, coastal industry and waterfront projects.

Forest Clearance is a second land-creation channel

Every Environmental Clearance answers “may this project pollute?”; Forest Clearance answers “may this project take this forest land?” — so it is the one register that measures land quantum. What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) should publish, on PARIVESH, a standard list of process stages and map all its raw status values to them, so filed, delisted, pending, rejected and granted counts can be read directly and reconcile exactly, rather than being consolidated from 56 status values by outside analysts.
  • MoEFCC should publish the rules for PARIVESH auto-delisting and quarterly counts of delisted proposals by reason — unanswered query, lapsed deadline, duplicate or other — by state and sector, for all four registers, so the 25,061 EC and 11,723 Forest Clearance delistings can be traced to specific procedural failures that applicants and appraisal committees can address.
  • MoEFCC should add the date of every status change to the PARIVESH 2.0 open API and publish median days spent at Terms of Reference, screening and appraisal, by state and sector, updated quarterly, so the backlog of 27,548 EC proposals, and the Forest, Wildlife and CRZ registers, can be tracked as waiting time, not only as a single pipeline snapshot.
  • MoEFCC should report EC grants and grant rates for fresh projects separately from re-appraisals, transfers, expansions and amendments, so a headline such as 54,406 grants or an 88.7% grant rate is not read as new capacity when only 49% of proposals are fresh.
  • MoEFCC should add a field on PARIVESH linking each re-appraisal or renewal to the original clearance and mining-lease identifier, so that a minor-mineral lease renewing on the same plot is not counted as a new proposal in state registers.
  • The State Environment Impact Assessment Authorities of Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Rajasthan should publish lists of minor-mineral ECs with lease identifier and lease expiry date, so that the recurring re-appraisals that inflate their registers can be forecast and separated from fresh mining proposals.
  • MoEFCC should validate the user-agency field in PARIVESH and correct existing records, so an individual's name does not appear as the top Forest Clearance user agency with 1,697 proposals and agency-wise counts can be trusted.
  • MoEFCC should add range checks on the forest-area field and publish corrections for implausible entries, so a single record of about 74,000 hectares cannot distort the 1.05 million hectare pipeline figure that users of the register will quote.
  • The National Board for Wildlife's Standing Committee should report, for proposals awaiting wildlife clearance, the time spent pending with the proponent and with the state and national boards separately, so the 4,319 cases in process can be attributed to whoever is actually holding them.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
