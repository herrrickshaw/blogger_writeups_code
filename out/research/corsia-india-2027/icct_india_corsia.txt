### SUMMARY

This study develops a greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions inventory for commercial aviation in India in 2023 and combines it with market growth projections to estimate India’s offset requirements under the International Civil Aviation Organization’s Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation (CORSIA). The inventory estimates that flights departing India generated 30 Mt of well-to-wake (WTW) carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) emissions in 2023. As India is one of the world’s largest and fastest-growing aviation markets, our results suggest that Indian carriers will face a growing compliance burden under CORSIA, while current CORSIA-eligible fuel policies are unlikely to substantially reduce offsetting needs. Less than 10% of India’s total offset requirement is projected to be met through the adoption of CORSIA-eligible fuels, and the remaining 90% will be required to be addressed through the purchase of offset credits. This heavy reliance on carbon credits could expose Indian carriers to offset price volatility, restricted credit availability, and uncertainty regarding credit sustainability.

Our findings demonstrate a need for a stronger sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) policy in India. India currently has a non-binding SAF target that covers only international flights. Converting this target into a mandate, together with a clear, post-2030 SAF blending trajectory, would support the scale-up of domestic SAF production and reduce the carriers’ reliance on carbon credits to comply with CORSIA. In addition, given that domestic flights represent the largest share of India’s departures and more than half of the country’s aviation emissions, aviation decarbonization policies in India could be expanded to cover not only international flights but also domestic aviation. Airport infrastructure expansion and policies aimed to increase regional air connectivity could be aligned with environmental safeguards such as emissions caps, limits on aircraft movements, and low-carbon ground operations to mitigate aviation emissions growth.

### INTRODUCTION

India’s aviation sector has expanded rapidly over the past decade, driven by passenger growth, record aircraft orders, and significant infrastructure investments (Walsh, 2025). This growth is expected to continue as Indian carriers expand their fleets and the country increases its airport capacity. As a consequence of air traffic expansion, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from aviation are also expected to increase. Although aviation currently represents a small share of India’s transport emissions, decarbonizing the sector remains challenging due to the limited availability and high cost of low- carbon technologies and sustainable aviation fuels (SAFs).

It is unclear to what extent aviation emissions will increase in the coming years and what this growth implies for India’s obligations under the International Civil Aviation Organization’s (ICAO’s) Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation (CORSIA), which will go into effect for India in 2027. Understanding the scale and drivers of India’s future aviation emissions is essential for assessing the country’s expected offsetting requirements and for informing policies aimed at mitigating the sector’s emissions.

This paper addresses this gap by presenting a GHG emissions inventory for commercial aviation in India for 2023 and estimating the country’s offsetting requirements under CORSIA based on projected market growth from 2027 to 2035. Using this inventory, we provide a comprehensive overview of India’s aviation emissions, examine the key characteristics of its air transport market, and compare them with global aviation trends. The 2023 results serve as the baseline for projections and for calculating future CORSIA offsetting requirements. By quantifying the magnitude and drivers of future emissions and the resulting CORSIA obligations, this study aims to support policy decisions as India prepares for CORSIA’s mandatory compliance phase.

### BACKGROUND

According to the International Air Transport Association (2025), Indian carriers transported more than 170 million passengers on domestic and international flights in 2024. The country’s air traffic in 2024 surpassed pre-pandemic levels by 10.9%, making India the world’s third-largest market that year in terms of departing passengers.

India’s air transportation market is expected to continue growing. Indian air carriers have placed orders for around 1,000 new aircraft to be delivered over the next 10–15 years, which would more than double the country’s current commercial fleet of about 800 aircraft (Ministry of Civil Aviation, 2024a). In addition, the number of airports has more than doubled over the last decade, from 74 in 2014 to 157 in 2024, and the country plans to reach 350–400 airports by 2047 (Ministry of Civil Aviation, 2024b).

The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (2024) estimated that aviation represents 4% of GHG emissions from the transport sector in India. Although this share is relatively small compared with road transport, which accounts for 94% according to the same source, the decarbonization trajectories of these modes differ significantly: while the electrification of passenger cars and trucks has steadily advanced in India and fuel efficiency standards have become more stringent (Bhatt & Kodjak, 2025), low-carbon technologies for aviation remain globally underdeveloped and expensive (Mithal & Rutherford, 2023).

The rapid expansion of India’s air transportation will increase the challenge of mitigating the sector’s environmental impact and complying with the global aviation industry’s goal of reaching net-zero carbon emissions by 2050. This target, known as the Long Term Aspirational Goal (LTAG), was implemented by ICAO in 2022 for international traffic (ICAO, 2022a). LTAG reaffirms commitments made by governments and industry stakeholders in 2016 to mitigate the climate impact of international aviation via ICAO’s offsetting initiative, CORSIA (Mithal & Rutherford, 2023).

CORSIA implementation is structured in three phases: a pilot phase (2021–2023), a first phase (2024–2026), and a second phase (2027–2035). While the two initial phases were voluntary, the final phase is mandatory for most ICAO Member States, with exceptions for certain developing countries and those that account for a minimal share of international aviation activities. In total, 134 countries are expected to participate in the second phase (ICAO, 2025c). India did not volunteer to participate in the first two phases and will be subject to offsetting requirements from 2027 onward. ICAO set the CORSIA baseline used to calculate the offsetting requirement each year at 85% of 2019 emissions through 2035, when the scheme expires (ICAO, 2024).

CORSIA obligations apply exclusively to international aviation routes. Emissions from these flights are attributed to the state administering the aircraft operator. The monitoring, reporting, and verification requirements under CORSIA are applicable only to aircraft operators whose annual carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from international operations exceed 10,000 tonnes (t), using aircraft with maximum take-off masses greater than 5,700 kg (ICAO, 2023).

Penalties for noncompliance with CORSIA obligations are not defined directly by ICAO and are instead established by national authorities of participating countries. Some jurisdictions have incorporated CORSIA requirements and noncompliance penalties into their domestic regulatory frameworks, including Brazil (Resolução N o 743, 2024), Canada (Canadian Aviation Regulations SOR/96-433, 1996), the European Union (Directive 2003/87/EC, 2003, Article 16), and the United Kingdom (The Air Navigation [Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation] Order 2021, 2021). These penalties generally include fines and, in some cases, possible operational restrictions or suspension for aircraft operators failing to comply.

In India, the Directorate General of Civil Aviation is expected to carry out enforcement of CORSIA obligations, although a comprehensive national policy defining noncompliance penalties had not yet been formally adopted as of the publication of this study.

### METHODS

This section outlines how we estimated GHG emissions from India’s commercial aviation sector in 2023 and projected the country’s CORSIA offsetting requirements from 2027 to 2035.

First, we developed a GHG emissions inventory for commercial aviation in India in 2023, using it to establish a detailed baseline of emissions from domestic and international flights in India’s aviation market. We further compared India’s emissions with those of other countries, analyzing how emission concentrations varied by airport, aircraft class, route, and airline. Establishing this baseline is essential for understanding the current scale and characteristics of India’s aviation emissions.

Second, we used this baseline inventory to project aviation emissions from 2027 to 2035. The projection applied traffic growth assumptions, aircraft energy intensity projections, and assumptions about the use of CORSIA-eligible fuels. By doing so, we were able to estimate how emissions might evolve under expected market growth and assess how much projected improvements in aircraft efficiency and eligible fuel use could reduce future emissions.

Third, we calculated India’s expected CORSIA offsetting requirements from 2027 to 2035. Because CORSIA applies only to eligible international routes, we identified international flights operated by Indian carriers and estimated the portion of projected emissions subject to CORSIA. We then compared these emissions with the CORSIA baseline to estimate offsetting obligations and determine the potential compliance burden for Indian aviation.

Together, these steps provide a quantitative basis for assessing the scale of India’s future CORSIA obligations and the role that aircraft efficiency improvements and CORSIA-eligible fuels could play in reducing offsetting requirements. ### **MODELING GHG EMISSIONS FROM INDIAN AVIATION IN 2023**

Our assessment of India’s aviation emissions was based on the International Council on Clean Transportation’s (ICCT’s) 2023 global emissions inventory (Eufrásio et al., forthcoming). We estimated emissions using the ICCT’s Jet and Turboprop Simulations for Trajectory-based Emissions and Meteorological effects (JETSTREAM) model (Benoit et al., 2025), which models fuel burn and emissions using inputs such as flight trajectory, meteorology, aircraft performance, and airline operational data.

The inventory accounts for CO2, methane (CH4), and nitrous oxide (N2O), which are combined and reported as CO2-equivalent (CO2e) emissions. It assesses CO2e emissions from domestic and international commercial flights departing from India in 2023. Emissions are calculated on a well-to-wake (WTW) basis, which includes both jet fuel combustion emissions and upstream emissions generated during jet fuel production. We assumed that jet fuel emits 3.84 kg WTW CO2e per kg of fuel, based on the CORSIA life-cycle emissions factor for jet fuel of 89 g CO2e/MJ (ICAO, 2025b) and a heating value of 43.15 MJ per kg of jet fuel (Eurocontrol, 2023).

More information on flight trajectory and airline operational data is provided in Appendix B.

### **PROJECTING GROWTH IN AVIATION EMISSIONS OVER 2027–2035**

For the second part of the analysis, we used the GHG emissions inventory to assess India’s potential offsetting requirements under CORSIA. We used the total revenue tonne kilometers ¹ (RTK) from Indian air carriers in 2023 to project emissions for the 2027–2035 period. In order to maintain consistency with CORSIA, we only accounted for tank-to-wake (TTW) CO2 emissions (i.e., only combustion emissions), calculated based on the assumption that 3.16 g CO2 are produced for each gram of fuel burned.

To estimate India’s CORSIA-covered emissions, we filtered the 2023 global inventory for international routes operated by Indian carriers where both the departing and arriving countries participate in CORSIA (voluntarily until 2026 and as mandated from 2027 onward). We used the same criteria to filter the ICCT’s Global Aviation Carbon Assessment inventory for 2019 (Graver et al., 2020) to determine CORSIA’s baseline emission level.

Using route–airline–aircraft-level RTK data from the 2023 global inventory, we projected India’s annual RTK for 2027–2035 by applying ICAO’s regional growth rates. These growth rates were derived from the ICAO air traffic compound annual growth rate (CAGR) projection for different departing and arriving regions under the 2021 Baseline: Mid scenario (ICAO, 2021). The CAGR was derived from ICAO’s long-term traffic forecasts models, which incorporate updated socio-economic projections, including changes in real gross domestic product and population following the COVID-19 pandemic. The forecasts also account for short-term disruptions and alternative recovery trajectories using adjustment factors aligned with each scenario. Overall, the projections reflect assumptions of a gradual recovery in air travel demand and a return to pre-pandemic traffic trends (ICAO, 2022c).

Based on the annual RTK projections, we estimated total annual CO2 emissions for 2027–2035 by comparing India’s projected emissions growth against the global sector- wide benchmark used under CORSIA. This required two separate emission estimates: global emissions from CORSIA-covered routes to establish the sector benchmark and India-specific emissions to capture India’s actual fleet and operating conditions.

For the global benchmark, we converted the RTK projections into fuel demand based on the energy intensity values from the Current Commitment scenario in the ICCT’s _Vision 2050_ study (Zheng et al., 2025), summarized in Table 1. We used global energy intensity values to estimate each country’s 2027–2035 emissions from the CORSIA-covered routes—except for India, for which we derived India-specific energy intensity values to better reflect the country’s fleet and operation conditions. Using the ICCT’s 2023 global aviation emissions inventory (Eufrásio et al., forthcoming), we derived India-specific energy intensity values from total fuel burn and RTK data for international flights operated by Indian carriers. We then adjusted the values using the global average annual reduction rate of 1.3%, reflecting the historical pace of new aircraft technology and fleet renewal absent additional policy intervention. ²

### **CALCULATING CORSIA OFFSET REQUIREMENTS**

We then estimated India’s CORSIA offset requirement during the mandatory phase of CORSIA from 2027 to 2035. For India, the offset requirement corresponds to the CORSIA-covered emissions from Indian carriers that exceed the CORSIA baseline (85% of 2019 emissions from CORSIA-covered international flights). Emissions above the baseline must be offset by purchasing CORSIA Eligible Emissions Units (EEUs) or reduced through the use of CORSIA-eligible fuels (CEFs). ³ We used Equation 1 to calculate the offset requirements, following the methodology described in ICAO Resolution A41-22 (ICAO, 2022b).

_Offseti,y_ = _%sectoral,y_ × _Ei,y × SGRy_ \+ _%individual,y_ × _Ei,y_ × _AGRi,y_ (Equation 1)

Where:

_Offseti,y_ is the offset required by airplane operator _i_ at year _y_ , measured in tonnes of CO2;

_Ei,y_ is the CORSIA-covered emissions of airplane operator _i_ at year _y_ , measured in tonnes of CO2 _;_

_SGRy_ is the sectoral growth factor at year _y_ , which is identical for all airplane operators and determined based on the aggregated emissions of all international flights; it is calculated as the difference between the CO2 emissions at year _y_ and the CORSIA baseline CO2 emissions, divided by the CO2 emissions at year _y_ ;

_AGRi,y_ is the individual growth factor of airplane operator _i_ at year _y,_ which depends only on the operator’s individual CO2 emissions increase; it is calculated as the difference between the CO2 emissions of operator _i_ at year _y_ and the CORSIA baseline CO2 emissions, divided by the CO2 emissions of operator _i_ at year _y_ ;

_%sectoral,y_ and _%individual,y_ are, respectively, the sectoral and individual weight at year _y;_ for CORSIA’s second phase (2027–2035), the weighting between the sectoral and individual components is as follows:

» From 2027 to 2032, sectoral weight is 100% and individual weight is 0%.

» From 2033 to 2035, sectoral weight is 85% and individual weight is 15%.

We calculated the _Ei,y_ and _AGRi,y_ by country rather than by individual airplane operator to estimate India’s total annual offset obligation instead of the obligations of individual airlines. The RTK projections employed in our analysis are based on regional-level growth rates, and the emission projections do not account for the distinct growth patterns of individual airlines, which supports the use of country-level averages.

To calculate the final offset requirements, we deducted Indian carriers’ emission reductions attributable to the use of CEFs, as described by Equation 2. The emission reductions are from international flights operated by Indian carriers that depart from regions with active SAF mandates or targets. We detail the calculation of the emission reductions attributed to CEFs in the next subsection.

_Final Offseti,y_ = _Offseti,y_ − _CEF Emission Reductioni,y_ (Equation 2)

Given that non-binding targets are rarely fully achieved (InfluenceMap, 2023), we also distinguished the emissions reduction impact from a country’s SAF mandates and non-binding targets to assess their respective impacts on the final CORSIA offset requirement. Additionally, to evaluate the extent to which India’s current SAF target may contribute to alleviating its CORSIA compliance obligations, we isolated the share of projected emissions reduction attributable specifically to India’s SAF adoption targets on international flights departing from India (1% SAF blending by 2027, 2% by 2028, and 5% by 2030 for international flights; Ministry of Civil Aviation, 2025).

### **CALCULATING EMISSIONS REDUCTION WITH THE ADOPTION OF CORSIA-ELIGIBLE FUELS**

We determined the CEF blending ratio of flights departing from a given country based on the country’s SAF policy, including both mandates and non-binding targets, covering Brazil, Canada, China, the European Union, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, New Zealand, Singapore, Thailand, Türkiye, the United Kingdom, the United States, and Vietnam. Global CEF adoption affects the offset requirement calculation of all participating countries through its influence on the sectoral growth rate, while India’s own SAF target primarily affects its national emissions growth rate, which would be factored into the offset requirement calculation starting from 2033, as reflected in the formulas above.

We distinguished the CEF CO2 emissions reduction expected to be achieved through India’s own SAF target and the CEF reduction due to other countries’ SAF targets, which affect flights operated by Indian carriers departing from those locations. The inclusion of the non-binding targets adds uncertainty to the estimation, as each country’s actual SAF uptake may fall below the target levels, leading to lower CEF emissions reduction. Therefore, we calculated the CEF emissions reduction from mandates and non-binding targets separately. We categorized implemented, announced, and proposed legally binding SAF regulations as mandates; these included regulations in Brazil, the European Union, India, Indonesia, Japan, Malaysia, Singapore, South Korea, Türkiye, and the United Kingdom. We categorized other policies as non-binding targets, including those in Canada, China, New Zealand, Thailand, the United States, and Vietnam.

Based on the ICAO database of SAF production facilities (ICAO, n.d.-c), we then estimated the SAF feedstock split according to the projected SAF production capacities of different regions from 2027 to 2035. At the time of our analysis, we referenced SAF production facility information from early 2025. Because the SAF industry is rapidly developing, current production capacity may therefore differ from our estimates. Also, because SAF produced in one region may be exported to others with blending mandates, the future SAF trade flow remains uncertain.

### **CORSIA OFFSET REQUIREMENTS FOR INDIA**

As detailed above, we used a different flight subset from the one adopted in the India emissions inventory to calculate India’s CORSIA offset requirements. For this analysis, we considered international routes operated by Indian carriers where both departing and arriving countries are CORSIA-participating countries. We filtered the 2019 and 2023 ICCT global aviation inventories based on these conditions and determined CORSIA’s baseline emission level by calculating 85% of the 2019 emission value. We then used the 2023 RTK value as the base from which we estimated emissions from 2027 to 2035. For India, estimated net aviation emissions exceed the CORSIA baseline. This surplus portion will be mostly offset by other countries due to the way CORSIA assigns weights when calculating offset requirements: sectoral growth is weighted more heavily than individual airline growth, particularly between 2027 and 2032, when the individual component’s weight is zero. Because aviation activity in India is expected to grow faster than the global sector average, it must offset a smaller share of its total emissions. From 2033 onward, the individual airline growth component is weighted at 15%, which will slightly increase the proportion of India’s offset requirements in relation to total emissions.

Emissions exceeding the baseline that India is not required to abate account for 16.1% of estimated total emissions (16.8 Mt CO2).

Of the 2.8 Mt of projected CEF-related CO2 reductions, 712 kt (26%) are attributable to binding SAF mandates, while 2.1 Mt (74%) result from non-binding targets. The estimated reductions are therefore relatively uncertain, as the majority are driven by non-binding targets. In addition, flights departing from India accounted for 47.8% of CORSIA-covered emissions from Indian carriers in 2023, indicating that approximately half of covered emissions would be directly affected by SAF policies in India.

### DISCUSSION

We find that India is the sixth-largest aviation emitter globally, with domestic narrowbody operations dominating both departures and emissions. Compared with the global average, three aircraft classes (commuter, regional, and narrowbody) exhibited a lower carbon intensity in India, while widebody aircraft performed slightly worse, with a carbon intensity around 2% higher than the global average. These differences likely reflect fleet composition and operational characteristics across aircraft types. Some aircraft types with relatively high carbon intensities account for a larger share of widebody emissions in India and operate at lower load factors or shorter stage lengths, which likely increases the class’s overall carbon intensity.

The results indicate that widebody aircraft exhibited lower carbon intensity on long- haul routes, in line with the global inventory. This trend is likely driven by the higher seat capacity of widebodies, which leads to a higher RPK over the same distance. However, on shorter routes, widebodies are less efficient than narrowbodies, likely because the greater weight of widebodies results in higher fuel burn that is not offset by the higher seat capacity. Narrowbodies showed comparatively better performance on short- to medium-haul routes. Regional aircraft in India diverged from the global pattern: although less fuel efficient than narrowbodies globally on average, India’s regional aircraft had lower emissions than narrowbodies on the shortest routes. This likely reflects the operational and fleet characteristics of regional aircraft in India, as well as the more limited regional aircraft types compared with the global market. In our sample, India had flights operated by only six different regional aircraft types, whereas the global inventory includes more than 40 types.

The growth of the Indian aviation sector observed over the last decade is expected to continue, supported by aircraft orders that will more than double the current fleet over the next 10–15 years (Ministry of Civil Aviation, 2024a). To meet this growing demand, the Indian government plans to at least double the number of airports in the country before 2050 (Ministry of Civil Aviation, 2024b). In parallel, substantial terminal improvements are being deployed in the existing airport network, alongside the expansion of major airports.

Exemplifying this expansion are the two highest-emitting airports in India, DEL and BOM, which combined accounted for more than 45% of CO₂e emissions from India’s departing flights in 2023. Following significant infrastructure enhancements in recent years, including the addition of a fourth runway and taxiway expansions (The Hindu Bureau, 2023), DEL has become one of the busiest airports in Asia: it was the fourth-largest airport in Asia by seat capacity in 2024, offering approximately 90 million airline seats that year (Fulton, 2025). DEL’s operator expects to finalize an

airport infrastructure expansion plan by 2026 (PTI, 2026). BOM, in turn, is among the busiest single-runway airports in the world and handled 55 million passengers in 2024–2025 (Sharma, 2025). Although the airport is constructed within the city limits and cannot expand its physical infrastructure (Awtaney, 2025), BOM’s flight-handling capacity is expected to increase by 2028 with the deployment of advanced air traffic management technology (Sharma, 2025). A second airport serving Mumbai, Navi Mumbai International Airport, was inaugurated in October 2025 and includes space for future growth; it is expected to alleviate operational strain on BOM (Awtaney, 2025; Shah, 2025).

India has expanded regional air connectivity through the UDAN scheme, ⁵ launched in 2016, which aims to make air travel more affordable and accessible by subsidizing air tickets and creating new routes to underserved areas (Ministry of Civil Aviation, 2021). UDAN has implemented approximately 650 routes and connected more than 90 underserved airports, although it has faced some implementation challenges, such as airport underutilization and airline discontinuations (National Herald India, 2025; Thakur, 2025).

India’s government has committed to complying with ICAO’s CORSIA scheme to offset emissions from international flights starting in 2027. Between 2027 and 2035, India’s CORSIA-covered emissions are projected to increase from 9.8 Mt to 12.8 Mt, while the offset requirement is expected to range from 2.6 Mt to 4.6 Mt CO₂. In total, Indian carriers are projected to face a cumulative CORSIA offset obligation of 31.8 Mt CO₂. India’s annual share of the global offset requirement is also projected to increase over this period, from 2.0% in 2027 to 2.9% in 2035, reflecting India’s faster-than-average traffic growth. As a developing economy, India is projected to expand air service and RTKs more rapidly than the global average, leading to higher emissions and a growing contribution to the global offset demand.

The fast growth of India’s aviation sector also poses a challenge for the country’s CORSIA commitments and the industry’s broader climate goals. To decarbonize the aviation sector and align with industrial commitments, India has begun to promote the use of SAF. The government has established SAF blending targets of 1% by 2027, 2% by 2028, and 5% by 2030 for international flights (Ministry of Civil Aviation, 2025). India has also joined ICAO’s Assistance, Capacity-building and Training for Sustainable Aviation Fuels Program to assess the potential for domestic SAF production and adoption in line with CORSIA’s sustainability criteria (ICAO, 2025a). In addition, India amended its Aviation Turbine Fuel Control Order in April 2026 to allow the use of SAF-blended jet fuel (Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas, 2026).

The emission reductions associated with India’s current SAF targets remain modest relative to the scale of projected emissions growth. A 1% SAF blending requirement for all international departures in 2027 is estimated to reduce Indian carriers’ international emissions by approximately 26 kt CO₂, lowering that year’s offset requirement by only 1%. Similarly, the 5% blending target in 2030 would reduce offset obligations by about 147 kt, corresponding to a 4.3% decrease. This limited impact reflects both the low blending levels and the structural scope of the policy.

Taken together, these findings suggest that small-scale, near-term SAF targets would fail to substantially reduce India’s long-term offset obligations under CORSIA. Without mandating higher blending rates or aligning with broader international policy, the majority of compliance will continue to rely on the purchase of carbon credits.

Reliance on offset credits may increase future noncompliance risks, as the limited supply of CORSIA-eligible credits is expected to drive up prices and could constrain some carriers from securing sufficient credits (Harrington, 2025). Over the 2027–2035 period, India’s CORSIA offset obligations would translate to $781 million of cumulative offset purchasing costs under a scenario with sufficient supply of CORSIA EEUs. Under a tighter EEU supply scenario, cumulative compliance costs for Indian carriers could rise to approximately $2.6 billion, a substantially heavier financial burden.

Beyond this market-wide risk, individual carriers are likely to encounter distinct compliance challenges under CORSIA. Our analysis is aggregated at the national level and does not reflect possible heterogeneity across carriers. In practice, carriers differ significantly by fleet composition, financial capacity, and operational scale, leading to structurally different emission profiles and compliance challenges. For example, IndiGo accounts for a large share of India’s domestic capacity and emissions, while Air India’s ongoing fleet transformation (India Brand Equity Foundation, 2025) and the financial constraints faced by carriers such as SpiceJet (Digital, 2025) imply divergent pathways to compliance. As a result, the ability to absorb higher offset costs or invest in in-sector mitigation measures is likely to vary considerably across carriers.

Reducing reliance on offset credits will require greater deployment of in-sector mitigation measures, particularly SAF. India has high potential as a SAF market because of its growing aviation sector and access to abundant feedstocks, such as sugarcane bagasse, wheat straw, rice straw, and municipal solid waste (ICAO, 2025a). Among the various SAF production pathways, HEFA is already commercially deployed at scale globally, while ATJ is particularly promising given India’s well-established ethanol production ecosystem (Das, 2025). In the longer term, India’s combination of abundant agricultural residues and low-cost renewable hydrogen could provide a unique competitive advantage for scaling advanced SAF production pathways such as power- and-biomass-to-liquids (Esposito et al., 2026).

India’s SAF sector has been rapidly expanding, with current activity concentrated in refinery co-processing pathways using used cooking oil and vegetable oil-based feedstocks. In 2025, IndianOil’s Panipat refinery became the first Indian facility to receive CORSIA’s International Sustainability and Carbon Certification for SAF production (Kolkata, 2025), while Hindustan Petroleum Corporation Limited has already demonstrated SAF production at its Visakh refinery through used cooking oil co-processing (ICN Bureau, 2026). At the same time, several larger commercial projects are under development, including HEFA-based facilities operated by Essar Future (Giam, 2026) and Numaligarh Refinery (ChiniMandi, 2026), as well as emerging ATJ pathways from lignocellulosic residues through projects led by GPS Renewables (Harrington, 2024) and TruAlt Bioenergy (Honeywell, 2026). These developments suggest that India’s SAF capacity could expand significantly toward the late 2020s.

To accelerate domestic SAF deployment, India could complement the SAF blending target with supply-side incentives that reduce production costs, improve investment certainty, and support industry development. Such incentives are particularly important for first-of-a-kind SAF facilities, whose production costs are currently estimated to be around 2–5 times higher than those of conventional jet fuel (Navarrete et al., 2024). At the subnational level, Uttar Pradesh is planning to unveil India’s first SAF manufacturing promotion policy, with fiscal and non-fiscal incentives to attract investment (Jha, 2025). Such initiatives could help build SAF production capacity, but a national policy framework can provide a consistent demand signal and support scaling production over time.

Aside from SAF policies and regulations, global practices exemplify other effective measures for mitigating emissions as India’s aviation sector continues to expand.

In particular, airport-level strategies can link sustainability initiatives to increased airport capacity, helping to limit emissions growth. For example, the UK government imposed an annual aircraft movement limit as a condition for the approval of Heathrow Airport’s Terminal 5 (UK Parliament, 2001) while operations at Düsseldorf Airport were restricted to the capacity of a single runway after construction of a second runway, due to environmental limits (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development & International Transport Forum, 2014). In the same vein, Boston’s Logan International Airport was required to comply with an emissions cap as part of a 1999 runway approval (U.S. General Accounting Office, 1998).

### POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS

Based on these findings, we present the following policy recommendations for supporting the long-term decarbonization of India’s aviation sector:

**Convert current SAF blending targets into a binding national SAF mandate with long-term, stable targets.** While the current SAF targets for international flights provide an important early market signal, they have a limited contribution to India’s CORSIA offset obligations because of the low blending levels and the limited policy scope. A binding SAF mandate in India would provide stronger market certainty and help mobilize investments in domestic SAF production. The mandate could specify the obligated parties, eligible fuels, sustainability criteria, reporting requirements, rules for demonstrating compliance, and penalties for noncompliance. Including a post-2030 blending trajectory would be important to provide fuel producers and carriers with greater certainty for long-term investment and planning. Such measures could allow India to progressively increase SAF deployment as production capacity expands, while reducing long-term reliance on offset markets.

**To accompany CORSIA implementation, mandate SAF consumption on domestic air travel.** Domestic aviation accounts for the largest share of departures and emissions from flights departing India and is expected to continue to grow as infrastructure expands, the fleet grows, and regional airport connectivity increases. However, these emissions are not subject to any GHG-related regulation. Introducing a SAF blending mandate or a SAF consumption target for all domestic flights would provide an effective policy mechanism to reduce emissions from India’s domestic aviation sector.

**Link approvals for new and expanded airport capacity to enforceable environmental performance targets.** Flight activity in India is expected to continue growing rapidly, and planned infrastructure expansion is likely to result in substantially higher aviation emissions. Connecting this capacity growth with sustainability requirements at the outset could help increase community acceptance of infrastructure investments. Potential measures include airport-level emission caps, airport-level SAF deployment, zero-emission ground equipment initiatives, and mandatory mitigation actions for other environmental impacts such as local air quality. Experience in other markets suggests that such conditions can be attached to airport expansion approvals rather than left to voluntary mitigation commitments. For India, such safeguards would be especially relevant amid plans for major capacity expansions at high-emitting airports and new infrastructure to relieve congestion in large metropolitan regions.

### CONCLUSION

This study developed a GHG emissions inventory for commercial aviation in India in 2023, which we used to estimate future emissions and calculate India’s CORSIA offset requirements. We also assessed the potential contribution of CORSIA-eligible fuels to reducing the compliance burden on Indian carriers.

Although global aviation had not yet fully returned to pre-pandemic levels in 2023, India’s passenger aviation activity had already surpassed 2019 levels in terms of demand and annual emissions. The rapid expansion of India’s aviation sector has made it a major contributor to aviation emission and a market that will face increasing challenges in meeting the 2050 net-zero target. We estimate that flights departing India generated 30 Mt of WTW CO2e emissions in 2023, making it the sixth-highest emitter in 2023. Domestic aviation made up the majority of India’s departures and over half of the country’s aviation emissions, highlighting the importance of considering domestic aviation in the country’s decarbonization policies, including any future SAF mandate.

India’s proposed SAF targets for international flights had a modest impact on projected emissions growth and are likely to have a minor effect on future CORSIA offset requirements. We estimate that between 2027 and 2035, only 9.2% of India’s total offset requirement would be met through the planned adoption of CEFs; this limited contribution depends on the full implementation of India’s SAF targets and the other binding and non-binding SAF targets considered in the analysis. The remaining 90.8% of the total offset requirement would need to be met through the purchase of CORSIA-eligible offset credits.

Deeper emission reductions will require ambitious and enforceable SAF policies in India. Although India’s current SAF targets provide an important first step, a binding mandate with a stable, long-term trajectory and support for domestic SAF production would contribute to scaling SAF deployment over time. Given continued fleet growth, airport expansion, and regional connectivity policies such as UDAN, extending SAF policy to domestic flights would be important for addressing the largest source of India’s aviation emissions and strengthening in-sector emission reductions.

Finally, airport expansion and regional connectivity policies could consider environmental safeguards. India’s aviation activity is projected to keep growing, and without the adoption of environmental conditions to moderate emissions growth, infrastructure expansion could lead to significantly higher emissions in the future. Measures such as emission caps, limits on aircraft movements, restrictions on runway use, and low-carbon ground operations could pair aviation growth with climate impact mitigation.

Future work could incorporate India’s specific demand and capacity expansion levers, including airport expansion plans, increases in domestic connectivity, and Indian carriers’ fleet growth plans. The current emission projections are based on regional- level growth rates, and a more targeted assessment would help better capture the internal dynamics of Indian aviation and refine estimates of future emissions growth. Additional work is also necessary to better characterize the mix of possible feedstocks for SAF production in India, their availability, and associated conversion costs. Finally, current CORSIA offsetting requirements are estimated at the national level; a future analytical refinement could consider heterogeneity in offset volumes across carriers and the different compliance challenges faced by each carrier.

### APPENDIX B: AVIATION EMISSIONS MODELING DATA AND ASSUMPTIONS

Flight trajectories data were derived from Spire Automatic Dependent Surveillance- Broadcast (ADS-B) data from 2023 (Spire, n.d.). Fuel burn and corresponding emissions from the climb, descent, and cruise phases were obtained from aircraft performance modeling, which was developed using the EUROCONTROL BADA 3 framework (Eurocontrol, n.d.). The fuel burn estimation considered the engine type information extracted from the ICAO Engine Emissions Databank (European Union Aviation Safety Agency, n.d.) and fleet data from IBA Insight (2024).

Fuel burn and emissions from the landing-and-take-off phase were modeled using ICAO’s standardized definitions, considering assumptions based on EPA guidelines (ICAO, 2020). Turboprop and piston engine emissions were estimated using data from EUROCONTROL (European Environment Agency, 2023), U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (1992), and Swiss Federal Office of Civil Aviation (2007).

Airline operational data from commercial aviation, including both passenger flights and dedicated freighters, were obtained from various sources. Flight schedules, passenger load factors, and freight weights were derived from matching and combining flight trajectories from Spire (n.d.), Official Aviation Guide (n.d.) schedules, the T100 dataset (U.S. Department for Transport, n.d.), ICAO Traffic by Flight Stage data (ICAO, n.d.-e), and Ch-aviation (n.d.).

For passenger flights, emissions were allocated between passenger and belly cargo proportionally to their payload share. The payload of passenger transportation was estimated using a default passenger mass of 100 kg (European Committee for Standardization, 2012; International Air Transport Association, n.d.; Smart Freight Centre, 2025), and a furnishing correction factor of 50 kg per seat was applied to account for seat mass (ICAO, n.d.-b; Rutherford et al., 2021). This allocation method follows the approach used in the ICCT’s global aviation emission inventories (Graver et al., 2020; Eufrásio et al., forthcoming). More details on methods and data sources are described in the JETSTREAM documentation (Benoit et al., 2025) and in the ICCT’s 2023 global aviation emissions inventory (Eufrásio et al., forthcoming).
