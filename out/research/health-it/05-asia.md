# 05 — How other Asian countries identify patients and track health data

Research slice for the PAN-vs-ABHA article. Compiled 18 Oct 2026 from 14 standard web searches.

**Labels used below**
- **[V]** = verified this session in a search result (source URL given).
- **[V-1]** = only one source found this session; not cross-checked.
- **[BG]** = background knowledge, NOT verified in this session's searches. Confirm before you publish, or drop it.
- Government sites were not fetched directly. Where a figure comes from a law firm, a news outlet or a trade site, the source is named as such.
- Where a search result listed several URLs without saying which one held a given number, the URLs are given as a group ("one of").

India baseline, as given in the brief: PAN since 1972, compulsory. ABHA since 2021, voluntary. About 96 crore ABHAs, but only about 1.3 lakh of 5.47 lakh registered facilities (about 24%) are connected. The national claims exchange (NHCX) is not mandatory.

## Notes per country

**South Korea.** In Korea the health identity is the national identity. Until May 2024 a patient could get the insurance discount just by giving their resident registration number at reception. Fraud (111,177 cases since 2021) pushed the government the other way: since 20 May 2024 a photo ID is compulsory. A health insurance certificate or mobile ID also counts. Under-19s, emergencies and some return visits are exempt. The mobile NHIS card had no photo, which caused early problems. All claims go to one reviewer, HIRA, over its EDI system, and HIRA's data covers about 50 million people. For India: Korea's argument is about tightening ID checks, not about whether to have a health ID at all. I found no Korean EMR adoption rate (gap).

**Taiwan.** A single payer since 1995 and a smart card since 2004 [BG]. Claims are electronic, with paper under 1% (single NHIA deck, undated). Since 2013, NHI MediCloud has pooled 12 categories of data per patient (drugs, tests and results) so any provider can query them. Primary clinics got access from 22 Nov 2016. NHIA's own estimate is about NT$7.4bn saved on drugs over about 6 years and about NT$2.6bn on tests in a year. These are self-reported. I found no 2023-26 change.

**Japan.** This is the closest parallel to India: a separate national digital ID (My Number) being pushed into health against resistance. The mandate came before adoption. Online eligibility checks became obligatory in principle in April 2023. New paper cards stopped on 2 Dec 2024, and old cards expired by 1 Dec 2025. Yet in Oct 2025 only about 37% of eligibility checks used the card, up from 15.67% in Oct 2024. A single source (ISVD) gives 63.24% for Dec 2025, which conflicts with the 37% figure, so I left it out of the table. Mis-linking scandals hurt public trust. EMR adoption is about 73% for clinics and small hospitals and over 80% for large hospitals (June 2026, Jiji). The 2030 target is near-universal adoption, with cloud EMR subsidies (Oct 2026). Japan is a multi-payer system [BG].

**China.** The national medical insurance e-voucher (QR code in WeChat, Alipay or the NHSA app) had more than 1 billion users by Nov 2023 (China Daily citing NHSA). The 15th Five-Year Plan (2026-30) targets on-spot settlement for over 80% of basic insurance spending, up from about 70% in 2025. I found no 2025 activation count and no information on national EHR standards or a mandate (gap).

**Singapore.** Fix the brief's premise. It is the **Health Information Bill**: first reading 5 Nov 2025, **passed 12 Jan 2026**, and planned to take effect **early 2027**. It is not a "Health Information Act 2025" already in force. It will oblige all licensed providers, including private GPs, labs, radiology and retail pharmacies, to contribute core data (diagnoses, medications, allergies, results) to NEHR. It also bans using NEHR for employment or insurance decisions and lets patients see who accessed their record. So NEHR ran on a mostly voluntary basis for private providers from 2011 to 2026, about 15 years, before Singapore made contribution compulsory. That parallels where ABHA/ABDM is now.

**Thailand.** Since 2002, Universal Coverage has keyed entitlement on the citizen ID [BG]. "30-baht treatment anywhere" (2024-25) removed the rule that tied patients to their registered hospital: 4 pilot provinces in Jan 2024, nationwide from 1 Jan 2025. Patients show only their ID card. The minister cited over 6.5 million users and 15 million visits at 13,000 units (secondary source). I did not find NHSO e-claim mandate details (gap).

**Indonesia.** This is the most direct legal contrast with India. A ministerial regulation (Permenkes 24/2022) made EMR **mandatory for every facility by 31 Dec 2023**, linked to the national SATUSEHAT platform. Compliance evidence is weak. Facility studies in 2024 found preparation still under way and more than 400 fragmented apps. I found no current SATUSEHAT integration count (gap). BPJS Kesehatan is a single payer since 2014 [BG].

**Vietnam.** This is the most aggressive "national ID = health card" case. Under Decree 188/2025 the chip citizen ID or VNeID replaces the paper insurance card, and since 3 Feb 2026 facilities may not demand paper. Chip-ID insurance visits are accepted at 100% of facilities, and 99.5% of facilities are connected to the insurance assessment system. Electronic health records sit inside VNeID: about 33-34 million (the two figures, from Jan and Jul 2026, conflict). About 1,210 of 1,650 hospitals say they have EMR. Figures come from Vietnamese news outlets and are not pinned to a single URL.
