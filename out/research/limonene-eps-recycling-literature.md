# D-limonene and expanded polystyrene ("thermocol"): what the literature says

Prepared 10 October 2026. **Status: a reading of abstracts, patents, project pages and search
summaries, not of full texts.** Publisher pages and PIB-style sources were not all openable from
this session, and OpenAlex (`api.openalex.org`) is blocked here, so no citation counts or
systematic search of the indexed literature were possible. Every figure below is tagged with
where it was seen. Treat the numbers as leads to check against the paper, not as verified.

## 1. Bottom line

- **The core claim is well supported in the lab literature and is old.** Expanded polystyrene
  (EPS) dissolves readily in d-limonene, shrinking to roughly a twentieth of its volume, and the
  polymer can be got back with little loss of molecular weight. The foundational papers are from
  1998 (Noguchi et al.). Nothing here is a new discovery.
- **What dissolves is the polymer, not "chemicals extracted from" it.** The solution is
  polystyrene chains in solvent. Reuse routes are (a) re-precipitate and pelletise polystyrene,
  (b) use the solution directly (fibres, adhesives, coatings), or (c) treat it as a volume-reduction
  step before something else. It does not return styrene monomer; that is pyrolysis or
  depolymerisation, a different technology.
- **The evidence stops at the lab bench and the prototype.** I found no independent
  techno-economic or life-cycle study of a limonene-based EPS process, no published data on
  residual limonene or odour in the recovered polystyrene, and no data on how the solvent ages over
  repeated cycles. Industrial solvent-based EPS recycling that exists (CreaSolv, Polystyvert) uses
  other solvents.
- **Limonene has real drawbacks for a process:** a flash point of about 45 to 48 degrees C
  (flammable liquid, hazard class H226), strong citrus odour, oxidation in air to products that
  sensitise skin, a boiling point (176 degrees C) that makes it hard to strip, and a supply that is
  a citrus-industry by-product of tens of thousands of tonnes a year worldwide.
- **"Orange peel" matters less than the grade of limonene.** Peel oil is mostly limonene, but a
  crude peel extract is not a purified, reusable process solvent.

## 3. The technical picture

**Why it dissolves.** Limonene's solubility parameter sits close to polystyrene's, so the polymer
swells and dissolves quickly at room temperature; EPS is mostly air, which is why the volume
collapses. Related terpenes (p-cymene, terpinene, phellandrene) work too. Of the terpenes tested
in one study p-cymene dissolved more polymer than limonene at 50 degrees C, and dissolution time was
a practical limit: slow terpenes are unsuitable. Hansen-parameter predictions are only about
two-thirds to three-quarters accurate across polymers, so measured data matter more than the
prediction (a 2019 assessment reported in a search summary).

**How much.** Reported capacity ranges widely with temperature and polystyrene type: about 0.26 g/mL
at 25 degrees C in one XPS study, and a much higher figure at 50 to 60 degrees C. One summary says
degradation sets in above about 60 degrees C, which is not an agreed limit and was single-sourced.
No source gave a clean dissolution-kinetics model; one gives a single point of 125 cm3 of EPS in 5 cm3
of limonene within three minutes.

**Polymer quality.** The consistent message is little loss of molecular weight, measured by gel
permeation chromatography. The claim that limonene itself acts as an antioxidant that protects the
polymer on heating comes from the Noguchi abstract and a patent; I found no independent test of
that mechanism. Note that limonene is also the more reactive molecule: in air it oxidises to
carveol, carvone and limonene oxide, and I found nothing on its radical chemistry with polystyrene.

**Recovery.** Three routes appear. (1) Add an alcohol as an anti-solvent so polystyrene drops out
(the Noguchi-type route, with the solubility limit of the three-component system not published until
2024). (2) Steam distillation, shown for p-cymene. (3) Supercritical CO2 for XPS. The solvent is then
recovered by distillation and returned to the dissolver; the industrial analogues (CreaSolv,
Polystyvert) work on this loop with solvents other than limonene. High boiling point (176 degrees C)
makes stripping limonene from the polymer harder and raises the question of residual solvent and
odour in pellets, on which the literature found is silent.

**What gets "extracted".** Residual pentane blowing agent is typically 0.1 to 1.5% by weight in EPS
waste (patent). Flame-retardant HBCD, a persistent pollutant in insulation EPS, stays in the liquid
when polystyrene is precipitated, which is the CreaSolv principle; recycled granulate is limited to
under 100 ppm. Food-packaging contamination and dye removal were not covered by anything found.
Typical "thermocol" packaging is not HBCD-treated insulation, so the HBCD argument matters for
building waste more than for boxes.

## 4. Where the literature is thin

1. **No limonene-specific techno-economics or life-cycle assessment** among the sources found.
   Noguchi's series has a third part (reported as a life-cycle study) that I could not see.
2. **No residual-limonene, odour or food-contact data** for recovered polystyrene.
3. **No data on solvent ageing over repeated cycles.** The ">=10 reuses" claim is a secondary one.
4. **No scale evidence.** No limonene-based EPS plant at industrial scale was found; the 1998
   prototype and Indian pilot work are the extent.
5. **Supply.** Old IARC figures put world d-limonene production at roughly 45,000 tonnes a year,
   a 2014 paper at above 70,000 tonnes, and one supplier at about 30,000 tonnes of technical grade
   with Brazil holding around 70%. These disagree, are of different grades and years, and prices
   found were weak (about 4.5 to 6 US dollars a kilogram from a market page, with a very volatile
   history). Nothing was found on Indian orange-peel oil availability.
6. **Competition for the solvent.** Limonene is already used in cleaning, fragrance and coatings,
   so a waste-recycling process would compete for a by-product with fixed volume.
7. **Safety at scale.** Flash point, oxidation and sensitisation all matter for a plant. No
   occupational exposure limit turned up in the sources read.

## 5. Reading the "orange peel" claim

Press coverage of the IIT Hyderabad work says polystyrene dissolves almost instantly in citrus
peel extract with no heat or acid and without changing its chemistry. That agrees with the
Noguchi-type result, but the Indian papers use a peel extract rather than isolated limonene, so
the exact composition matters. One preprint says limonene is about 98% of orange peel essential oil;
that is for the oil, not for any extract. Peel oil is seasonal and varies by fruit, so a reusable
process solvent needs a defined grade. The student and science-fair experiments that fill the web
were not used here.
