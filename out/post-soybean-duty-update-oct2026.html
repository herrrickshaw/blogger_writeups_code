Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra between them sow roughly 93 lakh hectares of soybean a year — a scale that ought to make India a soy-food powerhouse. Almost none of that crop becomes tofu, soy milk or protein isolate on an Indian plate. Trace the harvested bean through a crusher and it splits into meal, most of which used to be exported, and oil, which still leaves India roughly two-thirds reliant on imports to meet its own edible-oil demand. The one soy-derived product where India genuinely leads the world — lecithin — is a byproduct of that same oil-crushing step, not a separate industry. This piece follows the bean from MP and Maharashtra's fields through crushing, lecithin, and the still-small frontier of tofu, soy protein and edamame.

India Grows the Soybean, Exports the Meal, Leads in Lecithin — and Barely Makes Tofu Yet

Skip to article content
Text Size

The mismatch this piece traces. India is one of the world's largest soybean growers by area, concentrated almost entirely in two states. Yet its actual soy-food industry — tofu, soy milk, protein isolate — is a fraction of the size of countries that grow far less soybean. The gap isn't a mystery once you follow where the crushed bean is engineered to go.

1. The production base: two states, most of the crop

Government crop estimates, compiled and published by the Soybean Processors Association of India (SOPA), put Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra well ahead of every other soybean-growing state, in both area and output:

Between the two states alone, roughly 93-97 lakh hectares are sown to soybean depending on the season — and the two states together account for a clear majority of India's total soybean area and output every year, ahead of the next five states combined.

2. A genuine reversal worth flagging, not smoothing over

The table above isn't presenting one clean number twice — it's showing two different seasons, and between them Maharashtra overtook Madhya Pradesh on production even though MP still sows more area. In the 2024-25 final estimate MP led (55.40 vs 50.17 lakh tonnes); in the Kharif 2025 estimate Maharashtra leads (52.23 vs 43.25 lakh tonnes), on a yield that improved to 1,169 kg/ha against MP's yield falling to 889 kg/ha. That's not noise — it tracks a real, reported season: Madhya Pradesh's soybean belt, particularly the Mandsaur district cluster, faced a serious Yellow Mosaic Virus outbreak in 2025, compounding flood and heavy-monsoon damage that hit Maharashtra and Punjab as well. National output for the 2025-26 oil year is estimated by SOPA at around 105.36 lakh tonnes, down roughly 16% from the prior season's 125.82 lakh tonnes.

A second, structural pressure on the same two states. Farmers in MP and Maharashtra have also been shifting sown area away from soybean toward maize: national maize area rose to roughly 93.34 lakh hectares in 2025 from 83.58 lakh hectares in 2024, a 12% year-on-year increase, driven partly by maize's role as ethanol feedstock and partly by soybean's own price weakness — the government's floor price of ₹4,892 per 100kg has been running 10-20% above actual mandi prices since the October 2024 marketing year began, which is the opposite of the floor doing its job. This is the same maize-for-ethanol pull this blog has covered from the ethanol side; here it shows up as an oilseed-acreage problem instead.

The MP crisis wasn't left to farmers alone. The state government disbursed ₹653 crore to 8.84 lakh farmers across 3,554 villages, covering roughly 6.53 lakh hectares of combined rain-and-YMV crop damage — paid directly into farmers' bank accounts, and, per the state government's own framing, disbursed before the affected crop had even reached market. Madhya Pradesh separately relaunched its Bhavantar (price-deficiency) Scheme for soybean specifically, with registration opening 3 October 2025: farmers who register and then sell below MSP receive the shortfall from the state.

The price problem behind that relaunch is national, not just MP's. Soybean MSP for the season was set at ₹5,328 per quintal, while mandi prices were running ₹3,800–4,000/quintal — the same below-MSP gap already noted above, now wide enough to trigger a formal Centre response. In late October 2025 the Union government cleared a combined ₹15,000 crore Kharif procurement package for soybean across four states — Telangana, Odisha, Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh — with Telangana specifically allotted 25% of the procurement share and its procurement window extended 15 days beyond the standard 90-day period. That followed a direct request: Telangana's Agriculture Minister wrote to the Union Agriculture Minister on 2 November 2025 asking the Centre to begin MSP procurement of soybean and maize in the state, citing prices running below the floor. Telangana's own soybean base is small next to MP and Maharashtra — 1.25 lakh tonnes in the Kharif 2025 estimate above, against MP's 43.25 and Maharashtra's 52.23 — but it's leaning on the same price-support mechanism the two much bigger producers are also using.

3. What actually happens to the harvested bean

One harvested crop, three separate outcomes: a domestic/export commodity split (meal, feed), an import-dependent refined product (edible oil) that happens to throw off the one product India leads the world in (lecithin), and a still-small food-grade stream that is where the "soy foods" most people picture — tofu, soy milk, protein isolate — actually sit. Edamame, dashed and unconnected above, is not part of this flow at all.

A crushed soybean splits, roughly, into about 80% meal and 18-20% oil by weight — which is why the export story and the domestic-shortage story point in opposite directions for the same crop. India exported around 21 lakh tonnes of soybean meal in 2023-24, up 16% on the year, with soymeal exports for October 2023-June 2024 running at 19.5 lakh tonnes. That export flow has since weakened badly: India's oilmeal exports fell 51% year-on-year to just 1.62 lakh tonnes in November 2025, because Indian soymeal is currently priced out of the global market against Brazil and Argentina.

The oil half of the split doesn't close India's gap either. India still imports roughly two-thirds of its total vegetable-oil demand — palm oil alone covers 55-60% of that import bill, with soybean oil (mainly from Argentina and Brazil) and sunflower oil making up most of the rest. In May 2025 the government cut the import duty on crude soybean, sunflower and palm oil from 20% to 10% (an effective duty near 16.5% after cess), specifically to pull in more crude oil for domestic refining and cool retail edible-oil prices. A country growing this much soybean is, at the same time, actively lowering barriers to import more soybean oil — the crush economics, not the farm-gate harvest, are what actually govern the trade balance.

4. The one soy product where India already leads — because it rides on the crush, not against it

Soy lecithin is a gum recovered during oil refining (the "degumming" step), not a separately grown or processed product — which is exactly why India can lead the world in it despite the overall oil deficit: lecithin capacity is a byproduct of crushing infrastructure that already exists at scale for the meal-and-oil trade. By shipment count, India is the world's largest soy lecithin exporter (8,582 shipments), well ahead of Argentina (1,499) and China (586), moving mainly under HSN 2923 / 29232010 to the Netherlands, Germany and Nepal.

Lecithin didn't require India to build a new industry. It required India to keep the one it already had for meal and oil, and sell what used to be a processing waste stream.
5. The food-grade stream: mature in TVP, still small in tofu and soy milk

This is where the mismatch in Section 1's premise becomes visible. Despite the raw-material base, India's domestic soy-food sector is early-stage by most industry estimates — and those estimates disagree with each other by a wide enough margin that they should be read as directional, not precise:

Figures are vendor-modelled market-research estimates (IMARC, Mordor Intelligence, Spherical Insights and similar); methodology and market-definition scope are not disclosed in the public summaries, and the spread between sources for the same "2025" year is itself evidence of that — treat these as directional signals of a small, fast-growing category, not load-bearing numbers.

One food-grade category the table above understates: soya chunks, nuggets and granules. "Still small" describes tofu and soy milk fairly, but not this one. Made from defatted soy flour — the same food-grade meal stream, texturised (TVP, textured vegetable protein) rather than left loose — soya chunks/nuggets/granules have been an everyday Indian household staple for close to four decades, led overwhelmingly by one brand: Nutrela (Ruchi Soya/Patanjali Foods), sold as a roughly 52%-protein "meal maker" in every general grocery store, not a specialty health-food aisle. This is the actual mass-market form the food-grade stream has already taken in India; tofu and soy milk are a newer, smaller, more urban layer on top of an existing category, not the category's starting point.

Soya chaap is one further step downstream from that same TVP base: soya chunks or granules are ground and combined with wheat flour and gluten into a dough, wrapped around skewers, and grilled or pan-fried — a mock-meat preparation that emerged from Delhi and Punjab street-food stalls in the early 2000s and is now a fixture of North Indian restaurant and street-food menus generally, well beyond its original cities. It sits inside the food-grade stream this section describes, not as raw TVP but as a prepared dish built from it.

A separate, smaller category again is roasted soy nuts and namkeen — whole roasted soybeans sold as a snack, closer in processing terms to a roasted grain than to defatted meal, and marketed mainly on protein content as a "healthy snacking" alternative to conventional namkeen. Unlike TVP-based products, this category does not depend on the crush-and-defat step at all; it uses the whole bean directly.

Demand drivers cited across these reports are consistent even where the sizing isn't: lactose intolerance affecting an estimated 61% of Indian adults, rising urban vegetarianism/veganism, and health-driven interest in dairy alternatives. What none of the reports establish is domestic processing capacity specifically for tofu, soy milk or protein isolate — India's roughly 150 soybean processing units are overwhelmingly built for crush-to-meal-and-oil, not food-grade protein extraction, and this piece did not find a public plant-level breakdown separating the two.

6. Edamame is a different crop, not a downstream product of the MP/Maharashtra harvest

It's worth being precise here: edamame — the green, immature soybean eaten as a vegetable, common in Japanese and increasingly Western retail — is not what MP and Maharashtra grow at scale. Grain soybean is left on the plant to mature and dry before harvest, for crushing into meal and oil; edamame is harvested green, several weeks earlier, for direct food use, and is typically a distinct cultivar and growing programme rather than an early pick from the same fields. India's edamame activity is small and largely export-oriented, moving under HS code 0710 (fresh/frozen vegetables) or HS 2008 (processed) depending on form, aimed at markets like Japan, the US and the EU where frozen edamame is an established frozen-vegetable category. It sits adjacent to the story this piece has told so far, not inside it — MP and Maharashtra's scale is a grain-soybean story; edamame is a separate, much smaller, horticultural-export story that happens to share a species name.

What would fix this

  • The Department of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare should publish UPAG’s state-wise crop area, yield and production tables as dated, downloadable files with stable links, so that official soybean estimates can be cited directly rather than through an industry association’s compilation.
  • The Department of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare should publish soybean quantities actually procured at MSP, by state and week, under the ₹15,000 crore Kharif package, so that it can be checked whether procurement closed a gap of mandi prices at ₹3,800–4,000 against an MSP of ₹5,328.
  • The Madhya Pradesh Farmer Welfare and Agriculture Development Department should publish Bhavantar soybean registrations, quantities and payments by district, so that the relaunched price-deficiency scheme can be judged on who was paid and how much.
  • The Ministry of Food Processing Industries should publish a register of soybean processing capacity split between crush-for-oil-and-meal and food-grade protein lines, so that the size of India’s tofu, soy-milk and isolate base rests on plant data rather than vendor estimates that disagree.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
