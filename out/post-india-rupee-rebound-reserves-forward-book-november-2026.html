India's rupee: Rs 93.69 a dollar on 11 November, 3% above its early-October low

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • Rs 93.69 a dollar was the rupee’s close on 11 November, about 3% stronger than the early-October low of around Rs 96.80 (one source); the 4 November Reuters poll expected Rs 96.00 by January.
  • $68.65 billion is how far reserves fell from the 4 September record to $717.06 billion on 30 October; they were $719.55 billion on 6 November, and the fall mixes dollar sales, FCNR(B) deployment and valuation.
  • $239 billion is the RBI’s net short forward dollar position at the end of September, $143 billion of it due in three to five years; it must buy dollars as inflows return, which limits how far the rupee can rise.

What to do: The RBI should publish its spot and forward intervention weekly with a short lag, since market reports of dollar buying by state-run banks cannot be confirmed from reserves data.

What it means: Of the $150 billion drawn through swap windows, the balance of payments surplus of $65 billion becomes an $85 billion deficit without it, and the forward short means the RBI must buy dollars as inflows come back, which caps the rupee’s rise.

What to do: The RBI should publish the forward book by maturity every month, with the FCNR(B)-linked share, so repayment years and reserves can be read together.

What it means: The short-term range is 93 to 95.5 but every medium-term forecast read is weaker than spot, from 94 to 95 by March to 100 by June, so the rally rests on crude and inflows that the same forecasters expect to fade.

What to do: Importers and borrowers should plan hedges on the 96 to 100 range for mid-2027 rather than the 11 November level, since the November rally is the one part of the path no forecaster extrapolates.

What would fix this

  1. Reserve Bank of India: publish spot and forward intervention by week, and split reserve changes into transactions, valuation and income.
  2. Reserve Bank of India: publish the forward book by maturity each month, with the share tied to FCNR(B) swaps and the repayment years.
  3. Ministry of Finance and the RBI: publish a joint table of swap-window inflows and the dates on which the dollars are due, so the repayment years are visible.
  4. FBIL: publish the reference-rate series with the time of fixing and the volume behind it, so market closes and official fixes can be compared.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Method and definitions
  • The rupee is quoted in rupees per dollar, so a lower number is a stronger rupee. The gain from the October low is computed two ways: 3.2% fewer rupees per dollar, or 3.3% more dollars per rupee.
  • Reserves are the RBI’s weekly statistical supplement totals; the end-March value is derived from the 30 October total and the reported $25.95 billion rise.
  • The forward book is the RBI’s net short forward position in dollars, from RBI data as summarised by IDFC First Bank; it is a commitment to deliver dollars when buy-sell swaps mature.
  • Forecast values come from different dates and sources; the chart is not a forecast of this blog.
What this piece could not establish
  • The early-October low: Rs 96.80 comes from a single analyst quoted by Financial Express; the FBIL series for October was not read.
  • RBI spot intervention: no official figure; ABC Live reports bank dollar demand that traders linked to the RBI, while HDFC Securities describes no central bank intervention. Both are reports, not data.
  • Weekly reserve declines: ABC Live calls the 30 October fall an eighth straight weekly decline while Mint calls it the second in a row; this piece does not use either count.
  • The forward-book and swap-window figures rest on one bank’s report summarising RBI data; the RBI’s own table was not read.
  • Portfolio flows: a data site reported large November equity inflows from NSDL, but the figure conflicts with the roughly $5 billion in Financial Express, and is not used.
  • Rupee levels after 11 November are not covered.
