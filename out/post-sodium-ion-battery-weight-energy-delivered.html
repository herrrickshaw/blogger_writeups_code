Sodium-ion batteries: 17% more mass per kWh than LFP at claimed densities

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 17.1% more mass for the same energy: a 175 Wh/kg sodium-ion cell (CATL’s claim) against a 205 Wh/kg LFP cell, which is 46.5 kg more in a 40 kWh car pack under an assumed 80% cell-to-pack ratio and about 1.4% less range under stated vehicle assumptions.
  • 7,029 cycles is what the 175 Wh/kg sodium-ion cell needs to deliver as much lifetime energy per kg as LFP lasting 6,000 cycles; the only cycle figures for sodium-ion found here are company claims of 4,000 to 10,000, none verified in the field.
  • $89.9 a kWh is the least that LFP’s non-purchase costs would need to be for Wood Mackenzie’s 21% lower sodium-ion ownership cost to hold at its $229 against $200 upfront prices, with sodium-ion’s own costs at zero; the release gives no cost inputs to check it.

What it means: At equal pack fraction the mass ratio is the density ratio, so claimed sodium-ion is 17.1% heavier than 205 Wh/kg LFP: 46.5 kg more in a 40 kWh car pack (about 1.4% of range), 3.5 kg in a 3 kWh scooter pack and 2.32 tonnes in a 2 MWh container, and none of it against LFP near 160 Wh/kg.

What to do: Buyers should ask suppliers for pack mass and rated energy in kilograms and kilowatt-hours on a datasheet, because no opened source gives a measured pack figure for any of these chemistries.

What it means: Every sodium-ion cycle figure found is a manufacturer or developer statement while the LFP figures are lab tests, warranties and a decade of operation, so the two ranges overlap on paper but carry different weight as evidence.

What to do: Lenders and buyers should treat sodium-ion cycle life as unproven until independent pack-level tests and field degradation curves are published, as an opinion in pv magazine on 1 October 2026 also concluded.

What it means: A lower-density chemistry can deliver more lifetime energy per kilogram only if it lasts longer, and sodium-ion at 175 Wh/kg needs 7,029 cycles to match LFP at 6,000 (17.1% more), so the claimed 10,000 cycles would beat it by 42% while a 110 Wh/kg cell would need 11,182.

What to do: Procurement documents should state the cycle count, depth of discharge, temperature and end-of-life threshold behind every cycle claim, so that the break-even can be tested against a measured figure.

What it means: At the same cycles to 80% sodium-ion costs 14.5% more upfront per kWh delivered, and the 21% lower ownership cost means LFP’s other costs must be at least $89.9 a kWh (45% of its purchase price) even if sodium-ion’s were zero, a gap that rests on augmentation and operating inputs the release does not publish.

What to do: Wood Mackenzie should publish the operating cost, augmentation schedule and discount rate behind the 21% figure, so that buyers can rerun it with their own cycling and prices.

What would fix this

  1. Bureau of Indian Standards and the Automotive Research Association of India: publish measured pack mass, rated energy, cycles to 80% and the test conditions for every battery type-approved in India, so that pack Wh/kg is a certified number and not a derived one.
  2. Ministry of Heavy Industries: name the chemistry and the quarterly commissioned GWh for each beneficiary of the cell manufacturing scheme, so that sodium-ion output in India can be told apart from announcements.
  3. Ministry of Power and the Solar Energy Corporation of India: require the projects in the 92 GWh programme to report annual capacity and round-trip efficiency, by chemistry, for the life of the project.
  4. A national test laboratory named by the Ministry of Heavy Industries: test commercial sodium-ion and LFP cells to the same protocol and publish pack-level energy density and cycles to 80%.
  5. Wood Mackenzie: publish the operating cost, augmentation and discount-rate inputs behind its 21% total-cost figure.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Method and definitions
  • Pack Wh/kg is the cell Wh/kg times a cell-to-pack fraction of 70%, 80% or 85%, the same for every chemistry. This is an assumption: no source opened for this piece measured it, so every pack figure here is derived and is not a finding.
  • Pack mass for a stated usable energy is the energy divided by pack Wh/kg and by a 90% usable depth of discharge. At equal fractions the mass ratio between two chemistries is the inverse of their cell density ratio.
  • Range effect: a 1,000 kg vehicle body, consumption of 0.1125 kWh/km at the LFP 205 pack’s mass, with 40% of consumption scaling with total mass. The scooter payload effect is the extra pack mass over an assumed 75 kg rider.
  • Lifetime energy per kg is pack Wh/kg x 0.9 depth of discharge x cycles to 80% x 0.9 (capacity fading in a straight line from 100% to 80%) and, for storage, x 0.90 round trip. Break-even cycles are the reference cycles times the ratio of cell densities.
  • Cost per kWh delivered divides the upfront price by lifetime throughput per kWh of capacity. It ignores operating cost, augmentation, replacement and discounting, which is why the 21% total-cost claim is only back-solved.
  • The LFP cycle range of 2,700 to 8,960 multiplies the DNV Battery Scorecard’s 135 to 448 cycles per 1% of capacity by 20, assuming a straight-line fade. The scorecard figures were read in pv magazine’s 2021 report of them.
  • Where the sources disagree (Blade at 150 or 160 Wh/kg, cell or pack; KPIT at 110 or 160), both figures are shown and neither is chosen as fact.
What this piece could not establish
  • A measured pack-level Wh/kg for any chemistry. CATL gave a Qilin pack mass (625 kg) without the pack’s kWh; the 45 kWh Naxtra pack’s mass was not published; Electrek’s ‘around 165 Wh/kg’ for Hina packs does not say how it was measured. The cell-to-pack fractions are assumptions.
  • Sources that could not be opened: the IEA (Global EV Outlook 2026, the sodium-ion commentary), RSC Publishing (a benchmarking paper, a 2026 ageing study, a life-cycle assessment), Nature, CEEW, YourStory, Energy-Storage.news and Gizmochina returned a bot challenge or were outside the allowed network, and were not bypassed. Anything from them appears only as a labelled excerpt: LFP at up to 205 Wh/kg, the excerpt of the RSC benchmarking paper (sodium cells 10% to 15% below LFP), the 80% pack assumption in a life-cycle study, the Naxion and Rechargion claims, and the CEEW report on sodium-ion in India. None of it feeds a computed figure other than the LFP 205 Wh/kg cell density, which is why the LFP 160 Wh/kg case is shown beside it.
  • Whether Electrek and Gizmochina printed Naxtra’s density as kWh/kg: the Electrek and Best Magazine pages read Wh/kg, and the Gizmochina page could not be opened.
  • Energy per litre for sodium-ion or LFP. The only volumetric figures found are for NMC (a 760 Wh/L claim for CATL’s Qilin Condensed cell) and a claimed 112-litre reduction in the Qilin 3 pack, so a stationary footprint comparison is not made.
  • Independent cycle-life data for commercial sodium-ion cells beyond a search-excerpt mention of a 2026 ageing study. Naxtra’s 10,000 cycles was found only in an arXiv preprint that repeats it and asks readers to verify it; no CATL document was opened.
  • What Wood Mackenzie’s $229 and $200 cover (cell, module or system), and its operating and augmentation inputs. The Moonwatt claim of cost parity in two to three years was not opened (Energy-Storage.news blocked) and is not used.
  • Sodium-ion shipping at low state of charge and the voltage differences from lithium-ion cells: no opened source covers them, so they are not discussed.
  • Named Indian production volumes. Reliance’s sodium-ion status rests on one line in a Business Standard report; Reliance’s 2025 megawatt-scale target was not confirmed as met. A Renewable Energy Secretary’s expectation of commercial pilots in two to three years and Jitendra EV’s plan for sodium-ion e-2Ws in two years were not found in any source opened.
  • The pv magazine piece of 1 October 2026 is an opinion by the head of energy storage at a developer, not a measurement; the same goes for the Electrek e-bike article.
