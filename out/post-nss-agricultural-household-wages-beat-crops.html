The Wage Beats the Crop — In the Survey That Leaves the Wage-Earners Out

India's official measure of what a farm household actually earns comes from the NSS 77th Round's "Situation Assessment of Agricultural Households and Land and Livestock Holdings of Households in Rural India" — reference year July 2018 to June 2019, published as NSS Report No. 587 in September 2021. It remains the government's current official figure: a February 2024 Lok Sabha reply from the Ministry of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare cites this same 77th round as the latest available data, alongside the 70th round (2012-13) as the only earlier comparison point. There is no newer round. Every figure below is dated to 2018-19, not written as a present-tense claim about what farmers earn today.

What an "agricultural household" is

The survey does not count every farming family. A household qualifies as "agricultural" only if it received more than ₹4,000 of value from agricultural produce during the year and had at least one member self-employed in agriculture. That is a survey construct with a specific threshold, not a synonym for "farmer" — and it draws a boundary that matters for what the income numbers below can and cannot say.

Where the household's income actually comes from

NSS Report 587's Table 6 gives average monthly income per agricultural household on two different expense bases, and they are not interchangeable — the fuller basis also charges the imputed cost of the household's own unpaid family labour against crop and livestock income, which the paid-out basis does not:

Wages are unchanged across both bases — imputing a cost hits crop and livestock income, not wage income — so the wage-vs-crop comparison is clean on either one: wages exceed crop production by ₹265/month on the paid-out basis and by ₹1,005/month on the fuller basis. On the imputed-cost basis, wages are 49% of total household income and crop production 37%. In the survey the government itself uses to measure farm household income, the single largest income source for the average agricultural household is not the crop it grows.

Don't compute a trend from this. The 2024 Lok Sabha reply also tables the 70th round (2012-13) total at ₹6,426/month against the 77th round's ₹10,218 — but which of the two expense bases the ₹6,426 figure uses was not established for this piece, so no growth rate between the two years is calculated here. Treat 2018-19 as a single dated snapshot, not one point on an established trend line.

Who the survey leaves out

The release states its exclusion rule in its own words: "households which were entirely agricultural labour households and households receiving income entirely from coastal fishing, activity of rural artisans and agricultural services were not considered as agricultural households and they were kept outside the scope of the survey." That is not only landless labourers — it also removes coastal fishing households, rural artisan households, and agricultural-services households from the population the income table above describes.

The population this survey measures already skews toward cultivation. Wages beat crop income even inside a population defined to exclude the households most dependent on wages in the first place. A survey of India's full rural workforce, including the households this one excludes by design, would put an even larger share of rural income on the wage side of the ledger.
Two more scope notes. Wages here mean wage or salary income from any employment the household holds, farm or non-farm — not specifically farm-labour wages, so ₹4,063 should not be read as what farm labour alone pays. And the survey's landless share — 8.2%, with average area owned 0.512 hectares among the surveyed population — describes landlessness within this already-narrowed agricultural-household population, not India's rural landlessness rate as a whole.

The takeaway

This blog has already reported the other side of this picture: A 17% Rural Pay Rise That Nobody Received covered the index that sets what agricultural labourers are paid. This piece covers the official survey that measures farm household income and defines those same labour-dependent households out of its frame — and even so, finds that the wage still beats the crop. Any farm-income policy built on this survey's numbers is being built on a population that already excludes the households a wage-focused policy would most need to reach. (The same survey also finds 50.2% of agricultural households in debt, averaging ₹74,121 outstanding — a separate finding this piece does not develop.)
