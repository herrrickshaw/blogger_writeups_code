Sustainable aviation fuel: India targets 5% by 2030, the EU 6% and the UK 10%

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 5% by 2030 is India’s indicative target for sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) in jet fuel for international flights, after 1% in 2027 and 2% in 2028. The EU requires 6% and the UK 10% in 2030, on all flights and in law.
  • About 36,000 tonnes of SAF a year meets the 1% target at FY2025-26 sales: 1% of the 3.57 million tonnes of jet fuel sold for international flights (PPAC: 39% of 9.16 million tonnes). Certified refinery capacity is reported at 106,500 tonnes.
  • 1.1 million tonnes of SAF was supplied at EU airports in 2025, 2.79% of fuel against a 2% minimum and about 30 times India’s 1% need. EASA counts 3.7 million tonnes of EU capacity by 2030 against 2.1 million needed.

This piece compares the targets, their scope and the tonnes behind them. The hydrogen each litre needs is in Sustainable aviation fuel in India: hydrogen needed per litre, by pathway; eligibility is in Corn ethanol jet fuel under CORSIA; batch tracking is in How to use saf-monitoring-system.

What it means: India’s 5% is close to the EU’s 6% as a number, but it is an indicative target on international flights only, about 2% of all jet fuel sold in India at FY2025-26 shares, and the civil aviation ministry told Parliament on 20 July 2026 that the SAF policy was not final.

What to do: Airlines and fuel suppliers should plan for 1% on international uplift from January 2027, as press reports of the draft policy describe, and treat the 2035 and 2040 figures as unsettled until the policy is notified.

India’s document trail: what is official and what is press-reported
  • Official: the targets appear in Parliament answers from the petroleum ministry (15 December 2025) and the civil aviation ministry (20, 27 and 30 July 2026), and in a PIB release of 23 April 2026. All call them “indicative”.
  • Official: on 17 April 2026 the ATF Control Order was amended to bring SAF-blended jet fuel within it.
  • Official: on 20 July 2026 the civil aviation ministry said the SAF policy “has not been finalised yet”.
  • Press-reported: a draft national SAF policy, seen by four newspapers in early November 2026, makes 1% mandatory from 2027, adds 3% in 2029, penalties, and indicative 10% in 2035 and 20% in 2040. A PTI report carried by CNBC TV18 gives 40% by 2040.
  • Not found: the order or minutes that approved the indicative targets, and the draft policy text itself.

What would fix this

  1. Ministry of Civil Aviation: notify the SAF policy before 1 January 2027, stating who is obliged, the fuel base, the compliance year and the penalty.
  2. PPAC: publish how jet fuel is classed as international, with monthly tonnes, since the reported share moved from 26% in FY2024-25 to 49% in April to September 2026.
  3. Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas: publish SAF produced, blended and sold by refinery each month, as EASA reports for the EU each year.
  4. DGCA: open the SAF registry described in the draft policy, with batch-level records, so each tonne is claimed once.
  5. Both ministries: state whether imported SAF counts toward the targets; press accounts of the draft differ on this.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Method and definitions
  • India’s tonnes are PPAC jet fuel (ATF) sales, times PPAC’s international share, times the target. The 39% share is rounded, so the 1% figure carries about 500 tonnes of rounding either way.
  • The April to September 2026 basis doubles six months of sales. It is a rate, not a forecast.
  • India’s 5% as a share of all jet fuel is 5% times 39%, 1.95%.
  • The EU’s 2.1 Mt is EASA’s figure; it equals (6% minus 1.2%) of 44.385 Mt. The full 6% is 2.66 Mt.
  • Shares are as each scheme states them. The UK awards certificates by energy and emissions saving; Brazil sets an emissions cut, so it is left off the chart.
  • Capacity figures are nameplate tonnes a year. Co-processing units and HEFA plants can switch between SAF and other fuels.
What this piece could not establish
  • The text of India’s draft SAF policy, and the instrument that approved the indicative targets. The 2040 figure is 20% in four reports and 40% in one.
  • Why PPAC’s international share of jet fuel rose from 26% (FY2024-25) to 39% (FY2025-26) and 49% (April to September 2026). PPAC says the split comes from oil companies and is not based on tax treatment.
  • India’s 2030 requirement: 178,600 tonnes at FY2025-26 sales, about 250,000 (S&P Global) or 385,000 (the draft’s reported forecast).
  • Whether any Indian refinery has sold SAF commercially. Sources disagree on BPCL Mumbai (61,000 or 15,000 tonnes) and Indian Oil Panipat (30,000 or 35,000).
  • The EU’s 2030 requirement appears as 2.8 Mt, 2.66 Mt and 2.6 Mt, and realistic capacity as 3.2 Mt or 3.7 Mt, depending on the EASA edition used.
  • Singapore’s start year: 2026 in a CAAS release of November 2025 and in PIB’s April 2026 release, 2027 on current CAAS and transport ministry pages.
  • Japan: PIB cites 10% by 2030, an earlier airline-use goal; METI’s supply obligation starts at 1% in FY2030.
  • Indonesia and Malaysia rest on one secondary source each. Thailand, Vietnam, China and the United States are not covered, and CORSIA offsets are left to a separate piece.
