This is the long version of the guide. It installs the toolkit, explains the few habits that keep a screening result honest, and then goes through the commands by job: relief and venting, fire and explosion, functional safety, regulation, electrical, and the quantitative risk study. Every output below was produced by running the command on 5 November 2026 with illustrative inputs. An asset-specific walk-through of an LPG terminal, with every result, is in section 16.

Try it in the browser first. A clickable design demo, Process Safety Studio, walks through five tasks in the same five steps the command line uses: choose a task, enter inputs with units, run, read the result with its notes and limits, and export. The tasks are a gas relief valve sized to API 520, OSHA PSM applicability, LNG terminal basics, PSM revalidation due dates and fire-and-gas detector coverage. Its results are outputs captured from the command line, not live calculations, so use it to see the workflow and install the toolkit to run your own numbers.

It is a screen, not a safety case. Most of the governing standards are paywalled and were not read. Equations are derived from first principles where possible and tested against the standards’ own constants, and every remaining coefficient is flagged in the module’s docstring. A qualified engineer owns any result, and validated software should decide real designs.

2. Six habits that keep a screen honest

  1. Know which numbers are yours. Latent heat, set pressure, fill rate, failure frequencies and acceptance criteria are inputs. The toolkit will not invent them, and a diesel specific fuel consumption has no default for that reason.
  2. Check units on the command line. They are in each command’s help: --p0 and --set-g are kPa, blowdown --hole-d is metres, --wall-mm is millimetres.
  3. Read the note and warnings fields. They carry the limits of the result, such as the neutral-plume warning in plume.
  4. Trust the refusals. The toolkit declines to answer outside a method’s range instead of returning a number (examples below).
  5. Sample before you scale. For the risk study, run --sample first.
  6. Verify against the standard you are held to. The module docstring names it; coverage says whether its text was read.

Here are two refusals, both real outputs. The arc-flash command only answers for open-air three-phase cases, and says so for enclosed equipment; the worst-case toxic release tells you when the neutral-plume model is outside its validity.

{
  "applicability": {
    "ok": false,
    "why": "enclosed three-phase equipment (switchgear, MCC, panels): use IEEE 1584"

5. Functional safety and reliability

Layer-of-protection analysis (LOPA) tells you how much risk reduction a safety function must deliver. The probability-of-failure command then checks an architecture against it.

$ python3 -m process_safety lopa --ie 0.1 --ipl 0.1 0.01 --target 1e-6
  unmitigated_per_yr: 0.1   mitigated_per_yr: 0.0001   tolerable_per_yr: 1e-06
  required_rrf: 100   sil_needed: SIL 1

$ python3 -m process_safety pfd --arch 1oo2 --lambda-du 2e-6 --ti-years 1 --beta 0.05
  pfd_avg: 5.30e-04   rrf: 1,886   achieves: SIL 3

A hazard of 0.1 per year with two independent layers still leaves 1e-4 per year against a 1e-6 target, so a safety function with a risk reduction of 100 is required. A 1oo2 architecture with a dangerous undetected failure rate of 2e-6 per hour, a yearly proof test and a 5% common-cause factor gives a probability of failure of 5.3e-4, which meets the need on probability. Probability is not the whole test: the SIL tool also checks the architecture limits, and that is where a single valve usually fails (see section 9).

8. The risk study (qra)

The risk study chains six steps: hazard identification, frequency, consequence, risk, evaluation against criteria, and mitigation. Everything is driven by one JSON file. Copy the worked example and edit it.

python3 -m process_safety qra example --out study.json       # copy the worked example
python3 -m process_safety qra validate study.json            # units, sums to 1, references, provenance
python3 -m process_safety qra hazid study.json --out hazid/  # registers for the workshop
python3 -m process_safety qra run study.json --sample 3 --out sample/
python3 -m process_safety qra run study.json --out qra_out/
python3 -m process_safety qra buildings study.json --out qra_out/
python3 -m process_safety qra emergency study.json --out qra_out/

The study file has these top-level blocks:

On the worked example the full run takes about 13 seconds and finds 18 loss-of-containment scenarios with a total frequency of 0.0435 per year. Open qra_report.html first, then use the CSV files in Excel.

How to read the headline results on the example: the loading-bay operator has the highest individual risk (6.0e-4 per year), every receptor is tolerable if ALARP, yet the F-N curve is in the intolerable region against the UK preset. The mitigation table then says what to do: a loading-hose release coupling prevents 0.046 fatalities a year at a cost-benefit ratio of 0.033, so implement; extra shutdown valves and relocating a building are not justified on cost-benefit alone.

Sample first. --sample N runs N scenarios on a coarser grid, re-runs them at double angular resolution to test convergence, and projects the full-run time. The study found two of its own bugs this way.

12. Limits, and where to go next

  • No dense-gas model. Propane and chlorine clouds spread differently from the neutral plume. Feed validated footprints from a tool such as DNV Phast or RISKCURVES through the overrides hook. Section 16 shows what this means for a propane terminal.
  • Paywalled standards. API 752/753, IEC 60079-10-1 and IEEE 1584 were not read, so building-damage curves, zone extents and enclosed arc flash are inputs or refusals.
  • No default Indian criteria. The risk study ships UK and Dutch presets; supply a custom criterion for an Indian site.
  • Software-class studies. Load flow, harmonics, transient stability, pulsation and illumination are listed as not implemented.

Read next in the repository: docs/QRA.md (the study file and models), docs/STUDIES.md (the seven JSON tools), docs/ELECTRICAL_MECHANICAL.md (what was read versus corroborated), docs/DATACENTRE.md (data-centre helpers) and docs/IFLUIDS_SERVICES.md (consultancy services mapped to modules).

Inputs are checked against ranges

Each numeric input has a hard range (physically impossible outside it: the run stops with exit code 2) and a soft range (atypical: a warning on the error stream, and the run continues). The soft ranges are engineering judgement, there to catch unit slips; they are not a standard’s limits. --no-range-check skips both for a deliberate extreme case.

14. What is new since 0.10: derogations, EU impact, insurance and legal clauses

These additions are on pull request 4, stacked on the earlier pull requests and not yet merged. The command line now has 47 commands, 342 automated tests pass and the cross-check suite has grown to 186 checks, all within tolerance. The new work answers one question: when a design departs from a must clause, who approves it, what does it cost the business, what does European law say about it, what does it do to the insurance, and where does the law set the limit? Every default below (tiers, approval levels, thresholds) is editable and is not any company’s rule.

Must, should, can, and the derogation

Each clause in a standard is graded from its modal verb. A must (shall, must, shall not) is complied with or formally derogated. A should is comply-or-explain. A can is optional, and you record whether you adopted it. A derogation is a formal, approved departure from a must. It needs a justification, a risk assessment reference, at least one compensating measure, an expiry date, a consequence assessment and the sign-offs the matrix requires. A statutory clause cannot be derogated by company sign-off at all; only the regulator can exempt.

$ python3 -m process_safety compliance process_safety/examples/compliance.json --as-of 2026-11-06
  "by_tier":  MUST  complied 1, derogated 3, open 0
              SHOULD complied 0, derogated 1, open 1
              CAN    adopted 0, not-adopted 1
  "must_compliance": 1 of 4 satisfied (25.0%)
  "ready_for_gate": false          (condensed from the JSON)

The example register is deliberately faulty, so that it fails the gate and shows each failure code. A gate that cannot fail would tell you nothing.

The approval matrix: TA1, TA2 and TA3

The class of a derogation is the highest of its safety severity and six management ratings (next section). The class then sets who must sign.

Tierlowmediumhighsevere
MUSTTA2TA2TA1TA1 + TA2
SHOULDTA3TA3TA2TA1

TA1 is taken as the most senior level and TA3 the least: that ordering is an assumption you can edit. A higher level can stand in for a lower one, and each sign-off must come from a different person. With no consequence assessment the class defaults to severe. In the example, clause 4.2.2 is a medium must that needs TA2 but carries only a TA3 sign-off, so the review reports unmet: TA2 and the issue approval-insufficient.

PESTLE: mapping the management consequence

For each derogation the assessor rates six lenses from 0 to 5: political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental. 0 to 2 is low, 3 medium, 4 high and 5 severe. A rating of 3 or more needs a written management action, and a must derogation must address all six factors and describe the scenario if the derogated clause fails. In the example, clause 4.1.3 rates the economic factor 3 (a pump-house outage of about ten days) and records its action: a spare pump on site and business-interruption cover confirmed. The tool enforces completeness; the ratings themselves are the assessor’s judgement.

EU impact: Seveso III

A company derogation never waives an EU or national requirement. A clause can be marked as restating an EU provision (non-derogable) or implementing one (it needs an equivalence argument and is floored to the high class). The register also records the Seveso tier and screens each derogation against the Article 11 question: is the change significant, was the authority informed, and were the notification, the major-accident prevention policy, the safety management system and, for an upper-tier site, the safety report updated?

Insurance impact, from an actuarial perspective

insurance takes an exposure rating, scenario frequencies, loss distributions with an uncertainty (error) factor, and the deductible and limit. It computes the expected loss in the layer, its variance, a technical premium and the retained loss. Each case scales the scenario frequencies by the probability of failure on demand of the derogated safeguard. With the invented inputs shipped as an example:

Case (USD)Technical premiumChangeRetained expected loss
baseline3,128,8100%40,346
derogated6,639,142+112.2%133,464
derogated with compensating measure4,205,767+34.4%63,617

In that illustration the present value of the saving from the compensating measure is about 16.8 million dollars against a measure cost of 2.5 million. The inputs are invented, so read this as a method, not a finding. A modelled premium is not a quotation, and no market rates or loss data are shipped.

Legal clauses across eight regimes

legal-clauses searches 55 provisions from eight regimes: India (15), Australia at Commonwealth level (13), Singapore (6), Canada at federal level (6), South Korea (6), the EU (5), the US (3) and Great Britain (1). Each is filed under one of four topics: general duty, change, notify and exemption. Grade A (40 clauses) means the legal text was read. Grade B (15 clauses) means only guidance, a mirror or an OCR copy was seen: confirm it against the legislation. For India, the Manufacture, Storage and Import of Hazardous Chemical Rules 1989 treat a modification likely to cover major accident hazards as a new activity (rule 6) and need a further safety report at least 90 days before a material modification (rule 11), which is the central constraint on a derogation there.

15. What is new on 6 and 7 November: US units, input templates, a scenario suite, cost and plant tools

These are also on pull request 4, now at version 0.11.0 and still not merged. The command line has 52 commands, 364 automated tests pass, and a new official scenario suite runs 159 scenarios with 787 checks, all passing. Every output below was produced by running the command on 7 November 2026 with illustrative inputs; long JSON is condensed. The rule that runs through all of it is the same as before: no remembered number is typed into the code. Walking speeds, lux targets, unit rates, cost indices and discharge coefficients are inputs, not defaults.

US-customary units, and why gauge and absolute pressure will not convert

Put --units us before any command and type every number in US units: psig, °F, ft, in, lb/h, gpm, Btu/lb. Each is converted to the command’s working unit before the range check, and the result gains US sibling keys next to the SI ones. Every conversion factor is a legal definition (the inch, the pound, standard gravity, the 231 in³ gallon, the International Table Btu), not a remembered number.

$ python3 -m process_safety --units us relief-gas --W 22000 --T 140 --set-g 250 --M 44.1 --k 1.13 --Z 0.85
  "required_area_mm2": 518.3,
  "required_area_in2": 0.803,
  "orifice": "J",  "regime": "critical"          (condensed)

That is 22,000 lb/h of propane-like vapour at 140 °F and a 250 psig set pressure. Two deliberate refusals protect against the commonest unit mistakes in relief work. A gauge pressure will not convert to an absolute one unless you say so, because the difference is the atmosphere:

$ python3 -m process_safety convert 250 psig psia
error: cannot convert psig (pressure_gauge) to psia (pressure_abs)
$ python3 -m process_safety convert 250 psig psia --across-atmosphere
  "result": 264.70

Temperature rises are a separate dimension (dK, dF), so they cannot be pushed through the °F offset by mistake, and standard volumes (scf, Nm³) are not converted at all, because sources use different reference temperatures: give a mass flow instead. docs/UNITS.md lists the ten differences that cause mistakes.

Input templates: fill in a file instead of typing flags

template writes a fill-in JSON (or CSV) for any command, in SI or US units, with each argument’s unit, whether it is required and its valid range. run executes the filled file, and the file’s own units field decides how its numbers are read.

$ python3 -m process_safety template relief-gas --system us > relief_us.json
   ... fill W, T, set_g, M, k, Z ...
$ python3 -m process_safety run relief_us.json
  "required_area_in2": 0.803,  "orifice": "J"     (same answer as the flags above)

The templates are generated from the command definitions themselves and a test keeps them in step, so a new argument appears in its template the moment it is added.

Two-phase relief from real fluid data

twophase sizes flashing relief by the omega method, and --fluid now reads saturated-liquid properties from a bundled table of 18 fluids at 14 pressures generated with CoolProp. fluid-data shows what is in it.

$ python3 -m process_safety twophase --P0 1500 --fluid propane
  "G_kg_m2_s": 9025,  "omega": 5.38,  "regime": "choked",
  "G_hem_reference_kg_m2_s": 9018
  "note": "homogeneous equilibrium; Kb/Kc not applied; subcooled and reactive (DIERS) cases need other methods"

The omega flux for saturated propane at 1,500 kPa absolute is within 0.1% of a direct homogeneous-equilibrium integration; across all 252 rows of the table the two agree within 2.2%. There is no default discharge coefficient for two-phase service: to get an area you must give --Kd from your valve data, alongside the relief rate --W. Near the critical point the single-point omega degrades, and the command now warns above half the critical pressure.

Flame radiation by view factor

flame-flux integrates the view factor from a target to a flat, vertical rectangular flame face and multiplies by the surface emissive power you supply.

$ python3 -m process_safety flame-flux --width 10 --height 15 --distance 25 --sep 60
  "view_factor": 0.061,  "incident_kW_m2": 3.66

A 10 m by 15 m flame face at 60 kW/m², seen from 25 m, delivers about 3.7 kW/m². The toolkit’s own use cases found that its point-source pool-fire distances and this solid-flame method disagree by about four times in the far field, because they imply different radiant fractions. That disagreement is documented, not hidden: use both and say which you relied on.

Escape time and lighting

egress compares the required safe escape time (detection, pre-movement, then travel or queuing at the narrowest exit, whichever is longer) with the available time from your fire or toxic study. illumination applies the lumen method.

$ python3 -m process_safety egress --distance 120 --speed 1.0 --people 40 --width 1.2 \
    --specific-flow 1.3 --detect-s 60 --premove-s 90 --aset-s 420
  "governing": "travel distance",  "rset_s": 270,  "margin_s": 150,  "escape_before_untenable": true

$ python3 -m process_safety illumination --area 600 --flux 9000 --uf 0.55 --mf 0.8 --target 200
  "n_luminaires": 31,  "achieved_lux": 204.6

Walking speed, flow per metre of exit, detection and response times, utilisation and maintenance factors and the lux target are all yours to supply. They depend on the occupants and on the standard the site follows, so the toolkit does not guess them.

From recommendations to money: shutdown, cost and LNG capex

Four new studies follow a safety recommendation through to cost and schedule. Each takes an input JSON and ships a worked example (tool example <name> copies it), and every rate, margin and cost index in the examples is a labelled placeholder: the toolkit ships no cost data.

  • Shutdown risk (tool shutdown): schedule and cost risk for a turnaround by Monte Carlo on a critical-path network, with the opportunity cost of lost operating days. Worked example: a 25-day base plan comes out at 31.5 days at P50 and 35.6 days at P80. A 500-run sample was not stable enough, so the tool ran 5,753 runs; the biggest driver is activity A6.
  • Recommendation cost (tool recs): the cost of implementing study recommendations by discipline, with contingency, the effect on the shutdown and an ALARP cost-benefit test. Worked example: six recommendations, capex 616 at P50 and 656 at P80 (in cost units); the independent high-level shutdown on the feed drum passes the ALARP test; the shutdown items add about 0.1 day at P80.
  • Design workflow (tool workflow): relief screening, resizing, a bill of quantities, capex, opex and ALARP chained in one run. Worked example: the V-101 fire case needs 664 mm² against 506 mm² installed, so the PSV is resized to a J orifice, and the package passes ALARP (cost-benefit ratio 2.5 against a disproportion factor of 3).
  • LNG capex (tool lngcost): concept-stage liquefaction capex from public analogs (24 plants used, 2 excluded). Worked example: an illustrative 5 MTPA greenfield train comes out at about $11.1 billion at P50, or $2.2 billion per MTPA. A reference notebook’s unsourced shortcut gives $5.9 billion, below every analog.

The LNG figure is an AACE Class 5 screening number, not a budget. The cost index and location factors are the largest judgements in it, so change them and look at the sensitivity. An optional script, scripts/openpytea_bridge.py, turns equipment you have sized into purchased and installed cost using the open OpenPyTEA correlations, and flags sizes outside a correlation’s range, which OpenPyTEA itself costs without warning. The package stays standard-library only; the bridge sits outside it.

Reading a plant: heat and mass balance, P&ID registers and the PHA

tool plant reads stream tables, equipment and instrument registers (as CSV or Proteus XML) and a PHA register, then runs six scenario types with the existing models and checks the registers against seven P&ID rules. Where an input is missing it reports cannot_assess and names the input, instead of guessing. In the worked example it finds:

  • a blocked outlet on V-101 that needs 4,398 mm² of relief area (a Q orifice) against 830 mm² installed;
  • depressuring V-101 to atmosphere could chill the gas to about −105 °C against a minimum design metal temperature of −20 °C;
  • drum V-102 has no relief device in the register;
  • tank T-201’s PSV vents propane and butane to atmosphere.

Each finding is tied to a recommendation type, and gaps that the PHA register does not already cover are listed separately.

Your own documents, incidents and sources

requirements pulls the shall, should and must statements out of standards and specifications you hold (text, Word, or PDF via pdftotext), classifies them and compares them with a PHA register. Because those documents are usually someone else’s copyright, it prints counts unless you ask for the statements, writes only to a file you name, and refuses to write inside a git repository unless the path is ignored. incidents searches 17 U.S. Chemical Safety Board investigations mapped to the toolkit’s modules, and literature searches an index of 93 sources, each marked with whether its content was actually read.

The scenario suite: how the toolkit checks itself

scripts/run_scenarios.py runs 159 scenarios covering 72 of the 77 topics in the coverage map. Each scenario carries up to five kinds of check:

  • Bounds: a physical or logical limit holds.
  • Oracles: an independent recomputation from first principles agrees.
  • Metamorphic relations: doubling the flow doubles the relief area, for example.
  • SI against US: the same case typed in both unit systems gives the same answer.
  • Regression snapshot: current values are unchanged.

The report says plainly that the snapshot only detects drift and is not evidence of correctness. The five topics without scenarios are the ones still marked not implemented. Writing the suite found a real trap: blowdown --hole-d is in metres, so a hole typed as “25” meaning millimetres was accepted as 25 m and gave a 0.4 ms blowdown. The help text now says metres.

16. Worked example: an LPG terminal, start to finish

An LPG storage and loading terminal is a good test of the toolkit: pressurised propane bullets, transfer pumps, a road-tanker loading bay, a flare, and a control room and village close enough to matter. This section walks that asset through the commands above with the actual outputs, asking the questions a process-safety workshop asks first: how big is the fire relief load, is the overfill trip good enough, where does the risk come from, and how far must emergency planning reach. The individual-risk, F-N and cost-benefit results of the same study are read in section 8.

The terminal is hypothetical. It is the toolkit’s worked example: four sections (a propane bullet with pumps, a road-tanker loading bay, a sour fuel-gas header, a diesel tank in a bund), 60,000 kg of propane at 750 kPag and 303 K in the bullet. Pump failure frequencies, weather, wind rose, population, building vulnerability, the value of a prevented fatality and mitigation costs are illustrative placeholders; replace every one with site data before any decision.
  • How much vapour must the bullet relieve in a pool fire?
    relief-fire --D 3.5 --L 14 --level 2.5 --latent 330
    Wetted area 119 m², heat input 2,174 kW, relief rate 23,720 kg/h. The geometry and the 330 kJ/kg latent heat are inputs.
  • What pressure and fill limit does propane set?
    lpg propane --fill-C 15 --max-C 50
    Vapour pressure 1,616 kPag at 50 °C, liquid density 506 kg/m³ at 15 °C, a thermodynamic fill ceiling of 88%. Codes set the practical limit.
  • Is the overfill trip good enough?
    tool sif sif.json
    Target SIL 2. The PFDavg of 6.6e-3 (risk reduction 151) meets SIL 2 by probability, but a single type-A inlet shutdown valve with 40% safe-failure fraction caps the architecture at SIL 1. It fails.
  • Which barriers carry the risk?
    tool bowtie bowtie.json
    Top event 1.7e-4 per year. Given a loss of containment: 69% unignited, 21% vapour-cloud explosion or flash fire, 9% jet fire, 1% BLEVE. The independent high-high level trip is the safety-critical element.
  • Where does individual risk come from?
    qra run study.json
    Highest location risk 0.012 per year at (120, 40) m; population risk (PLL) 0.051 per year.
  • How exposed are the buildings?
    (same run as above)
    Occupant risk per year: control room 5.0e-4, admin building 9.7e-5, loading-bay shelter 6.1e-3.
  • How far must emergency planning reach?
    (same run as above)
    Credible case (4-inch line rupture, jet and pool fire): 58, 102 and 179 m to 37.5, 12.5 and 4 kW/m². Worst case (bullet fireball): 296, 551 and 996 m. Vapour-cloud explosion: 621 m to 0.3 bar, 786 m to 0.1 bar.

One reading is worth pausing on. The overfill function passes on probability and fails on architecture, so the fix is a redundant or higher-integrity final element, not a shorter proof-test interval. That comes from a check the toolkit runs, not from judgement added afterwards.

The limit that matters most for propane. The toolkit warns on this very study: propane is heavier than air, and the flash-fire and toxic footprints use a neutral Gaussian plume, so consider a dense-gas override. Dense clouds spread along the ground and reach further than a neutral plume. The vapour-cloud and flash-fire distances above (0.3 bar at 621 m, 0.1 bar at 786 m) and the risk that depends on them should therefore be treated as optimistic until footprints from a validated dense-gas tool such as DNV Phast or RISKCURVES are fed in through the overrides hook. The toolkit then still does the frequency, individual-risk, ALARP and mitigation bookkeeping.

What this terminal needs next

  • Replace the placeholders. Pump and equipment frequencies from a source such as the OGP 434-1 data or the UK HSE FRED rows, local weather and wind data, and the actual population around the site.
  • Add a dense-gas footprint. Run the propane releases in a validated tool and feed the footprints through overrides.
  • Choose the criteria deliberately. This run used the UK R2P2 preset; no single Indian tolerable-risk criterion exists in the public documents the toolkit read, so an Indian site should supply a custom one.
  • Do what the toolkit lists as not implemented. Layout and separation distances, hazardous-area classification extents, and fire-water design to the governing standards remain manual work.
  • Hold the workshop. The HAZID and HAZOP registers are a starting point; the studies are a team exercise.

The same flow applies to other oil and gas assets, a gas processing plant, a refinery unit or an offshore platform, with different modules in play (relief and flare systems, hydrocarbon fire, explosion). The terminal here is only the asset the toolkit ships a worked example for.
