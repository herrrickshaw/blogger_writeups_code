A dated post-quantum plan now exists, as a draft. The Controller of Certifying Authorities, under the Ministry of Electronics and IT, has published a draft report by an expert committee set up on 9 May 2026 (dated October 2026). Its comment deadline of 20 November has been extended to 10 December 2026. The draft proposes a CCA-led national post-quantum PKI test bed “to commence in January 2027” and a phased programme running to “July 2030 onward”.

It says July 2030 “is not a universal deadline” and that each use case should set its own date for ending pre-quantum-only signing.

It covers digital-signature and certificate services (eSign, certifying authorities) and recommends hybrid key exchange for websites and VPNs; it is not a plan for military or other government networks, and it is a draft, not adopted.

On 22 January 2026 the Chief of Defence Staff, General Anil Chauhan, released a Military Quantum Mission Policy Framework for the Army, Navy and Air Force. It rests on four pillars, quantum communication, quantum computing, quantum sensing and metrology, and quantum materials and devices, and ties the services to the civilian National Quantum Mission (NQM). The document itself has not been published; what is public is the list of pillars and a statement that it sets phased milestones and governing bodies.

Laid against what the services and the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) have actually built and bought, the four pillars are at very different stages. Secure communication has equipment on contract and software being rolled out. Computing is a laboratory processor, sensing is a set of prototype contracts, and the materials pillar is still waiting for its first Indian machine.

Communication: the one pillar the forces have bought

Quantum key distribution sends encryption keys as single photons, so that anyone reading them in transit disturbs them and is detected. The Army trialled QNu Labs’ system over 150 km with trusted relay nodes in 2022, through the defence ministry’s iDEX start-up programme, and signed a procurement contract on 30 September 2024 to replace algorithm-based encryption on some links. DRDO and IIT Delhi have shown the same idea over 100 km of ordinary telecom fibre and, in 2025, over 1 km of open air using entangled photons, at about 240 bits of key a second.

That rate is the limitation. QKD needs a dedicated fibre or line of sight, and its key rates fall with distance, so it suits fixed links between headquarters, not every radio in the field. Most of the protection against future quantum code-breaking will come from post-quantum cryptography, which is new mathematics running on existing networks. The Army said in June 2025 that applications developed at MCTE Mhow were being integrated across its networks; no deadline for the rest of government has been announced.

Computing, sensing and materials: still in the laboratory

DRDO tested a 6-qubit superconducting processor in August 2024. That is a research device: useful machines need far more qubits and far lower error rates, and every superconducting qubit has to sit near absolute zero. The refrigerators that get it there, dilution refrigerators reaching 20 millikelvin, are imported. On 24 September 2026 DRDO announced it had signed its first high-value contract from a ₹500 crore deep-tech fund with Zero mK India, an Alwar start-up, to build one; its first prototype is due in the fourth quarter of 2027.

Quantum sensing matters to the military because it promises navigation and timing that cannot be jammed or spoofed the way GPS can. DRDO’s missile laboratory has contracted a start-up for a quantum gyroscope and a Rydberg-atom radio sensor, and its Technology Development Fund has asked industry for quantum sensors for guided weapons. These are development contracts; none has a published delivery date.

The money behind the mission

The military framework has no published budget of its own; it leans on the civilian mission. NQM was approved at ₹6,003.65 crore over eight years, about ₹750 crore a year. The budget lines reported so far are much smaller:

Two later Parliament answers on the mission, of 30 July and 12 August 2026 (PIB 2291775 and 2298218), may carry newer figures; this blog could not open them. The figures are scattered across budget documents and press reports, and none comes with a year-by-year table of releases and spending from the Department of Science and Technology, which is the problem the first proposal below addresses.

What would fix this

  • Headquarters Integrated Defence Staff should publish an unclassified summary of the Military Quantum Mission Policy Framework: the milestones by pillar and year, and which service or agency owns each, so progress can be followed without disclosing capabilities.
  • The Department of Science and Technology should publish National Quantum Mission allocations, releases and spending by year and by hub, in one table each year, so the gap between the ₹6,003.65 crore outlay and actual spending is not pieced together from press reports.
  • The National Security Council Secretariat and CERT-In should set a dated plan for moving government and critical-infrastructure networks to post-quantum cryptography, as the Army has begun to do for its own networks.
  • DRDO should report the milestones of its quantum Technology Development Fund projects (the dilution refrigerator, the gyroscope and the RF sensor) against their contracted dates, so the move from imported to Indian hardware can be tracked.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
