NIWE's own wind atlas says Tamil Nadu has installed only about a third of its assessed onshore potential, and almost none of its offshore potential, despite being India's most mature wind state. This piece works through NIWE's numbers, what's actually been built, and why the gap between potential and installed capacity has less to do with wind resource and more to do with an ageing turbine fleet and a transmission grid built for a smaller sector.

Tamil Nadu's Wind Potential, According to NIWE: A Third Built, Two-Thirds Still on the Table

Skip to article content
Text Size
1. NIWE's headline number for Tamil Nadu

The National Institute of Wind Energy's Wind Atlas, assessed at 100m hub height under a 2% land-availability assumption, puts Tamil Nadu's installable onshore wind potential at 33,799 MW, split into three wind-density tiers: Rank I (the best wind quality) 11,251 MW, Rank II 22,153 MW, and Rank III a smaller 395 MW. That sits inside India's national total of 302 GW at 100m — NIWE's later reassessments at taller, more commercially realistic hub heights push the national figure much higher again: 695.5 GW at 120m, 1,163.9 GW at 150m, reflecting how much more of the wind resource becomes usable as turbine towers get taller.

NIWE's 120m and 150m atlases exist for the national total but a Tamil Nadu-specific figure at those taller hub heights was not found in the sourcing for this piece — the 33,799 MW figure is the 100m assessment specifically, and would understate potential at the hub heights modern turbines actually use.

2. How much of that has actually been built

Tamil Nadu's installed wind capacity stood at roughly 11,938 MW as of 31 October 2025 — India's second-largest wind fleet, after Gujarat's 14,493 MW. Tamil Nadu held the #1 spot nationally until 2023, when Gujarat overtook it.

The utilisation math. ~11,938 MW installed against NIWE's 33,799 MW 100m-hub-height potential works out to roughly 35% utilisation — meaning nearly two-thirds of NIWE's own assessed onshore potential in India's most mature wind state remains unbuilt. And that's before accounting for the fact that modern turbines run at 120–150m hub heights, where the same land would yield materially more installable capacity than the 100m figure captures.

3. Offshore: the resource nobody has touched yet

NIWE's separate offshore assessment puts wind potential off the Tamil Nadu coast at roughly 35 GW — a substantial share of India's national offshore estimate (cited at ~91 GW in recent coverage, though offshore assessments have varied across studies and years and shouldn't be treated as a single settled number). Almost none of it is built. The concrete steps taken so far: a LIDAR wind-measurement survey off Dhanushkodi completed in January 2026, with Tamil Nadu's first offshore wind tender expected in February 2026. This is the earliest stage of a resource India has known about for years but has not yet started converting into installed capacity anywhere in the country at meaningful scale.

4. Why the gap, if the resource is there: an ageing fleet, not a wind problem

NIWE's atlas describes a resource; it says nothing about why so much of it sits unbuilt. The actual constraint in Tamil Nadu is structural, not meteorological.

A large share of the state's existing turbine fleet is old, much of it installed during the sector's early-2000s boom years, and running at capacity utilisation factors of just 10–15% — well below the 25–30% CUF that modern, taller, larger-rotor turbines achieve on the exact same sites. Repowering — replacing old, small turbines with fewer, larger, more efficient ones on the same land — is the obvious fix, and Tamil Nadu has a repowering policy aimed at exactly this. But progress has been slower than planned, and developers point to three specific, recurring frictions:

  • Banking-charge disputes — disagreements over how much a generator has to pay to "bank" surplus power for later use, a mechanism repowering projects rely on more heavily during the transition period.
  • Grid-connectivity gaps during transition — a site being repowered typically needs continuous, negotiated grid access while old turbines come down and new ones go up, which existing rules don't handle smoothly.
  • Inadequate evacuation infrastructure — much of the wind corridor still runs on 11kV lines built for a much smaller, older fleet; those lines are already unstable and frequently disrupted, and can't absorb the higher output of repowered turbines without upgrades.

Land availability compounds this: assembling the larger, contiguous parcels that bigger modern turbines (or wind-solar hybrid projects) need is difficult in a wind corridor that's already been built out once.

4a. The biggest single lever: repowering by height, not just by turbine count

NIWE's own numbers make the case for taller towers more clearly than anything else in this piece. Wind speed — and therefore the energy actually extractable — rises with height above ground, because the atmosphere's near-surface layer is slowed and turbulent from friction with terrain, buildings and vegetation. NIWE's national atlas shows exactly how much that matters in practice: assessed potential goes from 302 GW at 100m to 695.5 GW at 120m to 1,163.9 GW at 150m — more than tripling as hub height climbs by just 50 metres. That is not new resource appearing out of nowhere; it is the same wind corridors becoming usable at a scale the 100m assessment simply couldn't see.

Tamil Nadu's ageing fleet is disproportionately short by modern standards — many of the state's early-2000s-vintage turbines sit at hub heights well under 80m, on towers sized for the smaller rotors and lower-capacity machines of that era. Repowering those sites isn't just about swapping in a more efficient turbine of similar size; the real gain comes from going taller on the same footprint, reaching into faster, steadier wind that the original short towers never touched. That's the mechanical reason repowered sites can hit 25–30% CUF against an old fleet's 10–15%: part of the improvement is better turbine technology, but a real part of it is simply standing higher off the ground on the same patch of land India has already cleared, connected and permitted once.

Why this matters for the 33,799 MW figure specifically. Because that number is NIWE's 100m-hub-height assessment (Section 1), and modern repowered turbines in Tamil Nadu's wind corridor typically run well above that height, the state's true installable potential under current turbine technology is almost certainly higher than 33,799 MW — possibly substantially so, if the national 100m-to-120m jump (302 GW → 695.5 GW, more than doubling) is any guide to how much a similar height increase would add in Tamil Nadu specifically. A state-specific 120m or 150m NIWE figure for Tamil Nadu was not found in the sourcing for this piece (see the caveat in Section 1) — but the direction of the correction is clear even without the exact number: height, not just land, is the resource Tamil Nadu has left mostly unclaimed.

The scale of what's riding on this: Tamil Nadu has set a target of sourcing 50% of its electricity from renewables by 2030, against a peak demand projected to rise 71% by 2034-35 (from 20.7 GW to 35.5 GW) — implying roughly 98 GW of total contracted capacity needed by then, across all sources. Wind alone won't close that gap, but at 35% utilisation of NIWE's own onshore estimate and near-zero offshore development, it's the renewable resource with the most obvious unused headroom in the state's own numbers.

NIWE's atlas isn't the constraint. A grid built for a smaller, younger fleet, and a repowering policy still working through banking and evacuation disputes, are the actual reasons two-thirds of Tamil Nadu's assessed wind potential remains on paper.

What this piece does not establish. This piece works from NIWE's published 100m-hub-height atlas figure for Tamil Nadu (33,799 MW); a state-specific figure at the taller 120m/150m hub heights NIWE has separately assessed nationally was not found in the sourcing gathered here, so the true installable potential at the hub heights modern turbines actually use is very likely higher than the headline number this piece uses throughout. The ~35 GW offshore figure and the ~91 GW national offshore estimate it's compared against both come from recent secondary coverage rather than a primary NIWE offshore atlas document, and offshore assessments in India have been revised across multiple studies; treat both as directional rather than final.
