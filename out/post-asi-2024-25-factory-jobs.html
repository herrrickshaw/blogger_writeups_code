India’s Factories Passed 2 Crore Workers. Since 2016-17, Most New Workers Came Through Contractors

The Annual Survey of Industries (ASI) for 2024-25, released by the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI) on 30 September 2026, counts 2.10 crore people working in India’s registered factories, the first time the survey has crossed two crore. Employment rose 7.19% in a year, gross value added 9.59% and total emoluments 12.08%.

MoSPI’s detailed series, which runs to 2023-24, shows how that workforce has been growing. Between 2016-17 and 2023-24 factories added 38.6 lakh workers; 22.5 lakh of them, 58%, were hired through contractors rather than by the factory. Of the 16.0 lakh workers the factories employed directly, 1.2 lakh were women.

Wages to workers grew faster than the number of workers, so the average wage rose by roughly 5% in the year (this blog’s calculation from the two growth rates). Tamil Nadu again had the most factories, 41,221 or 15.4% of the total, ahead of Gujarat (33,084) and Maharashtra (27,379). On value added the order changes: Maharashtra had 15.9% of manufacturing GVA, Gujarat 14.1% and Tamil Nadu 10.9%. Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh and Haryana together employ more than 56% of everyone in registered manufacturing.

Persons engaged grew 31% over these seven years and GVA, in current prices, 80%. The contract share rose every year, from 36.0% to 41.6%, through the pandemic year of 2020-21 and the recovery after it. Reports on the 2023-24 data put that share at its highest since 1997-98. Women’s share of directly employed workers moved the other way, from 20.4% to 18.1%; the number of women directly employed grew 7.6% while directly employed men grew 25%.

Tamil Nadu has the most factories and employs the most people, but its factories are small and add less value per person than the national average: ₹8.4 lakh per person engaged against ₹12.6 lakh. It also relies least on contractors, with 27.5% of workers hired that way. Maharashtra is the opposite case, with fewer factories, the largest share of value added and 54.8% of its workers on contract. Telangana hires almost two-thirds of its factory workers through contractors, the highest of these states.

The pattern does not line up neatly with investment intentions. In this blog’s reading of DPIIT’s IEM lists, only 12% of Tamil Nadu’s large-project filings from 2001 to 2018 were followed by a report of production, the lowest of these thirteen states, while Telangana’s, at 32%, were the highest; across the thirteen states there is no relationship between that follow-through rate and value added per worker. The factory survey and the investment register measure different things, and neither explains the other.

What the survey does and does not cover

ASI covers factories registered under sections 2(m)(i) and 2(m)(ii) of the Factories Act, 1948; it is not the whole of manufacturing, and the large unregistered sector is measured by a different survey. Values are at current prices, so GVA growth includes inflation. Contract workers are those the factory reports as engaged through a contractor, so the count is only as good as what factories report. The 2024-25 detail by state, industry and type of worker is in MoSPI’s factsheet but not yet in its data service, so the long series here stops at 2023-24.

What would fix this

  • MoSPI should put contract workers and directly employed women, by state, in the headline table of every ASI release, next to total employment, so the change in who is being hired is visible on the day the total is reported. Neither appeared in the reported 2024-25 headline figures.
  • MoSPI should load each year’s ASI results into its eSankhyiki data service on the day of release; as of 9 October 2026 the service still ends at 2023-24.
  • The Ministry of Labour and Employment should publish, state by state each year, the number of licensed contractors and of contract workers they report, so the survey’s count can be checked against the regulator’s own records.
  • State industries departments that rank themselves on factory counts should publish value added per worker and the contract share alongside, so a large count of small factories is not read as the same thing as a large industrial base.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
