Mandi harvest discount in India: 18 of 23 crops sell below trend, about Rs 7,300 crore

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • Rs 7,300 crore is the gap, over the past year, between what 18 crops’ mandi arrivals fetched and their trend price, at each crop’s usual seasonal pattern.
  • 53% of soybean’s arrivals land in Oct–Dec, when its price runs 6.3% below trend.
  • +5.3% is paddy’s price in Oct–Dec against trend: the new support price takes effect as the harvest arrives.

The previous piece in this series ranked 23 crops by how much reaches India’s regulated mandis and how much their prices move. This one asks when each crop arrives, and what the price is then. A seller who must sell at harvest sells when everyone else does.

What it means: For 18 of 23 crops the average tonne sold below its trend price, so the timing of sale, not the crop alone, sets part of what a seller gets.

What to do: Lenders and warehouse operators should look first at garlic, mustard and soybean, which store well and show the deepest discounts.

Paddy sells above trend at harvest, where the support price resets

What it means: Where the government buys at the support price in the harvest weeks, the dip shrinks to 2.9% for wheat or reverses for paddy, while soybean in its peak months falls 6.3%.

What to do: Support-price purchases of oilseeds would do most in October to December for soybean and March to May for mustard, the months their prices bottom.

What it means: Wheat, soybean, onion and mustard carry 61% of the total, wheat through volume, against about Rs 4.8 lakh crore of arrivals across all 23 crops.

What to do: Treat the figure as scale, not as money any seller could have banked, because if everyone stored, the seasonal pattern itself would change.

What would fix this

  1. Directorate of Marketing and Inspection: publish a seasonal price index for each crop and state beside the monthly Agmarknet prices.
  2. Warehousing Development and Regulatory Authority: publish monthly electronic warehouse-receipt issuance by commodity and state, to show whether receipt loans reach the deepest-discount crops.
  3. Department of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare: report storage projects financed under the Agriculture Infrastructure Fund by district and crop, for comparison with these discounts.
  4. Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices: report each support-price crop’s harvest-month price against trend in its kharif and rabi price-policy reports.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

How the harvest discount is measured

Prices and arrivals are Agmarknet’s monthly state figures, January 2019 to October 2026; state-months under 50 tonnes are dropped, and all-India prices are arrival-weighted. The trend is a centred 12-month moving average. A month’s seasonal index is its price over the trend, averaged across years.

A month’s arrival share is its average share of a calendar year’s arrivals over 2019–2024, before Agmarknet widened its market coverage in November 2025. The harvest discount is the arrival-weighted seasonal index, less one. A crop-state needs four years of index for every month.

Rebuilding the index from 2019–2024 alone moves no crop’s discount by more than 2.4 points and changes no sign. The rupee figures multiply the past year’s arrivals, valued at state prices, by the discount.

What this piece could not establish

Limits of the data

These are wholesale prices at regulated mandis, mixing varieties and grades, not what farmers were paid after commission and transport. The data does not show who sold, or whether they could have stored.

Storage costs rent, interest and some loss of weight; the earlier maize and rice analysis found the seasonal spread usually did not cover it for those grains. Crops sold outside mandis, or bought directly by government agencies, are undercounted.

November 2026 data, to the 20th, is in the source repository but left out here, because a part-month would distort the 12-month window.
