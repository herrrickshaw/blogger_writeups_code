India has about 101 million people with diabetes, by one estimate, and it also eats about 28 million tonnes of sugar a year. The two facts meet in the sweetener aisle. Sugar-free tablets, drops and drinks are sold on the promise that they spare a sugar bill that people with diabetes cannot afford. One intense sweetener, sucralose, shows up in this blog’s trade registry as a growing import, and it is made from sugar. That raises a question sugar mills are entitled to ask: could they make it?

This piece sets out the demand, the evidence on health, the trade figures, the chemistry and the options for a mill. The conclusion is that sucralose is a chemicals business that happens to start from sugar, and the sugar tonnage is too small to matter. The mill options that look more natural use the assets a mill already has.

The pull: diabetes

The ICMR-INDIAB study, published in The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology in 2023, estimated that 11.4% of Indians, about 101 million people, have diabetes, and 15.3% have prediabetes, about 136 million. The spread between states is wide: Goa at 26.4% and Uttar Pradesh at 4.8%. The 101 million is a projection; the survey ran from 2008 to 2020 across 31 states and union territories and was extrapolated to 2021 with census-type data. Reports note that the rise is levelling in richer states and still continuing elsewhere.

For sweeteners, the point is demand that is not going away. Industry commentary has long said that most intense-sweetener use was by people with diabetes. That older claim was not verified here.

Where the sugar goes

Domestic sugar use was about 28 million tonnes in 2024-25 by the sugar industry’s estimate, and a record 29 million in 2023-24. That is about 20 kg a head, on this blog’s arithmetic. The Indian Sugar and Bio-Energy Manufacturers Association (ISMA) found institutional buyers took 60–65% of demand in 2023-24, up from 50–55% in 2018-19; drinks were the biggest category and confectionery next. Households took the remaining 35–40%, and the study said affluent households were reducing refined sugar and moving to alternatives. So the sweetener question is as much about drinks makers and sweet shops as about the tabletop tablet.

What is permitted, and what is being said

FSSAI permits five artificial sweeteners: saccharin sodium, aspartame, acesulfame potassium, sucralose and neotame, each with a maximum in parts per million by food category. Aspartame, for example, is capped at 700 ppm in soft drinks. A 2020 draft would have required packaged foods with these sweeteners to say so on the label, with warnings for some groups; whether it was finalised was not confirmed.

The safety debate is in three parts, and they should not be run together. In May 2023 the World Health Organization advised against using non-sugar sweeteners to control weight or reduce the risk of non-communicable disease, on the basis of 283 studies that showed no long-term benefit in body fat and possible harms, with low or very low certainty. The advice excludes people who already have diabetes. In July 2023 the cancer agency IARC classed aspartame as “possibly carcinogenic”, while the joint expert committee JECFA kept its limit of 40 mg per kg of body weight a day; the release said an adult of 70 kg would need more than 9 to 14 cans of diet drink a day to exceed it. For sucralose, a 2023 laboratory study from North Carolina found that sucralose-6-acetate, a compound reported both as a manufacturing impurity and as a metabolite, damaged DNA in human blood cells. Those were test-tube experiments, and the authors’ comparison with a European intake threshold has not been confirmed by a regulator. No human trial of sucralose and blood sugar turned up in the searches.

Who buys these sweeteners is not the same as who is advised to. The WHO advice excludes people with diabetes, and Zydus Wellness’s Sugar Free, launched in 1988, says its share of the Indian tabletop market is above 90%, with aspartame, sucralose and stevia variants. That share is a company claim from Nielsen figures it cites.

The trade: a small and growing import

Sucralose is the one intense sweetener this blog’s trade registry singles out. Under tariff line 29321400, India imported $28.4 million in FY26 and exported about $0.7 million, a deficit of $27.7 million. Value rose 49.3%, volume 71.0% and the unit price fell 12.4%. A third-party aggregation of customs data puts FY25 imports at ₹161.79 crore, consistent with that growth. Basic customs duty is shown as 7.5% on two tariff sites, which was not checked against the official schedule.

On the supply side, China holds more than 85–90% of world capacity by two market reports, and global output was above 23,000 tonnes in 2024 at an average price near $22 a kilogram. At that price, $28.4 million is on the order of 1,300 tonnes, about 5–6% of world output; this is this blog’s arithmetic, since the FY26 price is not known. Sucralose is about 600 times as sweet as sugar, a commonly quoted figure. So 1,300 tonnes does the sweetening work of roughly 775,000 tonnes of sugar, about 2.7% of consumption. That is what the import displaces if it replaces sugar one for one.

How sucralose is made

Sucralose is sugar with three of its hydroxyl groups swapped for chlorine. The route in the patents is three steps. Sugar is first acetylated at one position to give sucrose-6-acetate, at reported yields of about 88–91%. That is chlorinated with a Vilsmeier reagent, made from thionyl chloride, or phosphorus pentachloride, and dimethylformamide (DMF), which swaps the three groups; patents describe temperatures from −20 to 120°C over 5 to 15 hours, and the crude product in the examples was only 68–73% pure. Last, the acetate is removed and the sucralose crystallised: one patent reports 99% purity at 79.9% yield, another over 98% purity at above 95% yield, and a crystallisation patent reports 99.6% by HPLC. Between 80% and 90% of the DMF can be recovered.

The sugar itself is the easy input. Sucralose weighs about 1.16 times a molecule of sucrose, so each kilogram needs at least about 0.86 kg of sugar, and in practice more. For India’s imports that is a minimum of roughly 1,100 tonnes, a few thousand under any plausible yield, against 28 million tonnes eaten. The hard parts are the chlorination, the reagents and the solvent and effluent handling.

Options for a sugar mill

No Indian sugar mill was found making sucralose. Online trade directories list a dozen or so Indian sellers of sucralose powder in Ahmedabad, Mumbai and Hyderabad, and a few describe themselves as manufacturers, but others name Chinese-made, branded product, and none gave a plant, capacity or licence that could be checked. So whether any of them makes it, rather than repacks it, is unverified; confirming it would take a factory registration, an FSSAI licence and a certificate of analysis. Three things follow. First, supplying sugar to a sucralose maker is real but trivial: the tonnage is a rounding error for a mill. Second, making sucralose is a different industry. It needs chlorination chemistry, a licence for it and a price set by Chinese producers; a 2014 trade report said smaller makers were expected to exit when the price fell. A mill’s strengths are cane, steam, power and, for those with distilleries, effluent handling, and none of those is chlorination. Third, the options that use what a mill already has are the ones with evidence in the table: bagasse already sits at the gate, and its xylose can become xylitol, which, as a sugar alcohol, falls outside the WHO’s sweetener advice. But the bagasse studies are laboratory ones, and bagasse has a buyer in the mill’s own boiler, as this blog’s piece on liquefying it noted.

What would fix this

  • The Department of Commerce should publish sucralose, aspartame, saccharin and acesulfame imports by origin and tonnage at the eight-digit tariff line, so it is known who supplies India and at what price rather than inferred from a value figure.
  • FSSAI should publish its non-sugar sweetener intake estimates against the acceptable daily intake and state whether the 2020 labelling draft was notified, since the draft’s status could not be confirmed.
  • The Indian Council of Medical Research should report non-sugar sweetener use among people with diabetes and prediabetes, by sweetener, in its next INDIAB round, because the WHO advice excludes this group and India has no figures of its own.
  • The Department of Chemicals and Petrochemicals should publish a costed route for sucralose within its import-substitution screening, with the technology licence, capital and effluent cost, so a mill or chemical firm can decide on numbers.
  • The Central Pollution Control Board should state the solvent-recovery and effluent norms that would apply to a sucralose plant, covering DMF, thionyl chloride off-gases and chlorinated waste, so compliance cost is known before a plant is planned.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
