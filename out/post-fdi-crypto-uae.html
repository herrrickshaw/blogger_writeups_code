In 2025 an Abu Dhabi fund paid US$2 billion for a stake in Binance in a stablecoin, the largest investment ever settled that way. The UAE is building the rails for more: dirham stablecoins, licensed dollar tokens, tokenised property and a programme to attract crypto firms as foreign investors. The research is less flattering: crypto has so far eased cross-border money mostly where governments were trying to stop it. India, the UAE’s partner in a central-bank digital currency pilot, allows no FDI to be paid in crypto at all, and is betting on central-bank money instead.

The deal that made the case

In March 2025 MGX, the Abu Dhabi technology investor backed by Mubadala and G42, announced a US$2 billion minority investment in Binance, the world’s largest crypto exchange. In May, World Liberty Financial, a crypto venture linked to the family of US President Donald Trump, said the deal would be settled in its new dollar stablecoin, USD1. It was reported as the largest investment ever paid in a stablecoin, and at the time it made up a large share of USD1’s circulating supply. MGX later said it chose USD1 because of its “compliance history”, though the coin had launched only weeks earlier; the political links drew sustained criticism in the US press.

Leave the politics aside and the transaction is a working example of something statisticians and regulators have not fully caught up with: a cross-border equity stake, the textbook definition of foreign direct investment, paid not through banks but by moving tokens on a public blockchain.

The UAE has built more of the plumbing for this than any other large economy. The main pieces:

All of this sits inside an investment push. UNCTAD’s 2026 World Investment Report ranks the UAE ninth in the world for FDI received in 2025, at US$48 billion. In November 2025 the UAE set up a US$10 billion National Investment Fund to double annual inflows to US$65 billion by 2031. It has also signed the OECD’s agreement on the Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework, under which crypto platforms report users’ transactions to tax authorities, and its federal anti-money-laundering law now explicitly covers offences committed through virtual assets.

Why the UAE is doing this

The pilots are part of a deliberate pitch for investment. In July 2022 the Ministry of Economy launched NextGen FDI, aimed at bringing 300 digital companies to the UAE within a year, with fast-tracked licences through partner free zones such as Dubai Internet City, DIFC and ADGM, accelerated bank accounts with Emirates NBD and Wio, bulk and golden visas, and help with office space and housing. Later ministry releases say it had brought in more than 90 companies, including Coinbase, Ripple, Polygon and Qualcomm, and was expected to add about US$500 million to the economy. Fluent Finance, the stablecoin firm above, came in through this programme.

In February 2026 the Ministry of Investment published a whitepaper, From Capital Exporter to Financial Architect: The UAE’s Evolving Role in Global Finance, setting out the case. It reports that FDI into the UAE rose 49% in 2024 to US$45.6 billion while global flows fell 11%, putting the UAE among the world’s top ten destinations, and that finance and insurance have accounted for 21% of inward FDI stock since 2022. It presents virtual-asset regulation as part of the offer: Dubai’s Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority, set up in 2022, as the world’s first independent regulator for virtual assets; RAK DAO as the first free zone built for digital-asset firms; and the dirham stablecoin planned by IHC, ADQ and First Abu Dhabi Bank. It cites the UAE’s third place in Henley’s 2024 ranking of crypto-friendly countries.

The logic is that crypto firms are themselves foreign investors — they set up regional headquarters, hire staff and lease offices — and that clear rules attract them. Most of the FDI the UAE counts from this sector is ordinary FDI by crypto companies, paid in dollars and dirhams through banks. Investment actually paid in tokens, like MGX’s, is still the exception.

What the research says about crypto and cross-border investment

There is almost no academic work on FDI paid in crypto, because there has been very little of it. There is a growing literature on crypto and cross-border capital flows more generally, and its main finding is consistent: crypto makes it easier to move money across borders mainly where governments are trying to stop it from moving.

Read together, these studies say crypto lowers the cost of moving money mainly by getting round controls and bank checks. That is the opposite of what an investment-seeking government wants from FDI, which is long-term, visible and registered. Where FDI already moves freely through banks, as into the UAE, crypto settlement saves time and fees but does not open a new door. Where the door is shut, it is mostly used as a way out. That is why the UAE’s pilots are built on licensed stablecoins, bank deposit tokens and central-bank digital money rather than on bitcoin.

Is it FDI if it is paid in crypto?

Yes, but the payment may not be counted where statisticians expect it. FDI is defined by what is bought — a lasting stake of 10% or more of the voting power in a foreign enterprise — not by how it is paid. The IMF’s new balance-of-payments manual, BPM7, adopted in 2025, sets out how crypto-assets themselves are recorded:

  • Crypto without an issuer behind it, such as bitcoin, is treated as a non-produced, non-financial asset, like land or a licence, and recorded in the capital account. Buying bitcoin abroad is not FDI.
  • Stablecoins and other tokens backed by an issuer’s liability are financial instruments.
  • Some platform tokens may be treated like equity when held across borders.

So MGX’s stake in Binance is FDI from the UAE, and the USD1 that paid for it is the financial instrument used. The practical problem is measurement. Most countries, India included, build their FDI statistics largely from bank reporting: each inward remittance passes through an authorised bank, which reports it. A payment that moves from one blockchain wallet to another never touches that chain of reporting unless the investor or the recipient declares it.

Where India stands

The UAE is India’s seventh-largest source of FDI equity, with cumulative inflows of about US$25.6 billion to March 2026 according to DPIIT, including US$2.45 billion in April–December 2025. Every rupee of it came through the banking system, because Indian rules allow nothing else. Under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, an Indian company receiving foreign investment must receive the money by inward remittance through an authorised bank or through a non-resident investor’s bank account in India, and report it on the RBI’s FIRMS portal. Crypto-assets are not “currency” or “foreign exchange” under FEMA. A UAE investor cannot pay for Indian shares in AED stablecoins, USD1 or bitcoin.

India’s route is central-bank money instead. The RBI’s 2023 agreement with the Central Bank of the UAE covers joint pilots of cross-border CBDC transactions, starting with trade and remittances; the RBI’s 2025–26 annual report says it is working with the UAE to operationalise the pilot. Its new Unified Markets Interface already settles tokenised certificates of deposit and corporate bonds in wholesale e-rupees at home. A cross-border link between the two would let a UAE investor pay for a tokenised Indian bond or share in central-bank money, with the transaction visible to both central banks — tokenisation without crypto.

The UAE is testing investment paid in private stablecoins. India is testing investment paid in central-bank money. Both want faster settlement; they disagree about who should issue the token.

What could go wrong

Visibility. FDI paid on-chain can bypass bank reporting. The UAE’s commitment to the OECD crypto reporting framework and its licensing of stablecoin issuers partly close this gap; neither India nor most other countries yet track the settlement medium of investment flows.

Money laundering and sanctions. The UAE left the Financial Action Task Force’s “grey list” of jurisdictions under increased monitoring in February 2024 and has since tightened enforcement; the central bank shut down and fined exchange houses in a 2025 campaign. Stablecoins can be frozen by their issuers, which helps enforcement, but large transfers between unhosted wallets remain hard to trace.

Issuer risk. A stablecoin is only as good as its reserves and its issuer. USD1 was weeks old when MGX used it for US$2 billion, and its issuer is a politically connected private company. A central-bank digital currency or a bank-issued deposit token carries no such question.

What would fix this

  • The Reserve Bank of India should state publicly whether FDI may be settled through regulated tokenised money — wholesale CBDC, bank deposit tokens or a licensed stablecoin — and on what conditions, so that the answer for an investor in Abu Dhabi does not depend on reading FEMA’s silence.
  • The RBI and the Central Bank of the UAE should extend their bilateral CBDC pilot from remittances and trade to investment flows, with FIRMS reporting built in, so that a UAE investor can pay for Indian securities in central-bank money and still appear in India’s FDI data.
  • DPIIT and the RBI should add the settlement medium to FDI reporting, so that any future inflow paid through tokenised money is counted separately and its scale is known.
  • The UAE’s Ministry of Investment should report what share of the country’s FDI is settled in stablecoins or other tokens, so that the pilots’ contribution to its US$65 billion target can be measured.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
