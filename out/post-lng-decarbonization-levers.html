There is no single "decarbonize LNG" technology. Every recent project layers several levers on top of each other, and the levers are not interchangeable: cheap, mature process fixes cap out well above zero-carbon; the one lever big enough to change the picture — electrification — only delivers on that promise if the power behind it is actually low-carbon; and the last mile, capturing the CO2 already in the feed gas, is a cost add-on with no efficiency payback at all. This is a generic, source-checked comparison of the levers plants are actually building against today, not a ranking of which one "wins."

How the Levers Stack, Roughly Low-Effort to High-Impact

  1. Process/incremental efficiency (turbo-expanders, AGRU chemistry, flare/APC) — cheap, mature, compounds with everything else, but caps out well above zero-carbon.
  2. Combined cycle — the biggest "free" step-change for a conventional gas-turbine plant; roughly cost-neutral.
  3. Water/hybrid cooling — a real but secondary lever, increasingly complicated by water-scarcity siting constraints rather than a simple win.
  4. Electrification — the largest lever by far, now commercially proven, but its emissions benefit is only as good as the power source, and its main constraint has shifted from technology to grid/transmission availability (see LNG Canada Phase 2, ADNOC Ruwais).
  5. CCS — the lever for what's left after electrification and efficiency (feed-gas CO2 in particular) — moving from pilot to real projects now, but adds cost rather than saving it.
Caveat. "Typical CO2 reduction" figures in the table are not on a common baseline — some are stated against a simple open-cycle plant, some against an air-cooled baseline at the same site, and electrification's "up to 90%+" holds only when the grid supplying it is itself low-carbon. Treat every percentage as lever-specific, not as a single ladder that sums to 100%.

The Net Takeaway

The real-world path is layered, not a single choice: efficiency plus combined cycle gets a conventional gas-turbine plant into the ~0.12–0.17 t-CO2/t-LNG range; electrification, grid-dependent, goes materially lower from there; and CCS is the backstop for what electrification can't reach, feed-gas CO2 in particular. The binding constraint across nearly every recent project is not the liquefaction technology itself — it is external power/grid availability (transmission, renewable supply) and, for CCS, storage and transport infrastructure.
