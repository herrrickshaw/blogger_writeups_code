Every diamond polished in Surat is polished with diamond — a powder of crushed synthetic crystals worked into an iron wheel with oil. India imported ₹239.5 crore of that powder in 2024–25 and exported about a ninth as much, and most of it comes from China, which put fine diamond powder on an export-licence list last October and has only paused it until 10 November 2026. India now runs thousands of lab-grown diamond reactors and has just approved ₹9,817.5 crore more in Odisha. Grinding those stones into powder is the obvious-sounding answer. The carats and the prices say it isn't one.

What actually polishes a diamond

Only diamond cuts diamond. A polisher in Surat presses the stone against a scaife — a cast-iron wheel roughly 30 cm across, spinning at around 2,500 rpm — whose surface has been charged with diamond powder mixed into olive oil or a similar carrier, working down from coarse grits of about 40 microns to below one micron for the final finish. The same powder, graded more tightly and suspended in oil, water or glycol, becomes the lapping pastes and slurries used to finish carbide dies, optical lenses, metallography samples, dental tools, hard-disk platters, and the silicon-carbide and sapphire wafers that power electronics and LEDs are built on.

Almost none of that powder is natural diamond anymore. The US Geological Survey puts world output of manufactured industrial diamond at more than 15.5 billion carats in 2024, against about 39 million carats of natural industrial stones — natural material is roughly a quarter of one per cent of supply. Synthetic grit is made in hydraulic cubic presses at high pressure and high temperature (HPHT), and micron powder is the fine fraction left from crushing and grading that grit. China is the largest producer by a wide margin, concentrated in Henan province, where firms such as Zhongnan Diamond and Henan Huanghe Whirlwind run capacity in the billions of carats a year. It supplied 77% of US imports of diamond grit, dust and powder over 2020–23, and about half of world exports of HS 7105.10 by value in 2024 — far more by carats, since Chinese powder is the cheapest in the trade.

India's bill, and who it pays

India's customs data separate diamond dust and powder (HS 710510) from gem diamonds, which makes the dependence easy to see. In 2024–25 India imported ₹239.49 crore of it, up 9.9% on the year, and exported ₹27.45 crore — a net deficit of ₹212 crore. At the year's average exchange rate that is about US$28 million, close to the roughly US$29 million of HS 7105 imports from China that UN Comtrade data record for 2024. The Indian Bureau of Mines' latest yearbook with a country breakdown puts 2021–22 imports at about 1,238 million carats worth ₹286 crore — about ₹2.30 a carat — with China supplying roughly 97% of the carats.

The grinding-wheel line is the contrast worth noticing. Indian makers such as Wendt (India) and Grindwell Norton turn diamond grit into finished wheels and exported 56% more of them in 2024–25, mostly to Germany and the US — though much of the German jump appears to be shipments to Indian abrasives companies' own German subsidiaries rather than new outside customers. Downstream, India adds value. Upstream, the grit and powder those wheels and pastes are made from is bought in.

The domestic paste and slurry trade is built the same way. Surat and Mumbai firms that describe themselves as manufacturers on trade portals mostly import bulk powder, blend it with a carrier, and pack it under their own name; the datasheet behind one Indian seller's "Splendis" diamond slurry is a Swiss manufacturer's (Microdiamant's) product sheet. Retail listings show where the money is made: bulk mesh powder sells in Surat for about ₹16.50–20 a gram, a domestic paste brand for ₹60 a gram at every grit from coarse to fine, and imported US-branded paste for ₹380–505 a gram. Price tracks the brand and the channel, not the fineness of the diamond.

The obvious-sounding fix: grind up lab-grown diamonds

India has spent the last five years becoming a lab-grown diamond power. Surat alone is estimated to run 4,000–6,000 of the country's 8,000–10,000 chemical vapour deposition (CVD) reactors, and the largest grower, KIRA, has been reported by the Gem & Jewellery Export Promotion Council's magazine at 4,000 reactors. The 2023–24 Budget gave IIT Madras ₹242.96 crore over five years for an India Centre for Lab-grown Diamond and cut customs duty on lab-grown diamond seeds to nil. On 17 June 2026, Odisha's High-Level Clearance Authority approved three lab-grown diamond plants in Khordha district worth ₹9,817.5 crore — KIRA Diam LLP (₹7,000 crore, 5,100 jobs), Anjali Labtech (₹1,617.5 crore, 2,000 jobs) and Grow Magnificent (₹1,200 crore, 1,000 jobs). With gem prices collapsing — lab-grown stones fell from about ₹60,000 to ₹20,000 a carat within a year, and polished lab-grown exports slid from US$1.68 billion in 2022–23 to US$1.13 billion in 2025–26 — turning surplus or unsaleable stones into polishing powder sounds like a neat way to kill two problems at once.

The numbers don't allow it, for two reasons.

  • Carats. At the reported average of about 125 carats per reactor per month, 8,000–10,000 reactors grow roughly 12–15 million carats of rough a year. India imported about 1,238 million carats of powder in 2021–22. Grinding up every stone the entire industry grows would cover around 1% of the powder bill by weight.
  • Price. Rough lab-grown diamond imported into India averaged about US$35 a carat in 2023 even after the price collapse; imported diamond powder costs about US$0.03 a carat at India's 2021–22 average, and US$0.21 at the US import average for 2025. A carat of gem-quality lab-grown rough is worth somewhere between about 170 and 1,200 times a carat of powder. No grower grinds a sellable stone.

What is left is genuine waste — cracked or heavily included stones and the polycrystalline rim that forms around a CVD crystal. That can be milled: laboratory work has crushed CVD diamond into powder, Chinese sellers already advertise "lab-grown diamond" abrasive powder, and at least two Indian growers, KIRA and ABD Diamonds, market industrial-grade lab-grown powder alongside their gems, though neither discloses volumes. It fits best in the dearer polycrystalline niche, where retail prices run from under US$0.50 to several dollars a carat — not in the bulk monocrystalline grit that Surat's scaifes consume. The powder India actually imports is a by-product of an industrial HPHT press business measured in billions of carats, and India has no such business at scale.

India's dependence is on HPHT grit presses, not on gem growing. A lab-grown boom, however large, is the wrong machine for the job.

Nor is building those presses an easy business case. China's own producers are oversupplied: Henan Huanghe Whirlwind reported a net loss equal to about 61% of revenue in its latest year, while India's listed abrasives makers earn net margins between about 3.5% (Carborundum Universal) and 13.8% (Grindwell Norton). Cheap Chinese powder is exactly why nobody has built HPHT grit capacity in India — and why the dependence has never looked like a problem.

Why it now looks like one

On 9 October 2025, China's Ministry of Commerce and General Administration of Customs issued Announcement No. 55, placing export licensing on "superhard materials": synthetic diamond micropowder and single crystals of specified sizes, diamond wire saws, grinding wheels, and DC arc plasma CVD equipment and process technology. It was due to take effect on 8 November. On 7 November, Announcement No. 70 suspended it — along with China's other export controls announced that month — until 10 November 2026, six weeks from now. A licence is not a ban, and the suspension may be extended. But a rule that would require a Chinese export licence for the powder behind most of India's diamond polishing is sitting on a timer, and India has no domestic fallback at scale. The same pattern — finished-goods strength sitting on a Chinese upstream input — runs through this blog's coverage of rare-earth magnets and pharmaceutical ingredients.

Demand is also about to broaden. Diamond slurries are the standard abrasive for lapping silicon-carbide and sapphire wafers, and India's first front-end semiconductor fab at Dholera is targeting production in 2027; any wafer-finishing work that follows would add a new, precision-grade call on imported diamond consumables. That is an inference from the fab plans, not a sourcing commitment this piece could find.

What would fix this

  • The Directorate General of Commercial Intelligence and Statistics and CBIC should split HS 71051000 into fine micron powder and coarser grit at the 8-digit level, mirroring the size-based line China's export control uses, so India can measure how much of its import bill would actually fall under a Chinese licence.
  • IIT Madras's India Centre for Lab-grown Diamond, and the Department of Science and Technology that funds it, should add HPHT industrial grit synthesis and micron-powder classification to its mandate, with a pilot press-and-grading line. The country's exposure is to industrial HPHT grit, not to gem growing or quantum-grade CVD.
  • GJEPC and the Surat Diamond Association should organise pooled procurement or a three-to-six-month buffer of polishing powder for small units before 10 November 2026, since most polishing workshops buy powder in small lots and have the least ability to absorb a licensing delay.
  • The Bureau of Indian Standards should publish a standard for diamond micron-powder grading, drawing on ISO 6106 and the FEPA and ANSI systems importers already quote, so that any domestic producer — including a lab-grown grower selling reject material as powder — can certify its grades against imports.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
