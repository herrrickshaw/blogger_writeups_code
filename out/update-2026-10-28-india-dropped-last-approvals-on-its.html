India Dropped the Last Approvals on Its Data-Centre Tax Holiday. It Never Said What the Holiday Costs

In the February Budget the Finance Minister offered foreign cloud companies a tax holiday to 2047: if they serve customers outside India from data centres in India, that income is exempt. The Finance Act made it law with conditions attached, including government notification of each foreign company and each data centre. On 17 August the President signed the Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Act 2026, which removes those notifications and lets the data centre be leased rather than owned by its Indian operator. Nasscom called it a step towards tax certainty.

This piece sets out what changed, who stands to benefit, what India collects and gives up, and how that compares with the power and water the same campuses need. It is not tax advice.

Updated 28 October 2026. Checked against the Gazette of India: the Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Act, 2026, assented to on 17 August 2026 (CG-DL-E-17082026-275521), matches the provisions described here. It defines a qualifying data centre as one “operated by an Indian company, whether by way of owning or leasing” that “satisfies such other conditions as may be prescribed”, so the old scheme approval is replaced by rules still to come; the table now says so. The Act is deemed to apply from 1 April 2026 and repeals the June ordinance (CG-DL-E-05062026-273164). No figure changed.

The changes turn an approval scheme into an entitlement. Before, a hyperscaler had to be named in a government notification and use a data centre approved under a MeitY scheme. Now any foreign company that meets the conditions and files the prescribed information qualifies, and so does capacity rented from a colocation company, which is how most cloud providers start in a new market. Nasscom’s point was that tax certainty should not depend on which entity happened to be named in an approval.

The Act was introduced on 4 August, passed by both Houses by 10 August, and received assent on 17 August, replacing an ordinance issued in June. The enacted text, read in the Gazette of India (CG-DL-E-17082026-275521), matches what PRS described in the Bill as introduced. It is deemed to apply from 1 April 2026, repeals the June ordinance, and leaves the remaining conditions for data centres to rules that had not been notified, as reported.

Who benefits

The beneficiaries are the foreign cloud companies building or renting large capacity in India, named in this blog’s piece on the gigawatt data-centre bets: Google (with AdaniConneX and Airtel in Visakhapatnam), Microsoft (Hyderabad), Amazon Web Services (Mumbai and Hyderabad) and Meta (leasing from Reliance in Jamnagar). The leasing change matters most for those that rent rather than build, and for operators such as NTT, Sify, CtrlS, Yotta and STT, whose business is renting capacity. Sales to customers in India do not qualify; they must go through an Indian reseller and are taxed in India.

What India collects, and what it gives up

India still taxes the Indian company that runs the data centre. Under the 15% cost-plus safe harbour, an Indian operator serving a related foreign cloud company can report a profit of 15% on its costs. On Rs 100 of costs it charges Rs 115, earns Rs 15 and pays about Rs 3.78 in tax at the 25.17% corporate rate: about 3.3% of what the foreign company pays it. What is exempt is the foreign company’s income from selling computing to customers abroad, which can be several times the cost of the capacity it runs on.

No official estimate of the revenue forgone was found, and none could be made honestly without knowing how much global cloud business will be booked through Indian capacity. To give a sense of scale, take a 100 MW campus running at 70% load with a power usage effectiveness of 1.4: it uses about 858 GWh a year, which at an assumed Rs 7 a unit costs about Rs 601 crore. If power is half the operator’s costs, its total costs are about Rs 1,202 crore and the safe harbour yields about Rs 45 crore a year of Indian tax. That is an illustration built on stated assumptions, not an estimate of any real campus.

Assumptions: average IT load 70%, PUE 1.4, power Rs 7/kWh, power 50% of operating cost, 25.17% tax on the safe-harbour margin. Change any of them and the result moves proportionately.

The trade: tax, power and water

The same campus, if cooled by open evaporative towers, would use about 3.68 million litres of water a day, by the arithmetic in this blog’s piece on data-centre cooling. On the illustration above, India would collect about Rs 338 of tax for every kilolitre of cooling water such a campus used, while the foreign company’s export income is untaxed. The point is not that the exemption is wrong: India is competing with Singapore, Malaysia and the Gulf for these campuses, and the investment, jobs and fibre they bring count too. It is that the tax holiday is the one part of the bargain the government has now made unconditional, while the power and water the campuses draw are still negotiated site by site with states. The record FDI of 2025-26 came with large outflows; how much of the data-centre money stays in India will depend on contracts this Act does not touch.

The government-securities exemption, which the June ordinance introduced, is the costliest-looking change for the exchequer: foreign investors’ interest income from government bonds was previously taxed at 20%. The Payment and Settlement Systems amendment matters for anyone who pays by UPI; this blog looked at what UPI fees could mean for petrol pumps and rural payments earlier this year.

What would fix this

  • The Finance Ministry’s annual statement of revenue forgone should list the data-centre exemption separately, with the number of foreign companies claiming it and the income exempted.
  • The prescribed information form should require each claimant to report the data centres it uses, their IT load, and their power and water use, so the exemption can be compared with what the campuses draw.
  • States allocating power and water to data-centre campuses should publish the allocation and the tariff, since the central tax regime no longer gives the government a project-by-project approval point.
  • The safe-harbour margin should be reviewed after three years against the margins actually reported by Indian operators.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
