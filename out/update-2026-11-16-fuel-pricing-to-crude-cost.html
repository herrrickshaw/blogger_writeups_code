Petrol and diesel at the pump are not a simple pass-through of the crude oil price — they run through a fixed-rate excise duty, a dealer commission and a percentage state VAT before they reach the retail selling price (RSP). This piece builds that chain from PPAC's own published price structure, re-prices it against live crude benchmarks from Rigzone and OilPriceAPI, and runs it across all 35 state capitals to show how much of a crude move actually reaches the pump — and how much gets held back or amplified by tax design.

A notification of 26 March 2026 cut the special additional excise duty from ₹13 to ₹3 a litre on petrol and from ₹10 to nil on diesel, with pump prices left unchanged at the time (Moneylife); with the other components unchanged, that leaves central excise at about ₹11.90 and ₹7.80 a litre. Four price rises from 15 May 2026 then took Delhi petrol to ₹102.12 and diesel to ₹95.20, as reported in this blog’s fuel-retail piece. The scenario prices below therefore start from an out-of-date base, and the excise “cushion” the government has already used is smaller than the table implies. Petrol and diesel sit entirely outside GST — Table 8.16A of the Ready Reckoner lists them (with crude oil and ATF) under "products on which GST is not levied." Instead they carry a four-part central excise stack, each component a flat ₹/litre regardless of the crude price: Basic Excise Duty, Special Additional Excise Duty, the Road & Infrastructure Cess, and the Agriculture Infrastructure & Development Cess. Petrol's stack (₹21.90/l: 1.40+13.00+5.00+2.50) is heavier than diesel's (₹17.80/l: 1.80+10.00+2.00+4.00) — a deliberate policy tilt against private motoring versus freight and agriculture. Because none of these four components move with crude, they are the lever government has historically pulled to cushion pump prices when crude spikes, and the reason the "full pass-through" scenarios below likely overstate how much of a crude rally would actually reach the pump.

The 25 Sep 2026 row uses the same formula and the same held FX rate (₹88.32/$) applied to $101.07/bbl, PPAC's own Indian Basket figure for that date as reported by Business Standard and Yahoo Finance — not an OilPriceAPI/Rigzone pull like the other rows, since this piece's research environment could not reach either live-quote provider or ppac.gov.in directly at the time of this update; a real 25 September rupee rate would also differ from the held ₹88.32/$, which this row does not model, consistent with every other row in this table.

The scenario range makes the excise lever visible: a 97% swing in crude (past-year low to high) would, on a naive full pass-through, move Delhi petrol by ≈43% (₹87.65 to ₹125.71) — a smaller swing than crude's own, because the flat-rate excise and commission layers don't move at all, and only the dealer-price and VAT layers scale. In practice the swing at the pump has historically been smaller still, because government has repeatedly cut excise duty when crude spiked (and raised it when crude fell) specifically to smooth this pass-through — a policy choice this build-up deliberately does not model, so as to isolate what the tax structure alone would do.

5. Enter a crude price, pick a blend · E0 to E35, all 36 states and UTs

Petrol at the pump also runs through an ethanol blend before it reaches the tank. Ethanol is excise-exempt, so the more of the litre it makes up, the less central excise the government collects on that litre — and, as the map below shows, that foregone excise outweighs ethanol's own cost premium over crude-linked petrol, so a cost-reflective blended price actually falls slightly as the blend rises. This map reuses the India state boundaries from this site's grain-cultivation/FCI-storage map, re-priced state by state.

Capacity is not the constraint. India's installed ethanol distillery capacity is roughly 2,000 crore litres for ESY2025-26 (CareEdge estimate), against 904.8 crore litres actually drawn by PSU OMCs in ESY2024-25 (PPAC Table 6.10) — about 1,095 crore litres of spare, already-built capacity sitting unused. Because ethanol's cost is fixed annually by government notification rather than tracking crude, a higher blend share structurally dampens how much a crude spike passes through to the pump: modelling the FY2021-22–FY2025-26 crude range ($69.89–$93.15/bbl) through the build-up above, petrol's cost-reflective price would have swung ₹15.43/litre at E0 versus ₹12.34/litre at E20 — about 20% less volatile. That spare capacity means blending can be pushed harder during a crude shock without waiting on new plants; the real limit is feedstock supply (46.4% of ethanol now comes from maize, per Table 6.10A) competing with food-security demand for the same grain, not distillery capacity itself.

In practice, pump prices today do not vary by blend grade — OMCs sell E10/E20/E0 at the same notified price and absorb the difference internally; this is the cost-reflective price implied by the tax structure, not a price you will see posted differently at two pumps.

6. Checked against the OMCs' own websites · coverage, not live cross-validation

IOCL, BPCL and HPCL were checked directly (5 August 2026) to see whether any publishes a live, scrapable multi-city price table that could validate the figures above against today's actual pump price. None does:

  • IOCL (iocl.com/petrol-diesel-price, page last updated 03/08/2026) carries no live price table. It routes the public to an SMS lookup ("RSP <dealer code>" to 92249 92249) against a published list of 42 dealer codes, or the IndianOil ONE app. The city list overlaps closely with PPAC's 35 state capitals, with minor naming differences (e.g. Jammu listed separately from Srinagar; Vijayawada/Visakhapatnam rather than Amaravati for Andhra Pradesh).
  • BPCL (bharatpetroleum.in, petro-prices page, site last updated 04/08/2026) likewise has no live table — only a station locator and downloadable PDFs titled "Price Build-up of Petrol/Diesel at Delhi at BPCL Retail Pump Outlets," Delhi-only, structurally identical to PPAC's own Table 8.4/8.5.
  • HPCL's direct price-page URL redirected to its generic homepage; no live table was reachable there either.

So PPAC's Table 8.14 — a snapshot dated 01.10.2025, not a live feed — remains the most complete multi-location source actually available in machine-readable form. The scenario figures in this piece should be read as "PPAC's last-published baseline, re-priced under a crude assumption," not as today's actual pump price at any of these 35 cities.

7. The link to petrochemicals · same crude, different tax regime

Naphtha and other petrochemical feedstocks come off the same crude barrel and the same refinery gate as petrol and diesel, but Table 8.16 draws a hard administrative line: petrol, diesel, ATF and crude oil sit outside GST under the flat-rate excise regime detailed above; virtually everything else — including the organic-chemical and polymer imports covered in this site's textile import-export strategy piece — is priced and taxed under GST (IGST plus, on imports, the BCD/AIDC/SWS stack detailed in the HSN Ch.27 dashboard). That is the structural reason India's petrochemical import bill is far more exposed to a crude/rupee swing than the pump price is: fuel pricing has a flat-rate excise shock absorber built in; ad-valorem GST-rated chemical imports do not.

What would fix this

  • PPAC should publish the monthly Indian basket crude price as a downloadable series within days of each month’s close, rather than in the half-yearly Ready Reckoner, so analysts are not left applying PPAC’s formula themselves across an eleven-month gap from the last official print.
  • PPAC should add a state-by-state price build-up for each of the 35 state capitals, showing statutory VAT rate, local levies and freight separately, so the effective incidence of state taxes no longer has to be backed out of the published retail price.
  • PPAC should publish the dealer price split into crude cost and refining-and-marketing margin for petrol and diesel, so the crude-linked layer of the litre is a published figure rather than a wedge inferred from a single quarterly baseline.
  • IOCL, BPCL and HPCL should each publish a daily, machine-readable table of retail selling prices for the cities they already list, so current pump prices can be checked without SMS look-ups, apps or Delhi-only PDFs.
  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should publish the cost difference OMCs absorb between unblended and E20 petrol at the same notified pump price, so the effect of ethanol blending on the litre’s economics is disclosed rather than modelled from outside.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
