Foreign companies put a record $94.5 billion of direct investment into India in 2025-26, according to the Reserve Bank of India. Net foreign direct investment, the number that matters for the balance of payments, was $7.65 billion. A year earlier it had been $0.35 billion, the lowest on record, on gross inflows of $81 billion. In 2019-20 and 2020-21, on smaller gross inflows, net FDI was about $43 billion a year.

Gross money has not stopped coming. What changed is how much leaves again: foreign investors selling out and taking their money home, and Indian companies investing abroad. This piece splits each year’s gross inflow into those parts, shows what drives the outflows, and asks what the low net figure does and does not mean. It complements this blog’s analyses of where FDI lands by state and which sectors it goes to, which use DPIIT’s gross equity data.

Updated 7 November 2026. The current year is running well ahead of 2025-26. RBI data for April–August 2026-27 put net FDI at about $13.8 billion ($13.79 billion), up from $9.6 billion in the same months a year earlier, on gross inflows of $54.93 billion against $45.55 billion (Angel One; NewsBytes, both citing RBI). Five months of 2026-27 have already produced almost twice the net FDI of the whole of 2025-26. Reports disagree on July (about $7.3 billion in some, $4.1 billion in another), so the monthly split is not used here.

Four numbers, not one

TermWhat it counts
Gross inflowNew equity, reinvested earnings and other capital that foreign direct investors put into Indian companies in the year
Repatriation and disinvestmentMoney foreign direct investors take back out by selling shares (to other investors, in an IPO, or back to the company) or winding down holdings
Direct investment to IndiaGross inflow minus repatriation
FDI by India (outward)Money Indian companies invest in their own foreign subsidiaries and acquisitions
Net FDIDirect investment to India minus FDI by India: the figure in the balance of payments that helps pay for the current-account deficit

DPIIT’s widely quoted FDI figures are gross equity inflows. The RBI’s balance-of-payments figures net out the money going the other way. Both are correct; they answer different questions.

Over the three years 2019-20 to 2021-22, gross inflows totalled $241 billion and net FDI $126 billion. Over 2023-24 to 2025-26, gross inflows were higher, $247 billion, but net FDI was $18 billion. Of that later $247 billion, $150 billion went back out to foreign investors and $79 billion went abroad with Indian companies.

Repatriation is the larger force. It rose from 25% of gross inflow in 2019-20 to 64% in 2024-25, and was still 57% in 2025-26. Indian companies’ investment abroad also climbed, from $13 billion to $33 billion a year, and in 2024-25 it alone absorbed 36% of gross inflow.

Who is taking money out

The RBI does not publish repatriation by sector or by type of investor, so the drivers have to be pieced together. Three stand out in the record.

Private-equity and venture-capital exits. Funds that bought into Indian companies in the 2010s and the 2021 boom have been selling. Bain and the Indian Venture and Alternate Capital Association put PE/VC exits at about $24 billion in 2024, and EY-IVCA counted $11.6 billion in the first half of 2025 alone. A January 2026 note by MUFG Research found that equity-market deal values, including IPOs, track gross FDI repatriation closely over time, and expected sales by existing shareholders in IPOs to stay high in 2026.

Foreign parents listing Indian subsidiaries. Hyundai Motor India’s IPO in October 2024, about $3.3 billion and the largest in Indian history at the time, was entirely an offer for sale by its Korean parent: none of the money went to the Indian company. LG Electronics India’s Rs 11,607 crore IPO in October 2025, about $1.3 billion, was likewise all sold by the parent. Each counts as a foreign direct investor taking money out, even though the factories, jobs and control stay in India.

A strong stock market. High valuations make India a good place to sell. The RBI has described the rise in repatriation as a sign of a mature market in which foreign investors can enter and exit easily, and pointed to the hot IPO market of 2024-25, including Hyundai and Swiggy, as the occasion for long-term investors to realise gains.

Indian companies going abroad

Outward FDI is the other half of the story. In April–September 2025 it was $16.32 billion, up from $12.17 billion a year earlier, and the main destinations were Singapore, Mauritius, the United Arab Emirates and the United States, according to RBI data. The sectors were mainly financial, insurance and business services, manufacturing, and agriculture and mining. UNCTAD’s World Investment Report 2026, which uses a different method, put India’s outward FDI for calendar 2025 at $35.66 billion, up 47%. Singapore and Mauritius are holding-company locations, so the final destination of much of this money is not visible in the data.

What the low net figure does and does not mean

It does not mean foreign companies have stopped investing in India: gross inflows are at a record, and much repatriation is a sale from one investor to another, often in an IPO, that leaves the company and its capital in place. Nor is outward investment a loss in itself; it reflects Indian firms buying assets and markets abroad.

It does mean that FDI now does much less to finance India’s current-account deficit than it did. At $43–44 billion a year before 2022, net FDI was a stable, long-term source of dollars. At $0.35–7.7 billion it is not, which leaves the deficit to be paid for by portfolio flows, which can reverse quickly, and by borrowing. And because a record-high gross number is the one most often quoted, it gives a misleading picture of how much new foreign capital is staying in the country.

What would fix this

  • The RBI should publish repatriation each quarter by route (IPO offer for sale, secondary sale, buyback, liquidation) and by broad investor type (PE/VC fund, foreign parent company, other), so that exits by funds can be told apart from parents reducing their stakes.
  • DPIIT’s FDI factsheet should show RBI’s repatriation and net FDI figures next to its gross equity numbers, with the difference explained.
  • Outward FDI data should report the ultimate destination of investment routed through Singapore, Mauritius and similar holding locations, as far as the reporting forms already allow.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

The RBI’s own pages could not be opened directly; figures for 2023-24 to 2025-26 come from news reports of its bulletins and from figures tabled in Parliament, and they are revised in later releases, so the most recent years may change. The 2023-24 outward figure here is a residual, not a published number. No breakdown of repatriation by sector, route or investor type was found, so the drivers above are drawn from IPO and private-equity data that overlap with, but are not the same as, the RBI’s repatriation figures. The LG Electronics dollar value uses an approximate exchange rate of Rs 88.8 to the dollar.
