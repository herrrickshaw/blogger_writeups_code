Where a record harvest, 918 lakh tonnes of storage and the world's oldest compounding puzzle meet

Updated 4 November 2026. Added the board chart, the Ambalappuzha version of the legend and a section placing world grain volumes on the board. Corrected the years in which the harvest first reached squares 51, 52 and 53 (1958-59, 1983-84 and 2019-20, not the 1960s, 1990-91 and 2020-21), and the FCI silo programme (111 lakh tonnes planned in 2022, of which about 19.5 lakh tonnes had been built by mid-2025).

The legend

The story is as Indian as chess itself. A sage presents a king with the game of chaturanga. Delighted, the king offers any reward. The sage asks only for grain: one on the first square of the board, two on the second, four on the third, doubling on each of the 64 squares. The king laughs at the modesty of the request and orders a sack brought. Long before the board is full, the royal granaries are empty.

Kerala tells its own version at the Ambalappuzha Sree Krishna Swamy Temple in Alappuzha. There the sage is Krishna in disguise, the king of the region loses a game of chess to him, and the grain is rice. When the king cannot pay, Krishna lets him settle the debt in instalments: rice served as paal payasam, sweet milk porridge, to pilgrims at the temple every day until the debt is cleared. The temple still serves it. On the board below, a rice grain is lighter than wheat, so the debt in rice is about 461 billion tonnes rather than 1.2 trillion — and still unpayable.

The arithmetic behind the legend turns out to be a surprisingly good ruler for measuring India's real grain economy.

The arithmetic of 64 doublings

Square n holds 2^(n−1) grains, and the whole board holds 2^64 − 1 grains: 18,446,744,073,709,551,615 grains, about 18.4 quintillion.

A wheat grain weighs roughly 65 milligrams; a rice grain about 25 mg.

Grain Weight per grain Whole board In lakh metric tonnes
Wheat ~65 mg ~1.2 trillion t ~12 million lakh t
Rice ~25 mg ~461 billion t ~4.6 million lakh t

A figure of "120 million lakh tonnes" is often quoted, but that is ten times the wheat answer — it would need each grain to weigh about 0.65 g, closer to a kidney bean. The correct wheat figure is about 12 million lakh tonnes (1.2 trillion tonnes): still more than 3,000 years of India's record harvest.

After half the board the king owes a few hundred tonnes — a single warehouse. The last square alone holds as much as all 63 before it combined.

Where India stands on the board

India benchmark Size Square reached
Central pool buffer norm (April quarter) 210.40 lakh t 49

Foodgrain production 2025-26 (record) 376.56 million t 53

India's entire public storage network sits on square 51. The full board is about 13,000 times that capacity.

A record harvest only reaches square 53. The full board equals roughly 3,200 years of India's 2025-26 harvest, and square 54 alone holds more grain than India grows in a year.

Storage sits two doublings behind the harvest. Stocks are nearly three times the buffer norm, yet public storage covers only about a quarter of annual output.

Seventy-five years in four squares

India's 1950-51 harvest of 51 million tonnes had already covered squares 1 to 49. Every gain since — the Green Revolution, the wheat and rice records, the 2025-26 high of 377 million tonnes — fits inside squares 50 to 53. The harvest first reached square 51 in 1958-59 (77 million tonnes), square 52 in 1983-84 (152 million tonnes) and square 53 in 2019-20 (298 million tonnes). Each new square needs a harvest twice the size of the threshold before it: the gaps between them were 25 years and then 36.

The world's whole cereal harvest sits on square 56, three squares beyond India's. Three squares is three doublings: the world grows about 8 times what India does, which is roughly what India's one-eighth share of world output implies. Even so, the full board would take about 400 years of the entire world's harvest to fill.

India's harvest and the world's grain trade share a square. All the wheat, maize, rice and other cereals that cross a border in a year, about 509 million tonnes, land on square 53, the same square as India's harvest. India grows the equivalent of about 74% of the world's annual cereal trade, which is why its decisions to ban or release rice and wheat exports move world prices.

China is one square ahead. Its 2025 grain output of 714.88 million tonnes, a record, reached square 54, as do the world's cereal stocks at the end of 2026/27, forecast at about 958 million tonnes. The year after a record 2025 harvest, FAO expects world production to dip about 2%, mostly on lower wheat crops; on this board, that is invisible.

These totals are not quite like for like. FAO counts cereals only, with rice in milled terms. India's foodgrain figure includes pulses and rice as milled output. China's "grain" also includes soybeans and tubers. None of the differences is large enough to move a benchmark by a square.

What the board teaches about storage policy

  • The cooperative programme to add about 700 lakh tonnes of storage would take public capacity to roughly 1,618 lakh tonnes — a historic expansion, and still only a move from square 51 to 52.
  • FCI's steel-silo programme (about 111 lakh tonnes at 249 locations, announced in 2022, with about 19.5 lakh tonnes built by mid-2025) modernises storage on square 51 rather than moving to a new square.
  • Storing a full year's harvest would mean reaching square 53 — roughly four times today's capacity.

Production has compounded for decades; storage grows in additive steps. On a doubling board, each new square is twice the size of everything behind it.

Method notes

Grain counts are exact (checked in code against 2^64 − 1). Grain weights are averages; a 10% heavier grain moves no benchmark by a full square. 1 lakh metric tonnes = 100,000 tonnes. "Square reached" is where the cumulative pile first exceeds the benchmark, using the 65 mg wheat grain; with a 25 mg rice grain, storage and the harvest would each reach one square later. 2025-26 production is the Third Advance Estimate; historical figures are from the RBI Handbook of Statistics.
