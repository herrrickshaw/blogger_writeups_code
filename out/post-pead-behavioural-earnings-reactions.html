Top line

  • 1.7% to 11.7% of US announcement-day returns explained by analyst surprises, 1984–90 against 2016–19 (Martineau); after 2005 the drift is not significant for large stocks, and after 2015 not for microcaps.
  • +7.3% against none is the gap between top and bottom earnings-surprise quartiles over 63 days on the NSE in 2008–14 (Krishnan and Rangan), against no drift found among 100 large NSE stocks in 2014–18 (Gupta and Dhusia).
  • 38 of 137 earnings reactions closed beyond the options-implied move on eight October 2026 trading days (EarningsWatcher pages, secondary; a small, selected sample).

What post-earnings drift is and what the original studies found

Post-earnings-announcement drift (PEAD) is the tendency of a share price to keep moving in the direction of an earnings surprise for weeks after the announcement. This blog’s own 19-market test of it is in Earnings drift and Piotroski F-score: both work in thinly traded stocks; this piece does not repeat those figures and covers the published research record, the behavioural explanations, the Indian studies and recent reaction patterns.

The earliest studies are not open here, so their numbers come from Fink’s 2020 literature review (University of Graz working paper, secondary). Fink reports that Ball and Brown (1968) split stocks into two groups and found a negligible drift, and that Bernard and Thomas (1989) found about 2% over 60 trading days for the best and worst surprise groups each, roughly 4% a quarter or 18% annualised. Fink also writes that, while several factors are supported, ‘an all-encompassing explanation remains out of sight’.

What to do: A claim that investors under-react should name the date, coverage or liquidity condition under which it predicts more drift, since each is testable.

What it means: The Indian record is mixed for a reason that matches the US dispute, since the studies that find a gap of 6.5% to 10% use broad samples of listed firms and the one that finds none uses only 100 large, liquid stocks, and every sample ended in 2018 or earlier.

What to do: NSE or SEBI-backed researchers should rerun the drift test on 2019–2026 results with exchange time-stamps, reported separately for large, mid and small stocks.

What to do: Treat a run of negative earnings surprises in a payer as a reason to re-check its payout cover, and use positive surprises only to time additions, not to choose what to own.

What an investor can and cannot conclude

  • The best-documented drift sits in small, thinly covered, illiquid stocks, which are the stocks where trading costs and limited liquidity are largest, so a paper gap is not a tradable return.
  • For large US stocks the primary evidence opened here finds no significant drift after 2005, though two 2025 papers dispute that for samples that include microcaps.
  • India has no study opened here that covers 2019–2026, so how far the 2008–14 NSE gap carries to today’s market is not known.
  • For an income investor, the documented damage from drift is on the downside: negative surprises and dividend omissions were followed by falls, while the return side is thin in large stocks.
  • A single day of reactions, or a handful of well-known names, is not a trend; this piece is research, not investment advice.

What would fix this

  1. NSE and SEBI: publish a time-stamped dataset of results filings (date, hour, consolidated or standalone) so that Indian drift studies stop relying on commercial databases and guessed announcement dates.
  2. NSE research initiative: commission a rerun of the 2008–14 drift tests on 2019–2026 results, with large, mid and small stocks reported separately.
  3. Academic authors: report every drift result with and without microcaps, and name the surprise measure, since both choices move the conclusion.
  4. Earnings-reaction trackers: publish the full list of names with the reported surprise, so that claims about selling on beats can be tested on a stated, complete sample.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Method and definitions
  • Martineau’s I/B/E/S sample runs 1984–2019 (312,462 announcements) and his Compustat sample 1973–2019 (593,654); microcaps are stocks below the NYSE 20th percentile of market value. Drift is the buy-and-hold abnormal return from day 2 to day 60.
  • The middle values of the R-squared series (2.8, 3.2, 6.0, 11.5, 11.7) were read from Table 3 Panel A of the paper’s text extraction; the first and last values are printed in the paper’s prose and were checked against it.
  • The October 2026 count is built by ew_fetch.py (downloads one page per weekday, one request a second) and the parser in build.py and ew_oct2026.json. Each parsed day is asserted equal to the page’s own measured, beyond-implied and closed-up totals. A page’s ‘beyond’ uses the first-session close, not the intraday peak, and the implied move is the options straddle price frozen before the report.
  • Indian windows differ across studies: 63 days (Krishnan and Rangan), three months (the FII paper and Sen), 30 and 60 days (Gupta and Dhusia), so the bars in the India chart are indicative.
  • Benchmarks in the quantitative section: if the implied move equals the expected absolute move, the chance that a normal variable exceeds its own mean absolute value is 42.5% and a Laplace variable 36.8%. The day-clustered standard error is the ratio-estimator form, the square root of the sum over days of (beyond minus share times measured) squared, divided by the total names; it assumes days are independent.
  • Percentages are as printed in the sources; differences between printed values are computed here with the assert noted in build.py.
What this piece could not establish
  • The Ball and Brown (1968) and Bernard and Thomas (1989) papers were not opened, so their figures are Fink’s summary, and Fink says Ball and Brown found a negligible drift.
  • The Choi and Kim conservatism model (cis.upenn.edu): the host was not reachable from this environment, so the conservatism explanation is not described.
  • The Fink, Palan and Theissen experiment: the Graz file opened is Fink’s review, not the experiment, so no experimental figure is given.
  • Wuyang Zhao’s 2022 paper and the competing-announcements working paper (CFR 20-10): neither was found; no number from them is used. The Hirshleifer, Lim and Teoh crowded-day result is Fink’s summary only.
  • Anchoring: only a review’s summary of George, Hwang and Li (2015) was found, with no Indian evidence. Retail investors: the evidence in Fink is described as mixed and no Indian retail study was found.
  • The Rangan paper in the IIMB repository (ICFMCG 2016) was not located. The NSE file 1516_BS3.pdf is a different paper (Brochet, Lee and Srinivasan) marked as a very preliminary draft not to be cited without the authors’ consent, so none of its figures are used. The NSE file 4.pdf is a summary of the Krishnan and Rangan insider-trading paper, not a paper on FIIs; the FII paper is NSE file 1415_BS5.
  • Sehgal and Bijoy: the full text was not opened, so no coefficient or return is given. Gupta and Dhusia’s drift tables (counts only) were not turned into a return.
  • The investinglive.com count of 18 names (6 beyond implied downward, 2 beats to the upside) and the reports on Apple and Palantir were not located and are not used. The Disney move differs between two secondary sources (7.2% in the brief, 7.4% on TIKR).
  • Whether beats are sold: the EarningsWatcher pages carry no surprise label, so the share of beats with falling prices could not be computed. October pages from 1 to 19 and 26 October returned no page (404), so the count covers 8 of 22 weekdays.
  • An India-specific 2026 earnings-reaction count: none was found.
  • The dividend section rests on abstract or search excerpts only: Michaely, Thaler and Womack (1995), Benartzi, Michaely and Thaler (1997), Grullon et al. (2005), Stacescu (2006), a Review of Accounting Studies paper on dividend payers and a 2024 dividend-premium paper. None was opened here, so sample periods, windows and the untabulated drift result are unchecked.
  • No study was found that tests drift separately for dividend growers against flat payers, that uses accruals or earnings quality to predict dividend cuts, or that covers Indian dividend payers.
  • The 4% yield is an illustration, not a data point, and the 29.2% annualised NSE gap is a naive upper bound.
