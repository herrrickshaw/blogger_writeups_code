Reading the tree: where the dependence actually concentrates

Split the 22 products above by feedstock and a pattern falls out that the chart alone doesn't spell out in words. The ethylene and propylene chains — the highest-volume, most commodity-like products (polyethylene, PVC, polypropylene) — carry the lowest import dependence on this list, roughly 20–40%, because India already runs large-scale crackers for them at Reliance, ONGC Petro-additions and others. The dependence problem sits almost entirely one layer downstream, in the specialty and engineering products built from the same feedstocks: styrene monomer and acrylonitrile at essentially 100% import dependence, bisphenol-A/polycarbonate at 90–100%, and the two isocyanates that make polyurethane foam and coatings — MDI and TDI — both at 85–90%. None of these four gaps needs a new crude source; each needs one more processing step India mostly doesn't run at scale, converting a feedstock the country already produces or imports cheaply into the finished chemical it re-imports at a markup.

The gas-based branch — methanol, at roughly 90% import dependence and ₹7,524 crore a year — is the one entry on this list with a weak link to petrol-range refining; it's included because methanol competes for the same downstream formaldehyde and acetic-acid demand pools as several petrol-derived products, not because it shares a feedstock with them. Everything else on the tree traces back to just two refinery streams: naphtha, cracked into the ethylene/propylene/C4 chain, and reformate aromatics, split into benzene, toluene and the xylenes. That concentration is the practical takeaway — a substitution policy aimed at these 22 products is really a policy aimed at two refinery outputs and roughly seven downstream conversion units, not twenty-two separate industries.

Sources and caveats. Values are approximate annual import figures compiled from the Ministry of Commerce's Statistics-at-a-Glance releases, DGCI&S/TradeStat trade data, and industry reporting for 2023–25 — a multi-year window chosen because single-year HSN-level chemical trade data is noisy, so the figures here should be read as an order-of-magnitude map of where the dependence sits, not a single fiscal year's exact import bill. Each product's ITC-HS 8-digit code is given so a reader can pull the live, current-year tonnage and value directly from Tradestat or Niryat rather than relying on this article's snapshot. Import-dependence bands (~100%, 85–90%, high, 20–40%, partial) are drawn from the same sources and industry commentary rather than a single official dependence-ratio release, since India does not publish one composite figure for most of these lines; where a precise percentage could not be confirmed, the article uses the qualitative band ("high," "partial") rather than inventing a number.
