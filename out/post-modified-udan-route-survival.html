This blog’s piece on jet-fuel pipelines noted that much of the new airport capacity India has added came through UDAN, the regional connectivity scheme, and that most of those airports still get their fuel by tanker truck. UDAN turned ten on 21 October. In March the Cabinet approved its successor, Modified UDAN, with Rs 28,840 crore for 2026-27 to 2035-36, and it was launched at Jodhpur on 4 July. The largest change for airlines is that a route’s subsidy now lasts up to five years instead of three.

That change is a response to the scheme’s main weakness: routes that close. This piece sets out what the new money buys, what the scheme’s own counts show about route survival, and whether a longer, tapering subsidy addresses the way routes have actually failed.

More than half the money goes to building and running airports and helipads rather than to flights. The airline subsidy, Rs 10,043 crore over ten years, is about Rs 1,004 crore a year. UDAN paid out Rs 5,091 crore in the roughly 9.5 years from its first flight in April 2017 to October 2026, about Rs 536 crore a year, so the new scheme roughly doubles the yearly rate. It will also come from the budget rather than from the levy on flights on busy routes that funded the Centre’s share of the original subsidy, as reported at launch. An airport now counts as unserved or underserved with up to 14 flights a week, up from 7, which widens the pool of routes that qualify.

How routes have failed

The Comptroller and Auditor General’s audit of the first three bidding rounds is the most complete record. Of 774 routes awarded, 403 (52%) never started. Of the 371 that did, 112 had completed their three years of subsidy by March 2023, and only 54 of those were still flying afterwards. That is 7% of the routes awarded. Heliport routes did worse: 34 of 83 (41%) started.

The Rajya Sabha reply of July 2026 gives the latest position across all rounds: of 679 routes that started, 348 were flying and 331 had closed. The useful detail is when they closed. 165 closed before their three years of subsidy were over, and the other 166 at or after the end of it. The reply lists low passenger demand, airlines’ marketing, public transport to the airport and the airport’s distance from the towns it serves as reasons.

What a five-year subsidy changes

The Civil Aviation Minister described the new support as tapered: the full viability gap in years one and two, then 75%, 50% and 25%. Over a route’s life that is 3.5 years of full support against 3 before, about 17% more. But it is spread more thinly. In year three a route now gets three-quarters of its gap covered, where it used to get all of it, and the drop from full support starts a year earlier.

Set against how routes have actually closed, that helps one group more than another. Routes that flew their three years and then stopped, 166 of the 331 closures, get a gentler end to support, which is what the change is designed for. The CAG’s figures suggest this matters: of 112 routes that completed three years, 58 closed after the subsidy stopped. But the 165 routes that closed while still fully subsidised were not short of time on support. A longer taper does not help a route that cannot fill its seats when every seat is subsidised, and those routes would get less support in year three under the new terms, not more.

The cost per passenger shows the scale of what is being bought. Over ten years UDAN paid about Rs 2,960 of subsidy per passenger, about Rs 1.38 lakh per flight, with an average of about 47 passengers a flight. Those are averages across aircraft from helicopters to 70-seaters and over routes that are flying and routes that closed, so they show the order of magnitude rather than the cost on any route.

Where jet fuel comes in

Fuel is typically a regional airline’s largest single cost, and at most UDAN airports it is trucked in. To bring it down on UDAN routes, states were asked to cut value-added tax on jet fuel to 1% or less for ten years; Maharashtra, for example, set its 1% rate from 1 April 2017 for ten years, and Uttar Pradesh set zero for ten years in its 2017 aviation policy. Those ten-year concessions start running out in 2027, the first full year of Modified UDAN. If they lapse, a route’s viability gap widens just as its subsidy starts to taper. The new pipelines in the earlier piece serve large airports; for the 100 new airports, fuel will again come by road unless the scheme plans for it.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Civil Aviation should publish a route-level register: award date, start date, end date, aircraft, seats offered, load factor and VGF paid each year, so that survival can be measured by round, region and aircraft type rather than reconstructed from parliamentary answers.
  • Routes that close while still fully subsidised should trigger a review of the bid before the airport or the route is offered again, since a longer taper does not address them.
  • States should extend the jet-fuel VAT concession for UDAN routes to match the five-year support, and the Ministry should report which states have done so.
  • For each of the 100 new airports, the plan should say how fuel will be supplied and at what cost per kilolitre, alongside the runway and terminal.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

The Cabinet release, the PIB notes and the Rajya Sabha reply could not be opened directly; figures come from their text as indexed by search engines and from news and exam-preparation summaries. A Civil Aviation Ministry factsheet reported by IANS on 20 October gave 1.49 crore passengers and 625 routes, figures that match the scheme’s April 2025 totals; this piece uses the October 2026 PIB figures (1.72 crore and 687). The route counts differ slightly between sources (679 in July, 687 in October). How many of the 348 flying routes are still within their subsidy period, and how much of the Rs 5,091 crore went to routes that later closed, is not published. The Rs 400 crore not itemised in the outlay table is the difference between the total and the four components the release lists.
