Cheap Yen, Rupee Roads: Where the Carry Trade Meets Project Bonds

Borrow at 1% in Tokyo, build a road that earns in rupees — the arithmetic looks irresistible and is mostly an illusion: hedging eats roughly the whole rate differential. What actually survives, on the deals this blog has already verified, is a credit-spread and investor-base advantage captured by intermediaries — and in 2025-26 India finally built the domestic leg that lets rupee project bonds reach pension money: NaBFID’s credit-enhancement facility, backed by a $500 million multilateral risk line.

The carry, priced honestly

The differential is real. The free lunch is not.

A project bond is debt sold against one project’s cash flows — an SPV’s toll receipts, a solar park’s PPA payments — rather than a corporate balance sheet. The carry trade is funding in a low-rate currency to deploy at higher rates. On the BIS policy-rate series this blog charted in the EuroYen piece, the gap is wide: RBI at 5.25% against the Bank of Japan at 1.00%. Covered interest parity is the discipline this subject usually dodges. Hedging rupee–yen forward costs roughly the rate differential, so a 2% yen coupon hedges into approximately a domestic-cost instrument. The naive carry — borrow at 2%, lend at 9% — exists only unhedged, and unhedged is not a strategy but an open currency position; 2013’s taper tantrum is what it looks like when it closes. What survives hedging — and what put IREDA’s all-in under 7% — is that Japanese lenders price Indian PSU credit tighter than the home market does, plus tenor availability and hedge-market basis. Choose the currency for the investor base and the hedge economics; treat any unhedged FX gain as luck.

Why cheap yen cannot reach a project directly

An SPV that earns rupees must not owe yen.

Strap a yen bond onto a toll-road SPV and you have bolted a currency mismatch onto an entity with no treasury desk, no fine-priced swap access, and revenue set in rupees by NHAI’s toll schedule. (The partial exceptions — airports and ports with dollar-linked revenue — prove the rule.) So the market that actually exists routes the carry through intermediaries, in three channels:

  • The PSU-NBFC transformer. REC, PFC and IREDA borrow offshore, hedge, and on-lend rupees to projects. The intermediary manages the FX; the project sees a rupee loan whose price embeds whatever spread advantage the offshore leg captured. This is how the EuroYen playbook’s benefit lands on an actual solar park.
  • Bank construction, bond take-out. Banks hold construction risk — the phase with no cash flows, which bond investors rightly refuse. Once the asset is operating, bonds refinance the bank debt: the bond buyer gets an observable annuity, the bank gets its capital back for the next project.
  • The InvIT aggregator. Operating assets pooled into a trust that issues at scale with diversification — the listed form of the same take-out.

2025-26: the domestic leg finally got plumbing

The constraint was never demand. It was the A-to-AA gap.

India’s deepest debt pools — insurance and pension money — are largely confined to AA/AAA paper. A single-project SPV rates around A. That one-notch gap, not investor appetite, is what kept the project-bond market thin. The Budget 2025-26 answered it directly: a Partial Credit Enhancement facility at NaBFID — a first-loss guarantee engineered to lift infrastructure bonds from A to AA, the exact threshold that unlocks the institutional pool.

The multilateral line is the carry trade in institutional clothing. ADB and the World Bank fund in hard currency at sovereign-adjacent cost and deploy that balance-sheet strength as first-loss capacity behind rupee bonds. The currency risk sits with institutions built to hold it; the project’s debt is domestic; the pension fund’s asset is AA. Nobody in the chain wears a mismatch they cannot manage — which is exactly what the naive version of the carry trade gets wrong.

The refined claim: the carry trade helps finance Indian projects not by making any project’s debt cheaper directly, but by widening and cheapening the funding of the intermediaries that lend to projects — at spreads that survive hedging — and, in its multilateral form, by putting hard-currency risk capacity behind the credit enhancement that lets rupee project bonds reach the deepest domestic money. The project gets rupees either way. That is not a limitation of the design; it is the design.

What this article does not establish. Hedge costs to the basis point — forward-implied costs move daily, and “roughly the rate differential” is a characterisation, not a quote. Whether NaBFID’s facility will actually be taken up — the 2015 precedent failed on pricing, and H2 FY2025-26 issuance is the live test. The spreads REC, PFC and IREDA charge projects over their own funding cost — commercial, not decomposed here. And the census of SPV-level foreign-currency bonds — asserted structurally rare, with airport/port exceptions noted but not counted.
