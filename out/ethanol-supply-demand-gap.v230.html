India's ethanol industry was offered 1,776 crore litres against a need of about 1,050 in 2025-26, but that surplus covers only about five more points of petrol blending. E30, isobutanol in diesel and jet fuel from ethanol would together need more than twice what today's distilleries can deliver.

On paper India has too much ethanol. Oil marketing companies (OMCs) received offers of 1,776 crore litres for the 2025-26 ethanol supply year and allocated 1,048. About 576 crore litres of offered capacity has no buyer, even counting a proposed Q4 top-up. That surplus is real, but it is small next to what comes next. Petrol demand is growing about 6% a year, the government has said it is moving towards 30% blending, diesel blending with isobutanol is in trials, and jet fuel from ethanol has notified targets. This piece sets out current availability from PIB and ChiniMandi, then forecasts demand with the method NITI Aayog used for its 2021 E20 roadmap.

Cane is crushed for only four to five months a year, so a mill's distillery sits idle most of the year. Conversion lets it run in the off-season on grain. That raises how many days existing capacity works, but it does not add feedstock, and the extra litres come from maize, the most expensive feedstock. The priority shift also cost dedicated grain plants: Mongabay India reported that Bihar's procurement quota was cut by about half, and some plants shut temporarily in December 2025.

2026-27 depends on sugar

ChiniMandi reports that sugar output next season may fall below 300 lakh tonnes against consumption of about 290 lakh tonnes. Ex-mill sugar is around ₹42–46 a kilo, at or above many mills' cost, so diverting cane juice and B-heavy molasses to ethanol pays less. The government has made domestic sugar supply its priority, with stock limits and up to 10 lakh tonnes of duty-free raw imports. Grain offers alone (1,305 crore litres) still exceed the full E20 need, so the more likely result is costlier ethanol, not a shortage.

How the forecast works

NITI Aayog's 2021 roadmap estimated ethanol demand as petrol sales (million tonnes) × 141.1 crore litres per million tonnes × blend share, under three electric-vehicle scenarios. The forecast here keeps that method and adds four things:

  • Higher blends carry less energy per litre, so the same distance needs slightly more fuel: about 1.9% more volume at E30 and 3.9% at E40.
  • Isobutanol at up to 10% of diesel.
  • Jet fuel from ethanol (alcohol-to-jet, ATJ) at the notified 1%, 2% and 5% of international aviation fuel.
  • A supply check at 85% of installed capacity.

NITI's petrol growth assumption of 3–4% a year proved too low. The forecast therefore grows underlying petrol demand at 6.5% a year to 2030 (FY26 actual: +6.45%) and subtracts electric vehicles separately: 6% of petrol energy by 2029-30, 15% by 2034-35 and 28% by 2039-40 in the central case.

In the central case, India's demand passes Brazil's entire output by 2029-30. By 2034-35 it roughly equals everything the US produces, and by 2039-40 it exceeds total US capacity. Both countries built that scale over decades on large, dedicated corn and cane surpluses, which is the feedstock question in the next sections. Brazil's turn to corn, now over a quarter of its output, is the same shift India has made with maize.

E100 is now legal: demand the forecast does not yet count

The forecast above treats petrol blending as a pool average. A second route now exists alongside it. On 27 April 2026 the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways published draft amendments to the Central Motor Vehicles Rules (G.S.R. 313(E)). They add E85, E100 and B100 as fuel categories and give E100 vehicles their own compliance annexure. On 13 June 2026 Nitin Gadkari said he had signed the final rules giving 100% ethanol legal standing as a motor fuel. On 1 October he said the next focus is flex-fuel engines that can run entirely on ethanol. Maruti Suzuki's WagonR flex-fuel and several Hero MotoCorp motorcycles have been shown, and more launches are expected.

E100 carries about 30% less energy per litre than E20, so moving 1% of petrol energy to flex-fuel vehicles on E100 adds about 90–100 crore litres a year on top of the blending path. A flex-fuel fleet doing 5% of petrol kilometres by 2034-35 would add about 500 crore litres, more than all of India's potable and industrial alcohol use today. E100 removes the vehicle-compatibility limit that caps pool blending. It does nothing for the feedstock limit set out below, and it makes that limit bind sooner.

Diesel is the swing demand; jet fuel is small

Diesel use is about 2.2 times petrol, so even a 5% isobutanol blend is as large as the step from E20 to E30. At 10% in 2034-35, India would need about 11.9 million tonnes of isobutanol. How much ethanol that takes depends on the production route:

Isobutanol routeEthanol, 2034-35 (crore litres)Note
Ethanol + methanol (Guerbet)1,180Also needs about 12.9 million tonnes of methanol a year
Fermentation (ethanol-equivalent feedstock)1,981Same sugar or grain, fermented to isobutanol directly
Ethanol-only upgrading2,511Pre-commercial; mostly yields n-butanol

The commercial path now points to the fermentation route. On 16 September 2026 Praj Industries and Gevo signed a Development & Commercialization Agreement for bio-isobutanol in India. Praj gets exclusive rights to deploy Gevo's technology in India, with diesel blending as the main application. Praj is also building India's first commercial bio-isobutanol demonstration plant for an oil marketing company, on Gevo's licensed technology. Gevo ferments sugar or starch directly to isobutanol rather than upgrading ethanol, so the 1,981 crore-litre fermentation row is the closest guide to the feedstock this would draw on. It is grain and cane that would otherwise make ethanol.

Jet fuel from ethanol takes about 2.4 kg of ethanol per kg of fuel. Even so, the notified 5% of international aviation fuel by 2030 needs only about 77 crore litres. The limits for SAF are plant capital and price, not ethanol supply.

Feedstock is the ceiling: rice, maize and sugarcane

To turn litres into crops, the calculation holds the 2025-26 allocation mix constant: maize 45.7%, FCI rice 22.3%, damaged grain 4.5%, sugarcane juice 15.8%, B-heavy molasses 10.5% and C-heavy molasses 1.2%. It then applies NITI's yields per tonne (maize 380 litres, FCI rice 450, damaged grain 400, and 70 litres per tonne of cane crushed for juice) to the fuel-ethanol demand in the central case. Sugar diversion uses this year's observed ratio, 289 crore litres for 34 lakh tonnes of sugar, which is about 850 litres per tonne. Production baselines are the Agriculture Ministry's third advance estimates for 2025-26.

Maize breaks first. At today's mix, it would take almost three-quarters of the 2025-26 maize crop by 2029-30, and more than the whole crop by 2034-35. Maize also feeds the poultry and starch industries, and it is already the dearest ethanol litre. Sugar is the next limit: 20 million tonnes of diversion in 2034-35 is about two-thirds of national sugar output, with consumption near 29 million tonnes. Rice has the most headroom on paper, but FCI's surplus depends on procurement policy, not on harvest size. The mix therefore cannot stay as it is. Each step beyond E27 needs either a new crop or higher yields.

A fourth crop: BPCL's sorghum research

One candidate is sweet sorghum. In February 2025, at India Energy Week, BPCL signed an MoU with the National Sugar Institute, Kanpur, and committed ₹5 crore of R&D. The work covers sorghum yields and farming practice, juice extraction and fermentation, and the use of leftover biomass for compressed biogas, through field trials and commercial-viability studies. Sweet sorghum grows faster than cane, and its stalk juice can be fermented in the same equipment as cane juice. That would let a cane distillery run in the months it now sits idle. Coverage of the MoU put yields at 4,000–5,000 litres of ethanol a hectare, but that is a temperate-climate or whole-plant figure (juice plus bagasse converted as second-generation ethanol). Indian trials of the juice route come in much lower. ICRISAT's multi-location trials computed 925–1,440 litres a hectare. Released cultivars give about 1,200–1,300, with experimental hybrids up to about 3,300. The ICRISAT and Vasantdada Sugar Institute pilot put the crop at about 2,800 litres a hectare a year over two crops (40 litres per tonne of stalk). A distillery trial in Andhra Pradesh recovered only about 16 litres per tonne of stalk. On the Indian evidence, one million hectares would add about 100–150 crore litres per crop, or about 280 crore litres a year with two crops. That is about an eighth of the 2029-30 petrol-blending need, not a fifth. Supplying the 2034-35 maize volume (about 2,860 crore litres) would take roughly 200 lakh hectares of single-crop sorghum, about the same land as maize, or about 100 lakh hectares cropped twice a year. Sweet sorghum's real advantages are timing and water, not yield. It is a four-month crop that fits the idle months of a cane distillery, and it needs about 8,000 cubic metres of water a hectare over two crops against about 36,000 for one cane crop. No BPCL–NSI trial results had been published as of October 2026.

A second channel for this kind of work opened in August 2026. On 19 August the Petroleum Secretary and the heads of the public-sector oil companies launched MC²+ Ignite at IIT Madras. It is an accelerator run by the MoPNG-backed MC² Foundation, chaired by the Petroleum Secretary, with partner institutions including IIT Madras, IIT Kanpur, IIT Kharagpur, C-CAMP and Venture Center, Pune. It takes technology problem statements from the oil companies to start-ups, offering up to ₹2 crore in funding, mentoring, and visibility for PSU procurement when a product is ready. The launch material does not single out ethanol. The feedstock and conversion problems above (sorghum agronomy, second-generation ethanol, isobutanol and ATJ catalysts) are the kind of problem statements it was set up to take.

A crop mix that could meet the demand, with SAF on domestic flights

To test what supply mix could meet these volumes, the model fills demand from the sources that need the least new land first, each within a cap. In order: molasses, second-generation (2G) ethanol from straw and bagasse, FCI and broken rice, cane juice (about 5,950 litres a hectare at UPAg yields), sweet sorghum (2,800 litres a hectare a year over two crops), then maize from trend growth after feed and food use. Whatever is left is a gap that needs extra maize land or imports. Isobutanol here uses the fermentation route that the Praj–Gevo agreement points to. Each demand case runs with the SAF mandate on international flights only, and on all flights. Extending it to domestic flights multiplies SAF ethanol by 2.5.

The Panipat build-up recovers capital at 10% over 20 years. Straw costs ₹1,900 a tonne at the gate, which is what IOCL pays straw-baling contractors, and yields about 150 litres a tonne. Enzymes add ₹7–15 a litre, other running costs ₹10–15, and maintenance 3% of capex a year. That lands on ICCT's independent ₹80–95. Two things follow:

  • Running rate matters more than straw price. Falling from 90% to 62% use adds about ₹20 a litre, against about ₹7 for straw at ₹3,000 a tonne.
  • 2G needs a subsidy. At a ₹15–30 premium over 1G, the 800 crore litres of 2G in the stretch mix would cost ₹12,000–24,000 crore a year.

Chempolis's bamboo plant carries more than twice Panipat's capex per litre, so it works only as a multi-product biorefinery. Clariant's closure in Romania is the cautionary case: the plant failed on running rate, not chemistry.

Fertiliser and natural gas: what the extra crops would need

The extra cane, maize and sorghum need fertiliser, and India makes its urea from natural gas. This estimate covers only the land added beyond 2025-26 ethanol crops (maize 33 lakh hectares, cane juice 2.8 lakh hectares). It uses ICAR's recommended doses: maize 120:60:40, sugarcane 250:90:120, and sweet sorghum 80:40:40 per crop over two crops a year, in kilograms of N, P2O5 and K2O per hectare. Phosphate comes as DAP, the rest of the nitrogen as urea, and potash as MOP. Urea made in India takes 22.8–30 MMBtu of gas a tonne. The low end is the NIPU-2026 plant basis; the high end is the industry all-in figure.

In the central case, the crop expansion adds about 3.1 million tonnes of urea a year by 2034-35. Replacing the nutrients removed with rice straw for 2G adds about 0.4 million tonnes more, for a total of 8–9% of today's roughly 40 million tonnes. That is about two and a half new 1.27 million-tonne urea plants, or more imports on top of the 10 million tonnes India already buys. Making that urea at home would need 5.4–7.1 mmscmd of gas, almost all of it imported LNG. That is 9–12% more than the fertiliser sector's 58 mmscmd (PNGRB, 2023-24). Phosphate and potash are an import bill rather than a gas bill: about 1.8 million tonnes more DAP and 1.2 million tonnes more MOP, all of the MOP imported. Straw removal nearly doubles the potash need.

The bigger gas lever is the isobutanol route. The ethanol-plus-methanol (Guerbet) route would need about 12.9 million tonnes of methanol a year at 10% isobutanol in 2034-35. At 28–30 MMBtu a tonne, that is about 28–30 mmscmd of gas, four to five times the fertiliser increase. The fermentation route that Praj and Gevo are commercialising needs none. Distillery fuel matters too: if new distilleries burned gas rather than bagasse, rice husk or stillage biogas, they would add up to 19–25 mmscmd by 2034–40, using the US dry-mill benchmark of about 23,900 Btu a gallon. E20 alone adds only about 1 million tonnes of urea and 2 mmscmd of gas.

Land: how much of India's farmland ethanol would take

India's reporting area is 306.6 million hectares. In 2022-23, 180.0 million hectares (58.7%) was agricultural land: 140.7 million hectares net sown, 24.6 million fallow (13.5 current, 11.1 older), 11.7 million culturable waste and 3.0 million under tree crops. Forests covered 72.0 million hectares (23.5%). Because much land is sown more than once a year, the gross cropped area was 219.4 million hectares, a cropping intensity of 155.9%. In 2023-24 the net sown area was 139.0 million hectares and the gross sown area 217.9 million.

Ethanol crops (about 33 lakh hectares of maize and 2.8 lakh hectares of cane juice) take about 1.6% of the gross cropped area today. In the central case they reach about 7.6% by 2034-35, or about 10% with E40 and isobutanol. Holding other crops at their current hectares, that needs about 16 million hectares more gross cropped area. Total maize area rises from 144 to about 228 lakh hectares. There are three places that land could come from:

  • Fallow land. The 16 million hectares is about two-thirds of India's 24.6 million hectares of fallow land.
  • Cropping intensity. With net sown area flat, intensity would need to rise from about 162% today (estimated) to about 174%: more rabi maize after kharif rice, and sweet sorghum in the cane off-season. The national figure has held at 155–157% through 2021–24.
  • Displacement. Otherwise ethanol crops would push out pulses, oilseeds or cotton, which India already imports. That food-versus-fuel trade-off does not show up in the land totals.

The 2025-26 gross cropped area of about 229 million hectares is an estimate. Land-use statistics lag crop estimates by about three years, so this holds the area under other crops (horticulture, fodder, plantations) at its 2022-23 level. That area was a similar 365 lakh hectares in 2023-24.

Caveats. The isobutanol blend has no notified mandate; ARAI has only run trials. SAF percentages after 2030 are extrapolations. Potable (290 crore litres) and industrial (110 crore litres) demand are estimates. E30 and E40 assume vehicles rated for them and dual dispensing. Fiscal years stand in for ethanol supply years (November to October).

What follows

  • Use the surplus for E25–E27. It covers about five blend points on today's petrol pool, and fewer each year after.
  • Build before E30. E30 with 5% isobutanol in diesel needs about 4,500 crore litres of capacity by 2029-30, against about 2,000 installed.
  • Start isobutanol on the route that uses the least ethanol, and plan for the methanol it needs.
  • Count E100 separately. Flex-fuel vehicles on E100 add about 90–100 crore litres for each 1% of petrol energy they take over, on top of the blending path.
  • Treat E40 as conditional on an E40-rated vehicle fleet and on new feedstock (second-generation ethanol, sweet sorghum, higher maize yields), not on the number of distilleries.
