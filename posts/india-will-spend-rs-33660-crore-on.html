India Will Spend Rs 33,660 Crore on Ready-Made Factory Parks. Its Existing Parks Have 1.25 Lakh Empty Plots

The Union Budget of July 2024 promised “plug and play” industrial parks in or near 100 cities, built with the states and private developers. In March 2026 the Cabinet turned that into the Bharat Audyogik Vikas Yojna, BHAVYA: Rs 33,660 crore over six years to build 100 parks where a manufacturer can lease a plot that already has roads, power, water, effluent treatment and worker housing. States sent 87 proposals in the first round, which closed on 31 July, and a second round closed on 30 September. The first selections are due within a year of the scheme’s notification in April.

The scheme’s premise is that India lacks industrial land that is ready to use. The government’s own land bank shows it does not lack industrial land as such: its mapped industrial parks already have 1.35 lakh hectares available and more than 1.25 lakh vacant plots. This piece sets out what BHAVYA offers, how it compares with the land already on the map, where states are proposing parks against where factory investment is actually waiting, and what earlier park programmes suggest about delivery.

The arithmetic is simple. At up to Rs 1 crore an acre, 34,000 acres would use almost exactly the Rs 33,660 crore outlay, so the average park would be about 340 acres and cost the Centre about Rs 337 crore. The jobs target works out to about 44 direct jobs an acre, or about Rs 2.24 lakh of central money per job. Those are the government’s targets, not results.

Land is not what is missing

The India Industrial Land Bank, the government’s map of industrial parks, listed 4,523 parks on about 7.70 lakh hectares in December 2025, with 1.35 lakh hectares available for industry and 1.25 lakh of 6.45 lakh plots vacant. BHAVYA’s 34,000 acres is about 13,759 hectares: about 10% of the land already available and under 2% of all mapped park land.

So the case for BHAVYA has to rest on quality, not quantity. Much of the available land sits in older state estates without dependable power, water, effluent treatment or connections to ports and highways, which is the gap “plug and play” is meant to close. That is a reasonable bet, but it means the test of the scheme is not how many acres it adds. It is whether its parks fill faster than the parks that already exist, and the land bank does not yet publish occupancy over time in a way that would show it.

Where states want parks, and where investment is waiting

This blog’s analysis of DPIIT’s industrial filings measures demand directly. Large manufacturers file an Industrial Entrepreneurs Memorandum (IEM) when they plan a project (Part A) and again when it is built (Part B). Filed but unbuilt investment in 2020–2025 was largest in Maharashtra (Rs 4.55 lakh crore), Karnataka (Rs 3.13 lakh crore) and Odisha (Rs 2.32 lakh crore).

The first-round proposals line up only partly. Maharashtra leads both lists, with 14 proposals. Madhya Pradesh sent the second most, 11, against a much smaller backlog of about Rs 58,000 crore. Karnataka and Odisha, the second and third largest backlogs, were not among the six states whose proposal counts were reported, which means each sent five or fewer. Gujarat, with six proposals, is building faster than it files: its implemented investment in the period exceeded its filings by about Rs 2.78 lakh crore, as earlier projects came through.

Only the six largest proposal counts and the five largest idle-SEZ counts were reported; a dash means the state was not among them, not that it has none. Filed-but-unbuilt investment is the difference between Part A and Part B filings in the period, so it approximates rather than measures the backlog; IEMs cover only large manufacturing projects.

Two things stand out. Haryana, Telangana and Karnataka already have the most notified SEZs that are not operating (14, 12 and 11; see this blog’s piece on the DESH Bill and SEZ reform), which is a reason to ask what a new park would do there that an idle zone cannot. And a proposal count measures states’ appetite for central money as much as demand from industry; the scheme’s scoring on connectivity, site suitability and the industrial ecosystem is where demand should count.

The pattern is slow filling, not a lack of approvals. The industrial corridor programme, which BHAVYA’s implementer NICDC also runs, has allotted about 5,348 acres in total across its cities, against BHAVYA’s target of 34,000 acres in six years. When the corridor programme’s apex monitoring body reviewed BHAVYA in August, the Finance Minister said the focus must move beyond approvals to completing infrastructure, allotting land, attracting investment and starting production. That is the right test, and it is not one the scheme’s published targets yet measure.

What would fix this

  • The India Industrial Land Bank should publish, for each park, vacant plots and hectares at the end of every quarter, so that occupancy in BHAVYA parks can be compared with existing parks over time.
  • DPIIT should publish every BHAVYA proposal and its evaluation score, not only those selected, so states’ proposals can be compared with demand.
  • For each selected park, NICDC should report land allotted, investment committed, units in production and jobs every six months, against the scheme’s own targets.
  • Where a state proposes a BHAVYA park, it should also report the occupancy of its existing state industrial estates and any idle SEZs within the same district.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

The Cabinet release, the scheme guidelines and the land bank’s pages could not be opened directly; figures come from their text as indexed by search engines and from news reports. Proposal counts for states outside the top six, the second round’s results and any selections had not been published by 27 October 2026. The corridor programme’s programme-wide allotment figure comes from a single, undated report.
