One Refinery Flipped Nigeria From Importer to Exporter. Africa's Import Bill Is Still Rising.

Dangote's refinery is the real thing: Nigeria now makes most of its own petrol and has cut fuel imports by more than half. Read the continent-wide numbers next to it, and the story changes. Africa's projected refined-product imports are forecast to rise from 2 million to 3.4 million barrels a day by 2050 — because demand is growing faster than the refining boom is adding capacity, not despite it.

What actually happened in Nigeria

The clearest refining success story on the continent in a generation.

The Dangote refinery, on Lagos's Lekki peninsula, is the world's largest single-train refinery. Commissioned in May 2023, it began processing crude in January 2024 and started producing Premium Motor Spirit (petrol) in September 2024. By January 2026 it was running at roughly 650,000 barrels a day, about 99% of nameplate capacity — processing 40.1 million litres of crude daily, a 25% jump from December's 32 million litres. Total investment has exceeded $19 billion.

Nigeria has since been reported as a net petrol exporter for the first time, supplying both West African neighbours and, per some reporting, contributing to European jet-fuel supply — this article treats the export-flow claims as reported, not independently traced through customs data.

The capacity figure itself carries a small discrepancy worth naming rather than smoothing over: Wikipedia's infobox lists a 700,000 bpd nameplate, while the refinery's own original design target and most current operating reports — including the 99%-utilisation figure above — are anchored to 650,000 bpd. This article uses 650,000 as the operating capacity, since that is the figure the utilisation percentage and the daily-litres numbers actually reconcile against.

Where this started: the 2018 baseline, and a four-year slip

The Dangote refinery was announced for December 2019. It started in January 2024.

An August/September 2018 trade-press account of African refining, contemporaneous with Dangote's construction, gives the “before” picture in plain numbers. Nigeria's four existing state refineries — two in Port Harcourt, one each in Kaduna and Warri — had a combined installed capacity of 445,000 bpd, but the Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation's own published utilisation was just under 37% in 2017 and 14% in 2018: even running flat out, the four together could have supplied only a quarter of Nigeria's fuel needs, and in practice supplied far less. Nigeria was importing roughly a million tonnes of fuel a month. The same account reports Dangote's investment at the time as an estimated $10 billion, targeting 600,000 bpd, expected complete by December 2019 — a target the refinery missed by more than four years, actually beginning crude processing in January 2024 and petrol production in September 2024.

The 2018 baseline for the rest of the continent was worse than four stalled Nigerian refineries. “Less than 50 refineries serve the entire continent,” most under-capacity and not built to modern fuel-quality standards. Angola's only full-scale refinery, at Luanda, dated to the 1950s, ran at 65,000 bpd nameplate but produced only 39,000 bpd, covering about a fifth of national demand while imports covered 80%. South Africa's government had announced, in 2017, its own intention to build a new 400,000 bpd refinery with BRICS partners — a project this article's 2026 research found no trace of; it appears never to have secured investment partners and to have quietly died, the same fate the 2018 account warned was typical of “the most ambitious refining projects announced in recent decades,” most of which “either remain on paper or have been mothballed due to financial constraints.”

Why the delay matters for reading today's announcements. Dangote's own refinery slipped more than four years past its announced date, and the 2018-era South African BRICS refinery appears to have slipped into non-existence entirely. That history is the reason this article treats the Kenya/Lamu proposal, the Angola Cabinda project, and the newly reported 24-project Nigerian pipeline below as directionally real but not as a settled timeline — not out of generic caution, but because this specific industry has a documented, recent track record of multi-year slippage on its highest-profile project.

What's next in the pipeline — and how solid each project actually is

One proposal at feasibility stage, several more named without a number attached.

Dangote itself is the headline name behind the next big project under discussion: a proposed 700,000 bpd refinery at Lamu, on Kenya's coast, carrying a reported $17 billion price tag. It is pitched to serve not just Kenya but Uganda, South Sudan, Rwanda, Burundi and the Democratic Republic of Congo — landlocked neighbours whose fuel currently arrives entirely by import. This is a real proposal with a confirmed site, not a rumour, but it is not under construction: it faces environmental opposition tied to Lamu's UNESCO-listed ecosystem, and Dangote has said publicly that the project would need anti-dumping protection against cheaper imported fuel before it could proceed — a condition, not a formality, given how easily a flood of imports could undercut a new domestic refiner's economics before it recovers its capital cost.

Named, not numbered. Beyond Nigeria and the Kenya proposal, the African Energy Chamber's own account of the refining pipeline names Angola's Cabinda refinery as expected to come online “alongside” Dangote's ramp-up, and cites planned refinery upgrades in Angola, Egypt and South Africa — without capacity, cost or firm timeline figures for any of them. That is not this article failing to look hard enough; it is what the primary source itself provides. Treat those three as directionally real and quantitatively unconfirmed.

The demand side nobody nets against the refining headlines

Africa isn't just building refineries. It's growing into a bigger fuel market at the same time.

Africa's total refined-product demand stood at roughly 4 million barrels a day in 2024 and is forecast to pass 6 million barrels a day by 2050 — more than 2 million bpd of pure growth, on top of whatever new refining capacity comes online. The growth is broad-based: gasoline demand alone is projected to exceed 2.2 million bpd by 2050, with Nigeria and other emerging markets leading; diesel/gasoil demand rises by roughly 880,000 bpd to just under 2.7 million bpd, nearly 50% above current levels, pushing Africa past Latin America as the world's top diesel-demand-growth region; jet fuel demand rises 65%, from 280,000 bpd in 2025 to 465,000 bpd by 2050. The demand drivers read like a development story rather than an energy story: a population rising from roughly 1.5 billion today to 2.4 billion by 2050, extractive-industry expansion (critical-minerals mining specifically named), and urbanisation across Nigeria, Angola, DR Congo, Zambia, Zimbabwe, Kenya and Côte d'Ivoire.

The number that reframes the whole story — and a newer number that complicates the reframing. Net refined-product imports are forecast to rise, not fall — from roughly 2 million bpd today to about 3.4 million bpd by 2050 — even after counting every refinery in this article's pipeline, built and proposed, at the scale that pipeline was understood to be when the Chamber's forecast was compiled. Dangote alone, at 650,000 bpd, is under a third of the continent-wide increase in demand the Chamber projects. But two developments from the same week this article was first researched, covered in the next section, put a materially larger number on Nigeria's own refining ambitions than anything folded into that 2050 forecast — and this article cannot confirm whether the Chamber's numbers already account for them.

The Chamber puts a number on what closing that gap would actually cost: over $20 billion in downstream infrastructure investment needed by 2050 — refining capacity, but also the storage, pipelines and distribution networks that determine whether a barrel refined in Lagos or Lamu can actually reach a pump in Kampala or Kigali on time. A refinery is the visible half of that number; the invisible half is what makes the fuel arrive.

Two updates from the same week: an IPO, and a pipeline that dwarfs the one above

Nigeria alone is now talking about scale that changes the continent-wide arithmetic.

Dangote Petroleum Refinery & Petrochemicals FZE has filed for an IPO targeting $5 billion, with an October 2026 listing on the Nigerian Exchange as the primary market and secondary listings under discussion in Kenya, South Africa, Egypt, Ghana and Rwanda — a genuinely pan-African offering, not a Lagos-only one. Marketing materials point to a $40–50 billion equity valuation, with proceeds earmarked for expansion, debt repayment and broadening African share ownership in the asset. The expansion plan attached to that raise is the number that matters for this article's argument: Dangote has reportedly agreed with its technology licensor to take capacity from 650,000 barrels a day to 1.4 million within three years — which would make it, on its own, the largest refinery in the world, more than double its current scale.

Separately, and even larger in aggregate: Nigeria is reported to have 24 grassroots refinery projects in some stage of planning or development, expected on stream before 2031 — more than the 11 each reported for the United States and Iran. If completed, they would take Nigeria's total national refining capacity to roughly 2.64 million barrels a day by 2031. Separately, Dangote's exports of jet fuel to Europe now have a harder number attached than this article had when first drafted: an estimated 466,000 metric tonnes exported to Europe in June alone, reportedly making the refinery Europe's largest external jet-fuel supplier, ahead of the United States — a claim this article treats as sourced to trade-press reporting rather than independently confirmed against EU import statistics, but no longer unquantified.

What this does to the article's own thesis. Nigeria's 2031 refining-capacity target (2.64 million bpd) and Dangote's own 2029 expansion target (1.4 million bpd) are both larger, on a shorter timeline, than anything this article's continent-wide 2050 forecast appears to have been built around when it named Dangote's 650,000 bpd as “under a third of the increase in demand.” Whether the African Energy Chamber's 3.4-million-bpd 2050 import forecast already incorporates this specific pipeline is not something this article can confirm — the forecast and the pipeline reporting come from different points in 2026 and this article could not establish which was compiled with knowledge of the other. If the 24-project pipeline is not priced into the Chamber's number, Africa's 2050 import trajectory may look meaningfully better than the “still rising” headline above suggests, provided the projects are actually built on the stated timeline — the same caveat this article has already applied to Kenya's Lamu proposal and Angola's Cabinda project applies here with equal force, and at even greater scale.

What this means for East Africa specifically, if Lamu happens

The region the Nigeria story doesn't reach.

Nothing about Dangote's Nigeria success flows automatically to Kenya, Uganda or the other East African markets the proposed Lamu refinery would serve — they sit on a different coast, buy from different suppliers, and today import essentially all of their refined fuel. If the Lamu project proceeds, it would be a genuinely new supply source for a region with, per this article's sources, near-total import dependence; if it does not — and the UNESCO-site opposition and the anti-dumping precondition are real obstacles, not paperwork — East Africa's import dependence continues unchanged while Nigeria's has already been transformed. The two halves of this story are not on the same timeline.

What this article does not establish. Whether the African Energy Chamber's 3.4-million-bpd 2050 import forecast already prices in Nigeria's newly reported 24-project, 2.64-million-bpd-by-2031 refining pipeline — not established here, and flagged above as the single biggest open question this update raises. Whether Nigeria's reported net-petrol-exporter status and the specific 466,000-tonne June jet-fuel-to-Europe figure have been independently verified against EU customs or shipping data, rather than trade-press reporting of industry claims — not traced to a primary statistical source. Specific capacity, cost or timeline figures for the Angola, Egypt and South Africa refinery projects named in the 2026 pipeline — still not available in the sources used here. Whether the Kenya/Lamu refinery or the 23 non-Dangote projects in Nigeria's 24-project pipeline will actually be built on their stated timelines — this article's own 2018-vs-2024 evidence on Dangote's own four-year slip is a reason for scepticism, not a prediction either way. And the exact 2024 product-level import split (how much of the ~2 million bpd baseline is gasoline vs diesel vs jet fuel) — the Chamber's source gives only 2050 product-level forecasts, not a matching 2024 breakdown.
