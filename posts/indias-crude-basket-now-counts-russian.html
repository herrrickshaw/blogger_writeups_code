India’s Crude Basket Now Counts Russian Oil as ‘Sweet’. The Barrels Its Refineries Run Are Still Mostly Sour

Exam-prep summaries in October reported the Indian Basket, the benchmark price of the crude India imports, at a ratio of about 65:35. It is easy to read that as two-thirds of India’s crude now being sweet, low-sulphur oil. It is not. The basket is a price formula, and in March 2026 the Petroleum Planning and Analysis Cell (PPAC) changed it so that Dated Brent, the sweet benchmark, carries 61% of the weight instead of 21%. The reason was pricing, not chemistry: much of what India now buys, Russian Urals above all, is sour crude that is sold against Brent.

This piece follows this blog’s piece on oil storage and the Hormuz shock and its piece on petroleum trade pairings and US crude. It sets out what the basket measures, how India’s crude sources have moved in 2026, and what the physical mix means for refiners’ costs and the hydrogen they need to remove sulphur.

Updated 7 November 2026. Provisional ship-tracking figures for October 2026 show the slate shifting after September. India imported a record of about 5.5–5.6 million barrels a day (S&P Global: 5.54 million). Russian arrivals were about 2 million barrels a day, roughly 35% of the total against about 56% in July, and Middle Eastern arrivals about 2.3 million against 2.7 million in September (Kpler data reported by ThePrint and PTI via The Week, search summaries). On those numbers Russia and the Middle East supplied about three-quarters of October’s crude, down from about 89% in September; the rest came from a wider spread of origins. The basket follows prices, not sulphur

Until February 2026 the basket weighted the Oman and Dubai average, the sour markers, at 79% and Dated Brent at 21%. Business Standard reported in April that the old formula had drifted from what refiners actually paid: under it the basket briefly reached about $156 a barrel during the Hormuz disruption, and Kpler data suggested more than half of India’s imports were priced off Brent once Russian purchases surged. PPAC moved to 61% Brent and 39% Oman/Dubai from March. The October figure of 65.27:34.73 that circulated in exam-prep notes did not say which side was which; it is consistent with the new, Brent-heavy formula.

So a higher Brent weight means more of India’s crude is priced like sweet crude. It does not mean more of it is sweet. Urals is a medium-sour crude, and Iraq’s and Saudi Arabia’s main grades are sour. On the usual definition, sweet crude has less than 0.5% sulphur; of the grades below, only US WTI Midland qualifies.

Where India’s crude came from in 2026

India’s sources swung twice this year. When the Strait of Hormuz came under attack from late February, refiners leaned on Russia, and Kpler put Russian crude at a record 2.82 million barrels a day in July, about 56% of imports. The discount that made those barrels attractive had already gone: Urals cargoes traded about $10 below Dated Brent in January, and in March Platts assessed Urals delivered to India above Dated Brent for the first time. As Gulf flows recovered and Chinese buyers competed for Russian oil, India’s Russian intake fell to 2.08 million barrels a day in August and 1.82 million in September, about 35% of imports. Ship-tracking data reported by Reuters put Middle East arrivals at 2.8 million barrels a day in September, up 32% on August.

Combining the two trackers, Russia and the Middle East supplied about 89% of September’s roughly 5.2 million barrels a day. US crude, the sweetest in the mix, went the other way: Kpler data show it falling from about 565,000 barrels a day in October 2025, when a wide Brent–WTI spread made it cheap, to about 75,000 in August 2026, a 87% drop. Whatever the trade talks with Washington say about energy purchases, they had not shown up in tanker arrivals by August.

What it costs

PPAC’s data show that the 2026 import bill is a price story. In September India imported 21.7 million tonnes of crude, 7% more than a year earlier, but paid $15.4 billion, 47% more, because the basket averaged $116.09 a barrel against $69.61 in September 2025, a rise of 67%. For April to September the bill was $90.6 billion against $61.9 billion, on almost the same volume (122.7 against 121.8 million tonnes). Import dependence on a consumption basis was 88.1%.

For refiners the margin case for Russian crude has narrowed from a $10 discount to roughly parity. Complex refineries can still gain from running heavier, sourer crude that sells at a discount to light sweet grades, but in 2026 that discount has come mostly from Gulf grades priced against Oman/Dubai rather than from Russia.

What sour crude asks of a refinery

Sulphur has to go somewhere. A barrel of Basrah Medium carries about 4.2 kg of sulphur, about 4 times a barrel of Murban and far more than WTI Midland. Most of it ends up as hydrogen sulphide removed in hydrotreaters, as sulphur in heavy fuel and coke, or as recovered elemental sulphur. As this blog’s piece on refinery hydrogen explains, every litre of BS-VI diesel has to be brought down to 10 parts per million, using hydrogen made mostly from natural gas. Chemistry sets a floor: removing a tonne of sulphur as hydrogen sulphide consumes at least 63 kg of hydrogen, and real hydrotreaters use several times that because they also saturate other molecules.

That means a sourer slate raises hydrogen demand, sulphur-recovery load and catalyst wear, at the same time as India is asking refiners to replace part of their grey hydrogen with green hydrogen at about three times the cost. A slate that has stayed sour while being priced as if it were sweet carries a processing cost that the basket does not show.

What would fix this

  • PPAC should publish, alongside the basket, the monthly physical slate of crude processed by Indian refineries: volume by origin, API gravity band and sulphur band.
  • The basket notes should state plainly that the weights reflect pricing benchmarks, so that a Brent-heavy formula is not read as a shift to sweet crude.
  • Refiners should report hydrogen consumption per tonne of crude processed and its sulphur content each year, so the cost of the slate shows up in the green-hydrogen debate.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

PPAC’s own basket notes and India’s customs data by grade could not be opened directly; the weights come from Business Standard’s report of the March change and the October ratio from a secondary summary that did not label its sides. Russian and Middle East volumes come from different tanker trackers (Kpler and Reuters), so the combined share is approximate; one weekly tracker reported much lower Russian arrivals in late September, which this piece could not reconcile. The US series comes from a single report of Kpler data. No published figure for Indian refiners’ gross margins in the July–September quarter was found, so this piece does not estimate the margin effect. Assay figures vary by cargo, and no reliable sulphur figure for Urals was found.
