India's sour crude: high-sulphur grades were 78.7% of refinery runs in 2025-26

Skip to article content
Text Size

Top line

  • 78.7% of crude processed in FY2025-26 was high-sulphur, up from 72.8% in FY2020-21 (PPAC); the IEA puts sour grades at 85% of imports in 2023, up from 77% in 2021 by this blog’s arithmetic.
  • 3% to 40% is Russia’s share of India’s imports between 2021 and 2023 (IEA, from Kpler), mostly medium-sour Urals, taking share from the Middle East (61% to 45%) and West Africa (12% to 4.5%).
  • −$1.58 was PPAC’s Brent-minus-Dubai spread for FY2025-26, a sour premium; it was +$3.53 in FY2022-23, so the discount that makes sour crude attractive reversed in 2026.

What it means: High-sulphur runs grew by 52.6 million tonnes between FY2020-21 and FY2025-26 while low-sulphur runs fell by 2.3, so all of the growth in refining came from sour grades, and the share slipped from 79.6% to 78.7% in FY2025-26 only because low-sulphur runs recovered by 3.1 million tonnes.

What to do: PPAC should publish its high-sulphur and low-sulphur series monthly with the sulphur cut-off it uses, so that the runs data can be set beside the import data.

What it means: The IEA’s shares show Russian medium-sour crude replacing Gulf supply and West African and Latin American barrels from 2021, and Business Standard’s customs-based figures for the same year put Iraq at 24% and Russia at 2%, so the two sources agree on the starting point.

What to do: The Commerce Ministry and PPAC should publish crude imports by origin and by API and sulphur band each month, because the only multi-year origin series found here is from a commercial ship tracker.

What it means: Russia’s volume rose 159% from February to July, fell 30% by October, while Angola and Venezuela together rose from 232 to 680 thousand b/d between September and October (these do not offset the Russian fall from July), and Kpler’s February call of US crude at up to 10% of intake matched October 2025’s 11.4% and not the 2.8% of October 2026.

What to do: Refiners and PPAC should state, with each month’s import release, which tracker or customs series the origin shares come from, since three providers give three different Russian figures for one month.

What it means: The average sour discount in the 9 positive years of the series was $1.59 a barrel and it swung by $5.11 between FY2022-23 and FY2025-26, so the saving that complex refiners earn on sour crude is small and unstable, and Jamnagar and Vadinar (31.7% of runs) carry most of the capacity to exploit it.

What to do: Refiners should disclose each year the share of runs under term contracts against spot and the average discount to Dated Brent by grade, so the margin from running sour crude can be separated from freight and timing effects.

What to watch next

  1. Each monthPPAC’s monthly import and flash reports show total crude imports; October 2026 flash data is already out, and the origin split still has to come from trackers.
  2. Each quarterIOC, BPCL and HPCL earnings calls give the spot-to-term split; IOC reported 84% spot for April–June 2026.
  3. Next price moveUrals delivered to India was a $2 to $3 discount to Dated Brent on 23 November, and BPCL expects fewer Russian cargoes in November, so Gulf share should recover first.
  4. OPEC+OPEC crude output rose 1.38 million b/d to 22.87 million b/d in October (as reported in this blog’s November basket piece); more Gulf supply favours Gulf sour grades over Russian ones.

What would fix this

  1. PPAC: publish crude imports by origin country and by sulphur and API band each month alongside the import release, so that the sour share does not have to be read from commercial ship trackers.
  2. PPAC: state the sulphur cut-off that separates high-sulphur from low-sulphur crude in Table 4.2 and publish the series monthly, so it can be compared with the IEA’s sour definition.
  3. Ministry of Commerce and PPAC: reconcile the customs and PPAC import tonnages and state which one feeds each published ratio, since press reports gave 234.3 million tonnes for both FY2023-24 and FY2024-25.
  4. Refiners: disclose term against spot purchases and the average discount to Dated Brent by grade in each quarterly result.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Method and definitions
  • PPAC’s high-sulphur share (Table 4.2) is of all crude processed, imported plus domestic, using the oil companies’ own classification; the IEA’s sour share is of imports, by quality band, from Kpler. They are different measures and are never combined into one series here.
  • The IEA prints 85% sour and 63% medium-heavy for 2023, and says sweet fell by 8 points to 15%. Adding 8 gives 23% sweet and 77% sour for 2021; that 2021 figure is this blog’s arithmetic. The IEA’s 62% (medium and heavy sour) plus 24% (light sour) is 86%, one point above its printed 85%, which is rounding.
  • Origin changes are the IEA’s printed 2021 and 2023 shares subtracted. The IEA text gives ‘just under 3%’ and ‘nearly 40%’ for Russia, entered as 3 and 40.
  • The Brent-minus-Dubai figures were read from the data labels on the PPAC chart (page 115); the labels sit beside, not on, some points, so each was matched to its year by the colour and x-position of the label and checked against the lines. The 2016-17 pair was initially read the wrong way round and corrected on the rendered chart.
  • Kpler figures are provisional ship-tracking estimates, not customs data. October 2026 totals are provisional to 30 October.
  • Landed prices from customs data are not a sour discount: they include freight, insurance, contract timing and quality differences.
What this piece could not establish
  • The sour share of India’s imports for any year other than 2021 and 2023, and any year before 2021. The IEA has an annual 2014–2023 chart but prints only those two years in text. PPAC’s high-sulphur series in the 2025-26 Ready Reckoner starts in FY2020-21; earlier editions were not read.
  • Crude imports by origin from an official table. PPAC’s site and Ready Reckoner give no origin split (only that sourcing grew from 27 to 41 countries). A DGCI&S Kolkata document found through search returned a placeholder page, so its FY2022-23 to FY2024-25 Russian shares (21.6%, 35.9%, 35.8%) are not used.
  • Origin shares for FY2015-16 to FY2020-21 and for FY2025-26 as a whole.
  • FY2015-16 import tonnage of about 202.85 million tonnes. PPAC’s current table starts in FY2018-19. The conflict over 243.22 and 234.3 is resolved against PPAC’s table: 243.2 is FY2024-25 and 234.3 is FY2023-24; the claimed FY2023-24 figure of 226.6 matches no year in that table.
  • Other 2026 Russian shares from the search (46.8% in March, about 54% in June, Gulf suppliers 39% in September, OPEC 53.2% and Russia 27.4% in December 2025): not opened here, different providers, and not used.
  • Urals discounts for February and March 2026 (about $9 below ICE Brent; Platts parity on 17 March; $28.75 below at Primorsk): not confirmed from a primary release. Only the Reuters-reported pattern is used.
  • The Russian arrivals conflict for October 2026 (Kpler 1.97 million b/d against S&P Global 692 thousand b/d) and the three July shares (55%, 55.5%, 50.83%).
  • HPCL Rajasthan Refinery: the reported 2 million barrels in August 2026 were Nigerian Okwuibome and Utapate crude from Glencore, bought by tender, and this piece did not check their assays, so it does not call them sour. IOC’s October 2025 Americas tender sought both low- and high-sulphur grades.
  • Refinery complexity indices by plant. PPAC publishes no Nelson index; processing share is used as a proxy.
