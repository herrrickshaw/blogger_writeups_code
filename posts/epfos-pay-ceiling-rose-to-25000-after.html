EPFO’s Pay Ceiling Rose to ₹25,000 After 12 Years. In 2014 Rupees, That Is Slightly Less Than the Old ₹15,000

On 16 September 2026 the Cabinet raised the pay ceiling of the Employees’ Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO) from ₹15,000 to ₹25,000 a month, effective the next day. The ceiling decides who must be enrolled in the provident fund, the Employees’ Pension Scheme and the linked insurance scheme: a worker who joins a covered establishment on pay up to the ceiling is enrolled compulsorily, and contributions for everyone are worked out on pay up to it. The government expects about 51 lakh more workers to come under compulsory cover, and puts its annual outgo at ₹11,339 crore, against about ₹10,250 crore of existing annual support, as reported.

It is the first change in twelve years, and in real terms it is a catch-up rather than an expansion. On MoSPI’s consumer price index, ₹15,000 in September 2014 had shrunk to about ₹8,710 in 2014 rupees by August 2026. The new ₹25,000 is worth about ₹14,520 in 2014 rupees, roughly 3% less than the ceiling it replaces was worth when it was set.

A ceiling with no rule for moving it

Nothing links the ceiling to prices or wages. It is changed when the government decides to change it, and the gaps have been long: eighteen years between 1967 and 1985, thirteen between 2001 and 2014, twelve since. In each long gap the ceiling falls behind wages, a growing share of formal-sector workers earn above it, and the compulsory part of India’s main retirement system covers fewer of them. A worker hired on ₹16,000 last year in a covered factory did not have to be enrolled.

The minimum wage had already passed it

The minister said, in announcing the change, that the unskilled minimum wage is above ₹15,000 in many states. The figures bear that out. Delhi’s unskilled minimum wage is about ₹18,456 a month from April 2026, and Haryana’s about ₹15,221 after a 35% increase. The Centre’s own rate for unskilled sweeping and loading work in the highest-cost ‘Area A’ cities is ₹827 a day, roughly ₹21,500 for a 26-day month. In those places a worker on the legal minimum was already above the old ceiling and outside compulsory cover.

The provident fund ceiling has also drifted away from the Employees’ State Insurance (ESI) threshold. The 2014 change brought the two into line at ₹15,000. ESI’s threshold was raised to ₹21,000 in 2017 and, as far as this blog could find, has not changed since; the provident fund ceiling is now ₹25,000. A worker earning ₹22,000 now gets compulsory provident fund and pension cover but not compulsory health insurance.

This blog’s arithmetic at the standard statutory rates; the insurance and administration charges are rounded at 0.5% each. Employers in some sectors and smaller establishments pay a 10% rate instead of 12%.

For a newly covered worker the cost is the same 13% of pay for the employer, and 12% out of take-home pay for the worker. For members already earning above ₹15,000, contributions are worked out on pay up to the new ceiling, so the statutory base rises from ₹15,000 to ₹25,000: the employer’s pension contribution goes from ₹1,250 to about ₹2,083 a month, a 67% increase, and the government’s from ₹174 to ₹290.

The pension side is where the change compounds. The Employees’ Pension Scheme pays the average pensionable salary times years of service, divided by 70, with pensionable salary capped at the ceiling. A worker with 35 years of service at the cap could have expected about ₹7,500 a month; at ₹25,000 the same service would give ₹12,500. Pensions are worked out pro rata for each ceiling period, so a worker who earned above ₹15,000 before 17 September 2026 gets the higher rate only for service after it. The scheme’s finances will carry that larger promise; its actuarial valuations are not routinely published.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Labour and Employment should index the wage ceiling to a published measure, such as the Consumer Price Index for Industrial Workers or the median wage of EPFO members, revised every two or three years by notification, so the ceiling does not lose two-fifths of its value between decisions, as it did from 2014 to 2026.
  • The Ministry of Labour and Employment should align the provident fund and ESI thresholds and revise them together, so the same worker is not compulsorily covered for retirement but not for health.
  • EPFO should publish the actuarial valuation of the Employees’ Pension Scheme with the new ceiling, showing the cost of the higher pension promise and how the government’s 1.16% contribution and the employers’ 8.33% are expected to meet it.
  • EPFO should publish monthly enrolment by wage band, so it is possible to see how many of the expected 51 lakh workers are actually enrolled, and in which sectors and states.
  • The Ministry of Labour and Employment should publish how the 51 lakh estimate and the ₹11,339 crore outgo were calculated, so the figures can be checked against enrolment as it happens.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
