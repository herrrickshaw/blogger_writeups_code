India’s 2026-27 Ethanol Tender Drops Cane Juice, Cuts Maize by Rs 3 a Litre and Puts the Cheapest Grain First

India’s oil marketing companies opened bidding on 16 October for about 1,100 crore litres of ethanol for the year from November 2026 (BPCL e-tender 26985). Compared with last year’s tender, three things change. Ethanol made from cane juice, sugar syrup and B-heavy molasses is not being bought at all, because the government has told the industry to divert no sugar to ethanol this year. Maize, the feedstock that carried the blending programme last year, gets Rs 3 a litre less. And after the preferences for cooperative sugar mills and plants with long-term contracts, which carry over from last year, the tender spells out that the rest goes to the cheapest feedstock first, which puts maize last in line. Every award is also provisional, pending Supreme Court cases over how ethanol is allocated.

How much to trust it. This year’s terms are read directly from the tender document. Last year’s come from the ESY 2025-26 tender as summarised in this blog’s research notes and from press reports of its outcome; the BPCL tender portal and government sites could not be opened from here. Where this piece calculates something, it says so.

Prices: maize loses its premium

The basic rates are unchanged for every feedstock still bought. The only price change is the maize incentive, cut from Rs 5.79 to Rs 2.79 a litre, which brings maize ethanol from Rs 71.86 to Rs 68.86. That is still the highest price in the tender, Rs 4.86 above damaged grain and Rs 8.54 above FCI rice. The All India Distillers’ Association has asked for the cut to be reviewed, citing higher raw-material, energy and logistics costs, according to ChiniMandi.

The saving to the oil companies depends on how much maize they end up buying. At last year’s maize volume of about 479 crore litres, Rs 3 a litre is about Rs 1,440 crore a year, by this blog’s arithmetic; the actual figure will be known only after allocation.

The cane gap

Last year cane juice, sugar syrup and B-heavy molasses together supplied about 26.4% of the roughly 1,048 crore litres allocated, about 276 crore litres. None of that is being sought this year. The government’s reason, as reported, is a smaller sugar crop in Maharashtra and Karnataka and lower opening sugar stocks. Only C-heavy molasses, the final residue of sugar-making, which supplied about 1.2% last year, remains on the cane side. Grain will have to supply almost all of the 1,100 crore litres.

One cost moves against distillers even where the ethanol price does not. FCI’s rice price for distilleries rises from Rs 2,320 to Rs 2,390 a quintal for November 2026 to June 2027, according to a Rajya Sabha reply reported by Business Standard, while the tender keeps rice ethanol at Rs 60.32 a litre.

The supply should be there. Last year distillers offered about 1,305 crore litres from grain alone, more than this year’s whole requirement. The question is which grain.

Who gets allocated first

When offers exceed the requirement in a quarter, the tender allocates in this order:

  1. Cooperative sugar mills registered with the national federation, for their eligible offers.
  2. Dedicated ethanol plants with long-term offtake agreements, up to the annual quantity in their contracts, cheapest feedstock first.
  3. Everyone else, cheapest feedstock first: C-heavy molasses and other feedstocks at Rs 57.97, then FCI rice at Rs 60.32 (capped at 108 crore litres a quarter), then damaged grain at Rs 64.00, then maize at Rs 68.86. Within the cheapest category still open, plants from the oil companies’ first long-term tender (2021) get preference up to their design capacity on a “best endeavour” basis, and the rest is shared pro rata.

Uttar Pradesh is allocated separately because of state excise rules, and a linear-programming optimiser then assigns depots, with at least three suppliers per depot cluster and no supplier above 40% of a cluster.

Because maize is the dearest feedstock, it is allocated only after cheaper offers run out, unless the plant has a long-term contract or is a cooperative. FCI rice is capped, and none at all can be offered for August to October, when the requirement is 250 crore litres. ChiniMandi’s analysis argues that damaged grain, mostly broken rice, at Rs 64 could take share from maize. How much depends on offers this blog cannot see.

The court cases

Both the opening disclaimer and the allocation annex make every allocation, award and purchase order provisional. They are subject to a writ petition of 2025, special leave petitions in the Supreme Court led by SLP 22411 of 2026, cases from the Bombay High Court’s Nagpur bench and the Patna High Court transferred to the Supreme Court on 4 August 2026, and an interim arrangement approved that day. Those cases challenge the oil companies’ allocation method, including last year’s tender 22376. Last year’s cooperative-first priority was contested by dedicated grain plants: one, VINP Distilleries, won a Karnataka High Court order in June 2026 for a larger allocation under its long-term contract, and the Supreme Court ordered status quo on BPCL’s appeal. On 4 August 2026 the Supreme Court allowed the oil companies to award an extra 149 crore litres for August to October 2026, taking last year’s total to about 1,197 crore litres. Bidders must accept that allocations can be cut, moved or cancelled without compensation. The tender’s long “best endeavour” clause, which says the preference for long-term plants is “not a guarantee of offtake”, reads as written with those cases in mind.

What is not known

  1. Offers. How much each feedstock will offer, which decides how much maize is bought. Bids close on 29 October.
  2. B-heavy and juice plants. What happens to distilleries built around cane juice and B-heavy molasses, and to the sugar mills that relied on ethanol sales to pay cane farmers.
  3. Court outcome. The Supreme Court could change the allocation method after awards are made.
  4. Later cycles. The tender is cycle 1. Whether cane juice returns in a later cycle depends on the sugar crop.

What would fix this

  • The oil companies should publish the allocation by feedstock, state and supplier type for each cycle, as ChiniMandi’s reporting of last year’s figures shows is possible, so that the effect of the maize cut can be seen.
  • Tie the FCI rice cap to live central-pool stocks, not a figure fixed for the year, so that rice for ethanol tapers if stocks fall.
  • Announce the sugar-diversion rule before the cane crushing season and before distillers commit feedstock, with a rule for when cane juice comes back, instead of deciding each year.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
