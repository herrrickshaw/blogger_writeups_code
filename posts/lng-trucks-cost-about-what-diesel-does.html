India had about 1,309 LNG trucks on the road at the end of 2025, up 75% from 747 a year earlier and about a third of the growth the industry had expected. An independent think-tank found their total cost of ownership roughly comparable to diesel's, with lower CO2 but more nitrogen oxide, and the Government of India offers no transport-sector incentive to tip the balance. Fleets run for GreenLine, Delhivery, UltraTech Cement and Tata Steel are growing all the same, concentrated on corridors where fuel is within reach.

The fleet grew 75% in 2025, about a third of the expected growth

India's fleet of LNG-powered trucks grew from 747 at the end of 2024 to about 1,309 at the end of 2025, a rise of 75%, according to Business Standard's January 2026 report (also carried by Essar). Forecasts from major players and Vahan registration estimates going into the year had pointed to roughly three times that growth rate. Some retail LNG stations have since shut for lack of truck traffic to serve, and state-run refiners paused expansion plans in 2026. Dispensing stations built ahead of demand rather than behind it are where a fleet that misses its forecast is felt first.

Counts for 2026 disagree. About 1,350 LNG trucks were registered by June 2026 (Autocar Pro). Transport ministry data show about 645 in operation, and PNGRB cites a base of about 700 trucks and about 20 filling stations (both as reported by India Seatrade News; PNGRB's outlook was reported in June 2025 by ETV Bharat). The gap is likely the difference between registered and operating vehicles.

Total cost of ownership is roughly comparable to diesel

The most rigorous independent analysis found for this piece is IEEFA's April 2025 briefing, "Assessing the Viability of LNG Trucks for India's Clean Goals." It found total cost of ownership for LNG and diesel heavy trucks in India roughly comparable, not dramatically apart, once the volatility of LNG's own price is factored in: a fuel-cost advantage that can narrow or disappear depending on where LNG prices sit, rather than a stable structural saving. On emissions, LNG combustion cuts CO2 by roughly 28-30% against diesel but produces 2 to 5 times more nitrogen oxide (NOx), a local air-quality cost that gets far less attention in LNG-trucking marketing than the CO2 figure. IEEFA also flags a stranded-asset risk: heavy investment in LNG fleets and fuelling infrastructure sits awkwardly next to India's longer-run zero-emission-truck ambitions, which point toward electric or hydrogen trucks rather than a fossil gas intermediate step.

A separate figure in trade coverage, attributed to NITI Aayog, puts LNG truck cost of ownership at roughly ₹34.38 per kilometre, described as "comparable" to diesel, which is broadly consistent with IEEFA. The same coverage puts an LNG truck's capital cost at roughly ₹40-42 lakh against ₹30 lakh for a diesel equivalent, a ₹10-12 lakh premium paid back over an estimated 36-40 months through fuel savings. None of these three figures was traced to a primary NITI Aayog document, and the payback estimate comes from vendor-adjacent trade coverage rather than an independent audit.

China reached about 30% LNG truck sales with subsidies India does not offer

LNG-powered trucks took close to 30% of China's heavy-duty truck sales in 2024, up from below 10% in late 2023 (Wood Mackenzie; IEEFA). Three things drove it: a widening LNG-to-diesel price spread, tighter emission standards, and subsidies aimed at replacing ageing diesel trucks with LNG ones. India has none of the third: industry sources describe the Government of India as offering no transport-sector incentive for LNG trucks, so adoption runs on fuel-cost economics alone. With a total cost of ownership close to diesel's and no subsidy to tip it, growth of 75% that falls well short of the forecast is closer to what the fuel-cost math would predict than the marketing suggests.

China's own comparison point has since moved. IEEFA reported that surging electric-truck sales had begun to stall China's LNG-trucking growth. LNG's share of China's heavy-truck registrations fell from 37% in March 2026 to 11% in July (Kpler data), and electric trucks took 50.3% of heavy-truck sales in September 2026 (Electrek). The 30% figure is a 2024 reference point, not evidence that LNG trucking is a settled long-term winner even in the market where it grew fastest.

Fleet operators: GreenLine, Delhivery, UltraTech Cement and Tata Steel

Real fleets are scaling faster than the cost numbers alone would predict, which suggests other factors (corporate ESG commitments, expected diesel-price or emissions-regulation risk, or manufacturer and financing incentives this piece's sourcing does not cover in depth) are doing work alongside the fuel-cost math.

GreenLine Mobility Solutions, backed by the Essar Group and running trucks built by Essar-backed Blue Energy Motors, reported about 650 LNG trucks in April 2025 and more than 1,000 LNG-and-EV trucks by May 2026, with over 90 million kilometres run and a reported 24,000-plus tonnes of CO2 avoided. It raised $275 million in April 2025, including from investor Nikhil Kamath, and describes a longer-run ambition of up to $1 billion in investment behind a ₹1,500 crore plan for 10,000 trucks and 50-100 of its own LNG stations. Its routes serve steel, cement, mining, FMCG and chemicals corridors, including Tata Steel's Meramandali, Odisha plant, where a partnership expansion was flagged off in May 2026; Tata Steel's LNG trucking runs through GreenLine rather than a fleet of its own. Essar's umbrella platform, combining Blue Energy Motors, GreenLine and Ultra Gas & Energy, now targets 30,000 LNG and electric trucks and 100 fuelling and charging hubs (YourStory, January 2026), above GreenLine's 10,000-truck figure.

Delhivery's LNG programme is real and distinct from its more widely covered EV fleet. Volvo Trucks India's press release confirms delivery of 20 LNG-powered FM 420 4x2 tractors, using Volvo's High-Pressure Direct Injection (HPDI) system, on 19 June 2024, for roughly 1,000-kilometre express-cargo and B2B routes alongside Delhivery's diesel fleet. Delhivery had been trialling LNG trucks since September 2022. A later Volvo Trucks India social-media post referred to a scale-up to 50 LNG trucks; that figure rests on the post alone.

UltraTech Cement runs 67 LNG trucks alongside 468 CNG trucks, including at its Awarpur Cement Works near Nagpur and a Pune bulk terminal, through logistics partner Green Planet Logistics, described in coverage as India's first dedicated LNG-fuelled heavy-trucking logistics company. This piece searched for, but found no sourced evidence of, LNG truck adoption at VRL Logistics, Coca-Cola India or ITC.

GreenLine's fleet, funding and investment figures come mainly from Essar Group's press channel and Autocar Professional, so they are company-reported rather than audited. Delhivery's programme is from Volvo Trucks India's press release, corroborated by Delhivery's blog, MarkLines and Transport Topics. UltraTech's fleet and its Green Planet Logistics partnership are from Business Standard's coverage of the company's ESG initiative.

Manufacturers: Blue Energy Motors, Ashok Leyland, Volvo and Tata Motors

Blue Energy Motors is the largest dedicated LNG-truck manufacturer in the Indian market, building 55-tonne-class trucks (India's heaviest LNG trucks) with FPT Industrial, part of the Iveco Group. It reports roughly 1,000 vehicles sold, more than 60 million kilometres run and over 15,000 tonnes of CO2 cut, by its own accounting. Ashok Leyland delivered the AVTR 1922, India's first LNG-powered haulage truck, to Mahanagar Gas in November 2023, and is the first Indian truck manufacturer with its own in-house LNG engine, built to BSVI Stage II norms. Volvo Trucks India supplies its FM 420 LNG tractor with HPDI fuel injection to fleets including Delhivery.

Tata Motors launched LNG versions of its Prima trucks and tippers at EXCON in Bengaluru in December 2023, and in October 2024 began delivering 150 Prima 5530.S LNG trucks to Clean Green Fuel and Logistics, with an agreement for 350 more (Autocar Professional). In October 2025 it also signed a memorandum of understanding with gas utility THINK Gas to expand LNG fuelling infrastructure for commercial vehicles, which adds fuel supply alongside its trucks.

Blue Energy Motors' figures are company-reported, via The Ken's profile and Essar's press coverage. Ashok Leyland's details are from its own press release and Autocar Professional. Volvo's are from its own press release. Tata Motors' launch, deliveries and THINK Gas agreement are from Autocar Professional; the launch and delivery details are search-result summaries of trade press, and no Tata release was opened.

Fuelling stations: PNGRB rules, public-sector plans and private corridors

LNG truck fuelling runs on a regulatory track PNGRB separated from its city-gas-distribution (CGD) regime. Building a CNG station ordinarily requires the CGD authorisation for that geographical area (GA). PNGRB's decision, reported in 2020 trade coverage, lets any entity build an LNG dispensing station in any GA, whether or not it holds the local CGD licence. That is what made a standalone, cross-territory LNG highway-station network possible, rather than a separate CGD deal in every district a corridor passes through. PNGRB also keeps a technical-standards regulation for LNG, the LNG T4S (Technical Standards and Specifications including Safety Standards), last amended on 23 July 2025, and published a case study on small-scale LNG (SSLNG) infrastructure dated 1 August 2025.

The network itself is thin against a national heavy-truck fleet in the millions. GAIL has committed roughly ₹650 crore to 29 LNG stations over two to three years. IndianOil's first phase targets 16 stations on the Golden Quadrilateral, of which 6 were reported commissioned. Adani Total Gas has commissioned its first LNG truck-fuelling station. One source names ExxonMobil and Mitsui as prospective partners in GAIL's Golden Quadrilateral build-out; ExxonMobil's own site confirms some involvement in LNG for commercial trucking in India without that partnership detail.

The 2025 shortfall split the field. State-run refiners have held back on retail expansion, while private players, Ultra Gas and Think Gas among them, are still building, using corridor-first strategies (dedicated freight lanes with guaranteed traffic) rather than the broader station-first rollout that left some outlets under-used. That suggests the underperformance is less about LNG trucking failing everywhere and more about which rollout model and which route clears the demand bar without a subsidy. LNG trucking in India today is concentrated on routes where an operator has built its own stations, as GreenLine plans to, or can reliably reach the few public ones.

On targets, PNGRB's outlook, reported in June 2025, puts the base at about 700 LNG trucks and about 20 filling stations and projects about 5 lakh trucks by 2040, with LNG the preferred long-haul fuel after 2030 (ETV Bharat; India Seatrade News). Trade coverage in Indian Infrastructure also circulates a national target of about 50,000 LNG long-haul trucks by 2030, an earlier ambition of 50 highway stations by 2025, and about 1,000 stations across highways, industrial corridors and mining regions. The 50,000-by-2030 figure is not in PNGRB's outlook as reported, and none of these three was traced to a PNGRB press release; they are industry-cited ambitions, not confirmed commitments.

The 2020 decoupling is corroborated across LNG Industry and NGS India; the PNGRB notice text itself was not pulled from pngrb.gov.in. The T4S amendment and the SSLNG case study are from PNGRB's own published PDFs. GAIL's, IndianOil's and Adani Total Gas's station figures are from Indian Infrastructure; the ExxonMobil and Mitsui claim traces to a single source (LNG Industry).

What would fix this

  • PNGRB should publish, in one dated document, whatever national targets it holds for LNG trucks and fuelling stations, or state plainly that it has none, and a quarterly list of LNG dispensing stations by operator and highway corridor showing which are operating, which have shut, and their monthly sales, so the 50,000-truck and 1,000-station figures are confirmed or retired, progress on GAIL's 29 and IndianOil's 16 stations is tracked from the regulator, and corridor-first and station-first rollouts can be compared on utilisation.
  • The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways should publish monthly Vahan registrations of goods vehicles by fuel type, state and manufacturer, with LNG shown as its own category and registered and operating vehicles counted separately, so the fleet size (1,309 at end-2025 from industry, about 1,350 registered or about 645 operating in 2026) comes from one official series rather than company and industry estimates.
  • The Central Pollution Control Board should commission and publish on-road NOx and CO2 measurements for LNG and diesel heavy trucks under Indian conditions, and the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways should publish type-approval emission results for both, so the 28–30% CO2 saving and 2–5 times higher NOx that IEEFA reports are tested before LNG trucking is scaled further or given incentives.
  • NITI Aayog should publish the document and assumptions behind the ₹34.38 per km LNG truck cost-of-ownership figure attributed to it, so fleet operators and lenders can check the number rather than relying on trade-press citation.
  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should publish the reasons and review conditions for the state-run refiners' 2026 pause in LNG retail expansion, so it is clear what fleet size or station throughput would justify resuming it.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

  • Which fleet count is right. The 1,309 end-2025 figure is from industry reporting; the 2026 counts (about 1,350 registered, about 645 operating, about 700 cited by PNGRB) come from search-result summaries, and the registered-versus-operating explanation for the gap is likely but unconfirmed.
  • Whether the targets are official. The 50,000-trucks-by-2030 and roughly 1,000-station figures rest on trade-press citation, not a located PNGRB or ministry document. The PNGRB decoupling rests on secondary reporting of the regulatory action.
  • The NITI Aayog-attributed cost figures. The ₹34.38/km, ₹40-42 lakh capital cost and 36-40 month payback were not traced to a primary document.
  • Independent fleet and funding numbers. GreenLine's and Blue Energy Motors' figures come predominantly through Essar-affiliated channels and are not audited. Delhivery's 50-truck figure rests on one social-media post, and the ExxonMobil and Mitsui partnership on one outlet. The absence of VRL Logistics, Coca-Cola India and ITC from coverage is an absence of evidence, not proof they run no LNG trucks.
  • What a comparable cost means for a fleet. A cost roughly comparable to diesel's with a real CO2 benefit can still be a reasonable bet for a large operator managing regulatory and reputational risk, which may explain why fleets are scaling. This piece does not show LNG trucking makes no economic sense, and treats the NOx tradeoff as a real air-quality cost rather than a footnote.
  • Method. Research was through web search rather than full page reads in every case; PNGRB's regulatory text was reached through search-visible PDF content. Nothing here is investment, procurement or fleet-purchasing advice; check current fuel-price, cost and station figures against an operator, manufacturer or regulator.
