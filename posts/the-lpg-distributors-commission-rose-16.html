The LPG Distributor’s Commission Rose 16 Times Since 2004. After Inflation, It Is Back Where It Was in 2012

The commission an LPG distributor earns on a domestic cylinder is set by the government and the public-sector oil companies, and PPAC publishes every revision since April 2004. Read in current rupees, the record looks like steady progress: Rs 16.71 a cylinder in 2004, Rs 73.08 since October 2023, a 4.4-fold rise. Read against consumer prices, it looks different. From 2012 to 2018 each revision did little more than restore what inflation had taken. The one real raise came in three monthly steps in 2019, and inflation has since taken most of it back. In December 2025 rupees the commission is worth about what it was in October 2012.

How much to trust it. The revision history to May 2022 is PPAC’s own table, posted on 4 July 2022. The October 2023 rate of Rs 73.08 is from PPAC’s commission page as this blog read it in August 2026; a Goa government notice of March 2024 gives Rs 73.03 instead, and the difference is not resolved. Inflation is MoSPI’s consumer price index, which in this series runs to December 2025. Cylinder prices and the distributor numbers come from Parliament replies, PIB and press reports as seen in search summaries. Where this piece does its own arithmetic, it says so.

The 5 kg cylinder has carried half the 14.2 kg rate throughout. From October 2012 to July 2019 PPAC also lists an “additional commission for sale at market-determined price”, paid on non-subsidised cylinders: Rs 0.75 to Rs 0.82 until 2015, then reset almost every month between Rs 0.06 and Rs 0.63. It ended when the August 2019 revision took effect. Real values start in July 2011 because the MoSPI index used here begins in 2011.

Three phases

2004 to 2011: small steps. Five revisions took the commission from Rs 16.71 to Rs 25.83, about 55% in seven years. The consumer price index used here does not reach back that far, so this phase is shown only in current rupees.

2012 to 2018: a jump, then a treadmill. October 2012 brought the largest single step in the record, 44%, from Rs 25.83 to Rs 37.25. Press reports at the time said the increase was passed on to consumers in the cylinder price, and the distributors’ federation still asked for Rs 65. Six revisions followed between December 2013 and November 2018. Each looks like a raise in current rupees, but in December 2025 rupees every one of them set the commission back to between Rs 70 and Rs 73. A 2014 Lok Sabha reply says the revisions were made on the recommendation of the oil companies and PPAC, and a November 2018 report, as relayed in search summaries, describes the Rs 50.58 rate as interim, pending a fresh cost study.

2019 to now: one real raise, then erosion. In August, September and October 2019 the commission rose from Rs 50.58 to Rs 61.84 in three steps, 22% in all, while the separate market-price commission was dropped. That lifted the real value to Rs 83.18, the highest in the series. Then came 31 months without a revision, while consumer prices rose 16.6%. The May 2022 step of 4.9% did not close that gap, and the October 2023 step of 12.7% left the commission at Rs 78.09 in December 2025 rupees on the day it took effect. By December 2025, with no revision found since, it was back to Rs 73.08 in December 2025 rupees, 2.7% above October 2012.

What the commission pays for

The commission is not all margin. A Goa government notice of March 2024, citing the Government of India, says the Rs 73.03 it quotes includes Rs 33.65 a cylinder for home delivery, and that distributors may not charge households extra for delivery. A 2015 Lok Sabha reply put the delivery charge at Rs 18. On the March 2024 figures, delivery takes 46% of the commission, leaving about Rs 39 a cylinder for the showroom, godown, staff and administration. The notice says the rate is revised from time to time on the basis of a cost study by IIM Ahmedabad. That study has not been found in public.

Against the cylinder price

Because the commission moves in steps and the cylinder price moves every month, the commission’s share of what a household pays swings widely. Unlike the petrol and diesel dealer, who since 2013 (petrol) and 2014 (diesel) has been paid a fixed amount plus a percentage of the product price, the LPG distributor gets a flat amount, so a higher cylinder price passes nothing through to the distributor.

The share ran from 6.2% to 9.6% across these dates. It was lowest when the cylinder was dearest, in December 2018 and July 2022, and highest when it was cheapest, in December 2020. These are prices at the Delhi counter. Before 2015 households paid a subsidised price; since direct benefit transfer the counter price is the market price, and Ujjwala households get a subsidy credited separately.

On these assumptions the average distributor delivers about 6,089 cylinders a month and earns about Rs 4.4 lakh in gross commission, of which about Rs 2.0 lakh is the delivery component. That is close to the Rs 4.77 lakh this blog estimated for the average petrol pump. No published study of distributor costs or net income was found. The 14.9 million tonnes is a half-year figure and doubling it ignores seasonality, the count of distributors may include ones outside the public-sector companies, and 5 kg cylinders are counted as 14.2 kg equivalents.

Compared with the pump and the kerosene dealer

Since 2017 the LPG distributor has done better in real terms than the petrol dealer. In December 2025 rupees the LPG commission was Rs 71.60 after the September 2017 revision and is Rs 73.08 now, up 2%. The fixed part of the petrol dealer’s commission fell from Rs 3.91 to Rs 3.14 a litre over a similar span, down 20%, because it went unrevised from August 2017 to October 2024, though the petrol dealer’s percentage component rose with prices. The wholesale commission on PDS kerosene, the third administered margin PPAC tracks, rose from Rs 204 a kilolitre in 2004 to Rs 1,341.88 in February 2019 and shows no revision after that in a table posted in July 2022, as PDS kerosene is phased out.

What is not known

  1. The exact current rate. Rs 73.08 (PPAC page, August 2026) against Rs 73.03 (Goa notice, March 2024).
  2. Any revision after October 2023. None was found, and the April 2025 demand for Rs 150 has no reported outcome.
  3. The cost study. The IIM Ahmedabad study the rate is said to rest on has not been found in public.
  4. Distributor income. The monthly figures above are this blog’s estimate from national totals, not a measured average.
  5. Inflation in 2026. The MoSPI series used here ends in December 2025; a new series on a 2024 base was not linked.

What would fix this

  • PPAC should keep the LPG commission table current and show the delivery component separately, so the share that pays for delivery can be tracked.
  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should publish the cost study behind the rate, so the basis for Rs 73 and for the distributors’ Rs 150 can be compared.
  • The non-delivery part of the commission could be indexed to consumer prices between studies, as the percentage component already does for petrol and diesel, so the real value does not depend on when the next revision comes.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
