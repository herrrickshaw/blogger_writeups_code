Gas in India's energy mix: share down from 7.5% to 7.0%, against a 15% target

Parliament’s Committee on Estimates reported on 7 August on the supply and distribution of natural gas. Its starting point is the government’s target of raising gas to 15% of India’s energy mix by 2030, from about 6% today against a world average near 24%. It recommends a yearly action plan, more help for deep-water and frontier exploration, a flexible way of allocating gas to idle power plants, and more balanced development of import terminals.

This piece checks the target against the official numbers. The Ministry of Statistics’ energy balance shows gas losing share, not gaining it, over the last decade. A 15% share by 2029-30 would need almost three times the gas India now uses, most of it imported, at a time when the 2026 Hormuz disruption has shown what depending on imported LNG costs.

The share has been falling

India’s gas use grew 31% in energy terms between 2014-15 and 2023-24, but its total primary energy supply grew 41%, mostly from coal and oil. So gas’s share slipped from 7.5% to 7.0%. Consumption then fell 3% in 2025-26 to about 69 billion cubic metres (bcm), according to PPAC, which puts the share near 6.4% on this blog’s estimate. The committee’s “about 6%” is consistent with that.

The decline is not spread evenly. City gas for vehicles and kitchens has tripled in a decade and is now the largest user, as this blog has reported. Gas to power plants fell from 10.9 to 8.0 bcm over the same decade.

On recent growth in India’s total energy use, primary energy supply would reach about 48,782 petajoules in 2029-30. Fifteen per cent of that is about 190 bcm of gas a year, or 522 million standard cubic metres a day (MMSCMD). India used about 189 MMSCMD in 2025-26. Reaching the target would need consumption to grow about 29% a year for four years, after a decade in which it grew about 3.7% a year.

Almost all of the increase would have to be imported. The IEA expects domestic output to stay just under 38 bcm by 2030, so the rest, about 152 bcm, would come as LNG: roughly 112 million tonnes a year, more than four times the 25.5 million tonnes India bought in 2025 and nearly twice its 58.5 million tonnes of terminal capacity.

The government’s own forecasters do not expect anything close. PNGRB’s expert committee, in a report this blog reviewed in September, put 2030 demand at 260–300 MMSCMD in its base case. On this piece’s energy projection that is a share of about 7.5–8.6%. The IEA’s outlook, with LNG imports rising to 64 bcm, gives about 8%. Both are roughly half of the target. An earlier industry vision, which this blog set against the outcome, expected 746 MMSCMD and a 20% share by 2030.

Idle gas power plants are a small lever

India has about 25 GW of gas-fired power capacity, according to Petronet LNG’s chief executive, much of it idle because imported gas is too expensive to burn for power. The Central Electricity Authority’s gas-station reports put the fleet’s average load at 10.5% in 2025-26, against a target of 14.8% and about 24% in 2017-18. The committee’s flexible allocation framework is meant to put more gas through these plants.

Even a large recovery would move the national share only a little. Taking the fleet back to its 2017-18 load would need roughly 10 bcm more gas a year, on this blog’s scaling of PPAC’s figure for gas burned in power. That would add about 1.0 percentage points to gas’s share of primary energy. Industry estimates put the LNG price at which these plants run at meaningful loads at about $6–7 per million British thermal units, and at $9–10 for evening peaks only. The Asian spot benchmark was assessed at about $10.9 in early February 2026, before the Hormuz disruption cut global LNG supply by about a fifth.

What 2026 showed about relying on LNG

About 60% of India’s LNG used to come through the Strait of Hormuz, mostly from Qatar and the UAE. After loaded Qatari tankers stopped transiting the strait in late February 2026, India’s LNG imports fell about 13% in March and April, and Petronet issued a force majeure notice to QatarEnergy. Buyers then shifted to cargoes from the United States, Oman, Nigeria and Angola: imports in May to July rose 15.4% from a year earlier to 7.08 million tonnes, even as supplies from Qatar fell 91%. Gas was rationed by priority, with city gas, fertilisers, power and ceramics first in line.

Demand had already been weakening before the disruption. Consumption in April to October 2025 fell 8.6% from a year earlier, which an Observer Research Foundation analysis attributes to price shocks, supply problems and falling domestic output. Imports recovered in 2026-27, up 4.4% in April to September, but they now meet 52.3% of consumption, up from 50.1%. Every step toward 15% would raise that share further.

Terminals are not the bottleneck

India’s eight LNG terminals worked at about 47% of capacity in 2025, down from 58% in 2024, according to the International Gas Union. This blog’s comparison of terminal expansion and new-build found that expanding Dahej cost about Rs 370 crore per million tonnes of yearly capacity, against Rs 950–1,290 crore for new onshore terminals, and that Dahej ran at about 92% while the other terminals ran at 10–40% in April–May 2026. The constraint is the pipeline capacity to move gas inland from the terminals and the price of the gas, not the tanks. That points the same way as the committee’s call for more balanced terminal development: fewer new terminals, more pipeline out of the ones that exist.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas should restate the 15% target with a published path: consumption, domestic output and LNG imports for each year to 2030, on the same basis as MoSPI’s energy balance, as the committee’s yearly action plan implies.
  • Gas policy should be judged against PNGRB’s own 2030 demand range, which implies about 7–9% of primary energy, rather than a 15% figure no forecaster expects.
  • The flexible allocation for gas power should be tied to peak hours, where higher prices can be paid, and its effect reported as gas burned and hours run each month.
  • Pipeline capacity out of the under-used terminals should take priority over approving new ones.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece could not establish

The committee’s report was read through PRS Legislative Research’s summary, not the report itself. The energy shares use MoSPI’s energy balance, which ends in 2023-24; the 2025-26 share is an estimate that converts PPAC’s volume into energy using the 2023-24 ratio of 38.4 PJ per bcm. The same ratio for 2015-16 is 43.2, so the two sources do not convert consistently, and the estimate could be off by several tenths of a point. Primary energy growth to 2029-30 is assumed, not forecast. The 2024-25 consumption figure is derived from PPAC’s reported fall in 2025-26. Gas-power capacity is 25 GW in industry statements but about 20 GW in the CEA’s monitored-station reports, and the extra gas needed to raise the fleet’s load is scaled from PPAC’s power-sector consumption rather than modelled plant by plant. Full-year LNG import figures for 2025-26 were not found.
