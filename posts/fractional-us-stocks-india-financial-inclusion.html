A Slice of Apple Costs About ₹1,700. A Slice of MRF Still Isn't Legal. Fractional Investing Hasn't Closed That Gap Yet.

Indian investing apps already let you buy a $10 slice of a $780,000 Berkshire Hathaway share. That's a real, working piece of financial inclusion, built entirely on foreign-market plumbing. The much harder version — a fractional slice of an Indian stock like MRF — is still against the law, and the regulatory path to changing that only opened in mid-2025.

1. The barrier this is actually solving

India has roughly 23.15 crore demat accounts as of June 2026, and the pace of new account openings has been accelerating again — over 2.5 million added in June 2026 alone, the highest monthly count in months. That's a real, broad base of retail savers with somewhere to put money. The problem it doesn't solve on its own is price: owning "one share" of a company is an arbitrary unit set by that company's own share count, not a number calibrated to what an ordinary saver can afford.

MRF's price is famous specifically because the company has never split its stock — a deliberate choice, not a reflection of the business being 10x the size of its tyre-industry peers. Berkshire's Class A shares are the same story taken to an extreme: Warren Buffett has kept them unsplit since the 1960s precisely to keep short-term speculators out, which also means keeping ordinary savers out unless they can find a fractional route in.

2. How fractional US-stock investing actually works today

This part isn't hypothetical — Indian platforms (Vested, INDmoney, Winvesta and others) already run it, and the mechanism is straightforward:

  • The broker holds the whole share; you hold a beneficial slice of it. When an Indian investor places an order for a fraction of a US stock, the broker buys (or already holds) a full share and splits the economic ownership internally. Several platforms use distributed-ledger-style record-keeping to track exactly who owns what fraction of each pooled share.
  • Minimums are genuinely small. Platforms advertise starting amounts as low as $5-10 — meaning a saver can put ₹500-1,000 into a fractional slice of Apple, Microsoft or Berkshire the same way they'd start a mutual fund SIP.
  • It runs through India's existing outward-remittance rules, not a special carve-out. Money leaves India under the RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS), capped at US$250,000 per resident individual per financial year, cumulative across all banks and linked to PAN. There's no LRS carve-out for "small" fractional purchases — the same ceiling and paperwork apply whether you're buying $10 or $10,000 of stock.
  • It isn't free of friction or cost. Remittances above ₹10 lakh in a financial year attract 20% Tax Collected at Source (TCS) — reclaimable against your income tax return, but a real upfront cash-flow cost in the meantime — on top of whatever FX conversion spread and brokerage the platform charges.

None of this requires Indian company law to change, because the shares being fractionalised are foreign-listed. The custody and splitting happens on infrastructure India doesn't control and doesn't need to legislate for.

3. Why the same trick doesn't work for Indian stocks — yet

Fractional ownership of an Indian-listed share is a different problem, and it's a legal one, not a plumbing one. The Companies Act, 2013 requires share ownership in whole units, and SEBI's own regulations reinforce that. A broker cannot simply do for MRF what Vested does for Berkshire — split one share into beneficial fractions internally — because Indian company law doesn't currently recognise fractional shareholding as valid.

This has been on the table before and failed: SEBI rejected a similar fractional-ownership proposal in its regulatory sandbox in 2021, specifically over unresolved questions about who legally custodies a fraction of a share. The Company Law Committee recommended in 2022 that the Companies Act be amended to permit issuance and transfer of fractional shares in dematerialised form — that amendment has not been made.

What changed in 2025 is narrower than "fractional shares are now legal," and worth stating precisely rather than rounding up: in July 2025, SEBI admitted a Bengaluru-based startup, Xaults, into its innovation sandbox to test a new custody model — holding the fractional entitlement at the depository level (NSDL/CDSL) rather than at the broker level, so ownership legally sits with the investor or the depository rather than being an internal broker ledger entry. Xaults co-founder Neeraj Singh described the approach as working "together with the depository and enable it at the depository level... ownership will still sit in the user's account or directly with the user." The company had a three-to-four-month window to demonstrate the model to SEBI and market participants, with a graduation to full regulatory sandbox testing — and a specific depository partner — contingent on SEBI's assessment afterward. As of this writing, this is a live pilot, not a completed rulemaking, and no announcement of the pilot's outcome was found.

A claim worth flagging and setting aside: several crypto-industry news sites (not any mainstream Indian financial outlet, and nothing found on SEBI's own press-release listings) have reported that SEBI has already launched "tokenized" fractional shares of Reliance Industries for retail investors. That claim could not be independently verified against SEBI's official communications or mainstream Indian financial press, and is not treated as fact in this article. The verified, sourced development is the Xaults sandbox admission described above — a real but much earlier-stage and narrower step than a live tokenized-RIL-shares product.

4. What this means for financial inclusion, honestly

The inclusion case for fractional investing is real, but it's currently asymmetric. It's already solved for accessing foreign high-priced stocks — an Indian saver with ₹1,000 and a functioning LRS remittance route can genuinely own a sliver of Berkshire Hathaway or Apple today, which was not meaningfully possible for a retail investor a decade ago. That's not a small thing for a market with 23+ crore demat accounts and a growing SIP culture, most of whose holders are working with far less than ₹1 lakh at a time.

What it hasn't solved is the domestic version of the same problem. MRF is the extreme example, but the underlying issue — high per-share prices as an unintentional wealth filter on which Indian companies an ordinary saver can own directly, one whole share at a time — sits entirely on the Indian side of the barrier, and closing it needs the Companies Act amendment the Company Law Committee already recommended in 2022, not just a clever custody workaround. The Xaults sandbox pilot is the first real regulatory motion toward that outcome since SEBI's 2021 rejection, but "admitted to a sandbox to demonstrate a concept over three to four months" is a meaningfully earlier stage than "fractional Indian shares are now available to retail investors" — and it's worth being precise about which of those two things is actually true right now.

Verdict: Inclusion via fractional investing is real but currently one-directional. Indian savers can already buy fractional slices of expensive US stocks through existing apps, LRS limits, and ordinary FX/TCS costs — a genuine, working reduction in the price barrier to global blue chips. Fractional ownership of expensive Indian stocks is still not legal, is going through its first real regulatory sandbox test only as of mid-2025, and needs a Companies Act amendment that hasn't happened yet. Treat "fractional Indian shares are coming" as a real but early-stage regulatory process, not a launched product — and treat any claim that it has already shipped, including the tokenized-Reliance-shares reports circulating in crypto press, as unverified until SEBI itself confirms it.
