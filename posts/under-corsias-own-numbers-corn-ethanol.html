CORSIA, the International Civil Aviation Organization's Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation, only counts a fuel as a Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) if its full lifecycle carbon intensity sits at least 10% below a fossil-jet baseline of 89 gCO₂e/MJ. That test runs on default values published by ICAO-approved certification schemes, one feedstock and process at a time. Run India's most heavily promoted ethanol feedstock through it, and the fuel that comes out the other end does not merely fail to help — on the certification scheme's own default number, it is dirtier than the fossil jet fuel it would replace.

The number that doesn't survive contact with the table

Reading ISCC CORSIA 205 "Life Cycle Emissions" v2.0 — the document by which ISCC, an ICAO-approved Sustainability Certification Scheme, reproduces ICAO's own default carbon-intensity tables — the alcohol-to-jet (ATJ) route via the ethanol pathway gives corn/maize grain a default core-plus-indirect-land-use-change value of 90.8 gCO₂e/MJ using US assumptions, or 100.6 gCO₂e/MJ on the global default. Both sit above CORSIA's 89 gCO₂e/MJ fossil-jet baseline. A fuel has to come in at least 10% below that baseline to qualify as a CORSIA Eligible Fuel at all; corn ethanol's own default doesn't merely miss the 10% cut, it doesn't clear the baseline in the first place. This is a default value from the certification scheme's own published table, not a modelled estimate from this piece.

One caveat worth stating plainly. Different life-cycle models disagree on corn ethanol's carbon intensity by a wide margin: the CORSIA/ISCC default used above (90.8–100.6) carries a heavy indirect-land-use-change penalty, while the US Department of Energy's more recent GREET model puts unmitigated corn-based ethanol-to-jet closer to 69.1 gCO₂e/MJ direct plus roughly 9.0 for ILUC — around 78 total, which would pass the CORSIA test. The GREET figure is not what CORSIA certification actually runs on today, so it does not change what a producer can currently claim, but it is the strongest argument industry has for why the ISCC default might eventually be revised.

MoPNG has been promoting exactly this feedstock

The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas has actively promoted maize as an ethanol feedstock since a PIB release in November 2023, and this blog has separately documented how far that push has gone on the road-fuel side: roughly 37% of India's annual maize crop is now estimated to be diverted to ethanol production, a diversion this blog traced through to a documented rise in egg prices via the poultry-feed market — see The Diesel Truck Isn't Why Your Eggs Cost More. The Ethanol Tank Is and India Hit E20 on Maize and Rice, Not Sugarcane (this blog's own reporting). None of that ethanol is currently being converted to jet fuel at scale — India's SAF programme today runs overwhelmingly on used cooking oil, not grain ethanol. But if maize-ethanol capacity built for the road-fuel mandate were ever redirected toward SAF, CORSIA's own default table says that specific fuel would not qualify. The maize-ethanol build-out and the SAF programme are, on the numbers, pointed in opposite directions.

Molasses passes — through a route India doesn't use

The confusion compounds when the feedstock is India's dominant one, cane molasses. Molasses does not appear at all in CORSIA's ethanol-to-jet default table — it has no listed default value on that specific route. It does appear, with a default value, on the isobutanol-to-jet route: 34.3 gCO₂e/MJ (Brazil default) to 36.1 (global default), a roughly 59–61% cut against the 89 baseline, comfortably clearing the bar. The catch is that India's ethanol industry is built around ethanol, not isobutanol — this blog has covered separately why the world's ATJ plants, and India's own limited alcohol-to-jet efforts, lean on ethanol rather than isobutanol despite isobutanol being certified two years earlier; see India Wants Isobutanol in Both Diesel and Jet Fuel. It Has Built a Domestic Supply Chain for Neither (this blog's own reporting). A molasses-to-ethanol-to-jet producer in India cannot simply cite a default value the way a molasses-to-isobutanol-to-jet producer could; it would have to run a full actual-value lifecycle calculation through an approved certification body instead — a real cost and audit burden that has nothing to do with molasses actually being a dirty feedstock, and everything to do with which named chemical route a plant is built around.

India's targets sit at the slow end internationally and apply only to international flights: the EU runs 2% in 2025 rising to 6% by 2030 and 70% by 2050; the UK runs 2% in 2025 to 10% by 2030; Japan targets 10% by 2030; Singapore runs 1% from 2026 to 3–5% by 2030. India has no domestic-flight SAF mandate notified at all, and no SAF-specific national policy — SAF is not integrated into the National Policy on Biofuels 2018, entering only implicitly through that policy's "drop-in fuels" definition.

The feedstock India can actually use today is mostly wasted

Used cooking oil (UCO) is the only feedstock behind India's certified SAF production so far — IOCL Panipat's certification runs on it. India generates an estimated 1.8–2.6 million tonnes of UCO a year, of which only 110,000–156,000 tonnes, roughly 6%, is collected for reuse of any kind; the remaining 94% is uncollected, and a further share of what is collected re-enters the food chain or is exported rather than converted to fuel. Meeting even the 5%-by-2030 international target is estimated to need roughly 0.4–0.5 million tonnes of SAF annually — a quantity that dwarfs current UCO collection rates. Agricultural residue is the larger long-term resource (roughly 230 million tonnes of surplus residue, on the government's own figure), but converting residue to jet fuel needs Fischer-Tropsch gasification or advanced cellulosic alcohol-to-jet technology that has no commercial-scale plant in India yet; first-of-a-kind Indian SAF plants beyond simple co-processing are not projected before 2029, after both the 2027 and 2028 targets fall due.

What would fix this

  • Rule maize ethanol out of SAF policy explicitly, in writing, now. Nothing currently stops maize-ethanol capacity built for the road-fuel mandate from being pitched at SAF investors or procurement programmes; an explicit statement that it does not qualify under CORSIA's own default values would prevent capital being misallocated toward a route that cannot be certified as built.
  • Get an actual-value CORSIA calculation done for molasses-ethanol-to-jet, once, and publish it. A single approved-body actual-value study, done centrally and made public, would let every Indian molasses-ethanol producer cite a validated number instead of each commissioning its own audit from scratch.
  • Publish a UCO collection target with a funded mechanism, not just a feedstock estimate. A 94%-uncollected figure restated every year without a collection-infrastructure plan attached is a fact, not a policy.
  • Name a domestic-flight SAF trajectory, even a modest one. International-only targets leave the much larger domestic aviation market with no signal at all to plan capacity against.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

What this piece does and doesn't cover

  • The CORSIA/ISCC default values above trace to ISCC CORSIA 205 v2.0 (December 2023). ICAO's own CORSIA Eligible Fuels document is now in its 8th edition (November 2025); trade reporting on that edition describes refined core and ILUC values across several pathways, so treat the exact decimals here as potentially an edition or two behind the current official table — the structural findings (corn ethanol fails, molasses has no ethanol-route default) are independent of which edition is current, but a producer seeking a certifiable number should pull the current edition directly, not from this piece.
  • This piece does not model the cost side of SAF pathways (production cost, market price, or the case for India's PBtL biomass-plus-green-hydrogen route, which independent modelling puts below the global SAF cost benchmark but which has no commercial plant operating anywhere) — that is a separate piece of analysis this blog may cover on its own.
  • PNGRB has no role in SAF regulation. ATF marketing runs through MoPNG's ATF Control Order, 2001, and SAF oversight sits with DGCA (CORSIA compliance), MoPNG (marketing) and BIS (fuel specification) — worth stating plainly since this blog has covered PNGRB extensively on adjacent gas-sector topics.
