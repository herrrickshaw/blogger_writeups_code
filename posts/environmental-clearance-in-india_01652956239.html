Top line

  • 105 days is the limit the EIA Notification sets from the final EIA report to a decision: 60 days for the expert committee and 45 for the authority. If the authority stays silent, the applicant may proceed as the committee finally recommended.
  • 225 of 256 accredited EIA consultant organisations on the NABET portal are category A, the only kind that may prepare the EIA for a category A project. 27 of the 256 entries show a validity date that had already passed on 27 November 2026.
  • 5 km from a protected area, critically polluted area, eco-sensitive area or inter-state boundary moves a category B project from the State authority to the Ministry, which then appraises it as category A (10 km for river valley, thermal power, industrial estates and hazardous-waste sites).

Who decides: category A goes to the Ministry, B to the State, and 5 km can move B to A

  1. 1. Find the item in the ScheduleThe Schedule to the EIA Notification lists each activity with a threshold. The threshold makes the project category A or B.
  2. Category AThe Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) decides, on the advice of the Expert Appraisal Committee (EAC). Scoping, an EIA report and a public hearing apply, with a few exceptions.
  3. Category BThe State or Union Territory Environment Impact Assessment Authority (SEIAA) decides, on the advice of its State Expert Appraisal Committee (SEAC). The SEAC screens the application first.
  4. B1 and B2B1 needs an EIA report, terms of reference and a public hearing. B2 skips all three and is appraised on Form 1 and its documents.
  5. Minor minerals, 5 ha or lessThese are B2 projects. A District Environment Impact Assessment Authority (DEIAA) decides on its district committee’s advice, and the SEIAA supervises it.
  6. The General ConditionA category B project within 5 km of a protected area, critically polluted area, eco-sensitive area or inter-state or international boundary is appraised by the Ministry as category A. The distance is 10 km for river valley, thermal power, industrial estates and parks, and common hazardous-waste facilities. Two States sharing a boundary can agree to reduce or waive the boundary distance.
  7. When the State body does not existA Ministry memorandum of 28 February 2025 says a category B project is considered centrally if no SEIAA or SEAC is duly constituted. A notification of 13 July 2026 (S.O. 3862(E)) sets a four-year term for a SEIAA’s chairman and non-official members and lets a Standing Authority on EIA act for up to six months, extendable by six.

What it means: The category, set by the size of the activity in the Schedule, decides whether the Ministry or the State authority grants the clearance, and a site next to a protected area or a State boundary can override the size.

What to do: Companies should read the Schedule row, the General Condition and the project’s coordinates together before choosing the authority, because a wrong choice means filing again.

What it means: The notification caps the official review at 105 days after the final EIA report and lets the applicant proceed if the authority is silent, but it sets no limit on the time spent preparing the study.

What to do: Companies should plan the schedule from the date of the final EIA report and track each query on PARIVESH, because the statutory clock covers the review and not the preparation.

What it means: Only a consultancy accredited for the project’s sector and category can write the EIA that the committee will take up, and while 225 of 256 listed organisations are category A, accreditation is sector by sector and 27 entries show a validity date already passed.

What to do: Companies should check the consultant’s sector, category and validity date on the NABET portal on the day they sign, and again before the EIA is filed, because an expired certificate can stall an appraisal.

What it means: The finders cover different ground, with NSWS answering which licences a business needs across ministries and States and the PARIVESH tool answering which environmental, forest, wildlife and coastal clearances a particular site needs, and each says its result is guidance only.

What to do: A company should run NSWS for the full list of approvals, then the PARIVESH tool with the site boundary for the clearances, and treat both results as a starting checklist to confirm with the Schedule.

After filing: rejection is rare, delay and delisting are the risk

What it means: Once a proposal reaches a decision it is almost always granted (88.7% of 61,361), so the risk for a company lies in an application that lapses or waits and not in a rejection.

What to do: Companies should answer queries inside the portal’s time limits and watch for delisting notices, because an auto-delisted application starts again from the beginning.

The path a company follows on PARIVESH

  1. Check the Schedule and the site. Find the activity and its threshold, apply the General Condition to the coordinates, and run the PARIVESH Know Your Approval tool with a KML file.
  2. Register. The PARIVESH steps are Know Your Approval (optional), e-KYC (mandatory, with a PAN), a Common Application Form and the clearance-specific form.
  3. Engage a NABET-accredited consultant for the sector and category, and file Form 1 with the pre-feasibility report. Construction projects add Form 1A and a conceptual plan, and minor minerals up to 5 ha use Form 1M.
  4. Get the terms of reference (category A and B1). The committee, or the standard ToR route, sets what the EIA must cover.
  5. Prepare the draft EIA and request a hearing from the State pollution board, with at least 10 hard copies and the summary in English and the local language. The board gives at least 30 days’ notice.
  6. File the final EIA with the answers to the hearing, and Part C of the form. Under the 2022 PARIVESH form structure, Part A and Part B entered at the ToR stage can be edited if the detailed studies change them.
  7. Appraisal and decision by the EAC (category A, Ministry) or the SEAC (category B, SEIAA).
  8. After the grant: forms exist for a corrigendum (Form 2), amendment of ToR or clearance (Forms 3 and 4), extension of validity (Form 6) and transfer (Forms 7 and 8). Compliance reporting runs on the same portal.

Clearances from other bodies are not needed before an application is received, screened, scoped, appraised or decided, unless the sequence depends on one in law (para 8(v)). Forest, wildlife and coastal clearances have their own paths; see this blog’s article on them.

What would fix this

  1. NABET: print an ‘as on’ date on the accredited list and mark entries whose certificate or extension date has passed, so that a company can tell a lapsed entry from a current one without opening each certificate.
  2. MoEFCC: publish the median days each stage takes on PARIVESH, by category and by State, beside the statutory limits, so that planning does not depend on anecdote.
  3. NSWS and PARIVESH: let the NSWS questionnaire hand its project details to the PARIVESH Know Your Approval tool, so that a company enters the site and capacity once.
  4. The Ministry: publish a consolidated EIA Notification that states in the text, not only in footnotes, which clauses are suspended or under appeal.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Method and definitions
  • Rules and limits are read from the EIA Notification 2006 (S.O. 1533(E), 14 September 2006) in the consolidated text hosted on PARIVESH, marked as on 13 July 2026, with the Schedule rows quoted from that text. Amendments cited by their S.O. numbers are those footnoted in it.
  • Counts of accredited consultancies come from one fetch of the NABET portal list on 27 November 2026. The list is saved with this piece (names, certificate numbers, category and dates only). A certificate counts as current if its validity date, or its extension-letter date, was on or after 27 November 2026.
  • The funnel figures are from the PARIVESH 2.0 register as analysed in this blog’s earlier article, not recomputed here.
  • The NSWS counts are the portal’s own statements on its pages; the ratio is a division of those two numbers.
What this piece could not establish
  • How long clearances take in practice. The notification gives limits, and the PARIVESH register could be used to measure elapsed days by stage, but that was not done here.
  • How a query returned to the applicant, or a postponed hearing, affects the statutory clock: the paragraphs read give the limits but not the stop-and-restart rules.
  • The time and cost of preparing an EIA, including baseline data, and the fees for the application, the consultant or the hearing. None is in the notification.
  • Whether the NSWS questionnaire lists the environmental clearance among its results for a given project. The NSWS pages read were its user guides, FAQs and home page, not a run of the live questionnaire, so the NSWS row above describes its inputs and stated purpose only.
  • Which clauses of the notification are suspended. The consolidated text marks some 2016 amendments (S.O. 141(E)) as suspended by the National Green Tribunal on 11 December 2018 and under appeal in the Supreme Court; which obligations that changes today was not worked out.
  • The exact Schedule boundary cases (for example a distillery at exactly 100 KLD), because the symbols did not survive text extraction.
  • Whether the 27 NABET entries with a passed validity date are awaiting renewal, and the portal’s own ‘as on’ date, which was blank in the fetched page.
  • Consents under the Water and Air Acts from the State pollution board, building permissions and other licences that sit outside the environmental clearance. A commercial summary site was used only to cross-check the stage timings and is not cited for any figure.
