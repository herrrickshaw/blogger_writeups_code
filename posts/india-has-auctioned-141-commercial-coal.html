The Ministry of Coal announced the 16th round of commercial coal mine auctions on 16 September 2026: 25 blocks in nine states, 21 of them fully explored. The releases around it describe six years of auctions as a success: 147 mines sold over 15 rounds, a combined peak capacity of nearly 378 million tonnes a year, and bidders offering the government an average of 24% of the value of their coal, six times the 4% floor.

The ministry’s own reply to Parliament in August gives the other half. Of the 141 mines auctioned in the first 14 rounds, 132 had allocation orders by 30 June 2026, 23 had permission to open, and 15 were producing coal. Commercial mines produced about 26 million tonnes in 2025-26, roughly 7% of their rated capacity. As with this blog’s piece on oil and gas blocks, an awarded block is not the same as a producing one.

Updated 7 November 2026. Newer monthly figures from the Ministry of Coal, as reported: captive and commercial mines together produced 117.23 million tonnes in April–October 2026, up 11.3% on a year earlier, and 22.03 million tonnes in October alone, up about 42%. The Lohari mine in Jharkhand received opening permission in October, taking operational captive and commercial mines to 87. These are the combined captive-and-commercial series described below, not the commercial-only count of 15 producing mines, for which no newer figure was found. No bid results for the 16th round have been reported yet.

From auction to coal

Each step in the funnel is a separate approval. After the auction comes the allocation order and the vesting of the block. The owner then needs a mining plan approved by the coal ministry, environmental and forest clearances from the environment ministry, consent from the state pollution board, the land itself, and finally mine opening permission from the Coal Controller. Most of the drop in the funnel is between allocation and opening permission: 109 allocated mines did not yet have permission to open.

Why 7% is unfair, and why it still matters

Comparing 26 million tonnes with a rated capacity of 366 million tonnes overstates the gap. Rated capacity is what a mine produces at full development, and many of these mines were sold only in the last two or three years. ICRA, the rating agency, says a coal block typically takes four to five years to develop. Mines from 2023 onwards were not due to produce yet.

The fairer test is against the ministry’s own schedules. The auction documents give a winning bidder 51 months to start producing from an explored block and 66 months from a partially explored one, cut to 40 and 52 months from the 15th round, as reported. Blocks from the first rounds of 2020 and 2021 are past those dates. An analysis of Right to Information replies, reported by the Indian Express in March 2026, found that 31 of 98 blocks that should have been operational had missed their deadlines, by between six months and more than seven years. It also counted 35 blocks surrendered and 55 producing out of 209 auctioned or allotted since 2015. Those are a single newspaper’s figures from RTI replies; the ministry does not publish a block-by-block schedule against which they could be checked.

Two series are mixed in public statements. “Commercial” mines are those auctioned since 2020, whose owners may sell coal freely. “Captive and commercial” adds the blocks re-allotted or auctioned after the Supreme Court cancelled 214 allocations in 2014, most of them tied to the owner’s own power, steel or cement plants. Most of the 210 million tonnes comes from those older captive blocks.

The ministry’s headline production number is for captive and commercial mines together: 210 million tonnes in 2025-26, a record, though short of the 226 million tonne target. That group’s share of India’s coal output doubled in four years, from 10.9% to 20.2%. Most of that coal comes from blocks allotted or auctioned after 2015 to power utilities and steel and cement makers for their own plants, not from the mines auctioned for open sale since 2020. The commercial mines added about 26 million tonnes. The 2029-30 target of 384 million tonnes depends on the funnel above emptying faster.

The 16th round

The new round offers 25 blocks: 21 explored and 4 partially explored, in nine states, 6 under the Coal Mines (Special Provisions) Act and 19 under the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act. At the launch the ministry handed over production agreements for six mines from the previous round, with a combined capacity of 11.62 million tonnes a year and reserves of about 1,504 million tonnes. The shorter development schedules apply to this round, and the ministry has pointed bidders to the Koyla Shakti dashboard and the CLAMP land portal launched in October 2025 to speed up the steps after the auction.

Shorter deadlines matter only if missing them costs something. The auction documents provide for appropriation of the performance security when milestones slip, but the ministry has not published how often that has happened, or how much has been collected.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Coal should publish a public milestone tracker for every auctioned block on Koyla Shakti: the scheduled and actual date of the mining plan, each clearance, land possession, mine opening permission and first coal, so delays are visible block by block rather than through RTI replies.
  • The Ministry of Coal should publish, each year, the performance security appropriated on delayed blocks, by block and amount, so the penalty clause in the auction documents can be seen to work.
  • The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change should show the status and age of every pending coal block clearance on PARIVESH, and report how many exceeded the time limits in its own rules.
  • The Ministry of Coal should publish why each of the surrendered blocks was given up before it is offered again, so later bidders know whether the problem was geology, land, clearances or the bid itself.
  • State governments with auctioned blocks, chiefly Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh, should publish land acquisition and rehabilitation timelines for each block through the CLAMP portal, since land is the step the owner controls least.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
