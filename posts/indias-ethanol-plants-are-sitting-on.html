How an ethanol plant ends up with food-grade CO₂

Fermentation is stoichiometrically simple: one molecule of glucose becomes two molecules of ethanol and two molecules of carbon dioxide (C₊H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂). By mass, that works out to roughly 0.95 kilograms of CO₂ for every kilogram of ethanol produced, before accounting for real-world fermentation efficiency losses. At plant scale, a 200 KLPD (kilolitre-per-day) fuel-ethanol distillery — not an unusual size in India's ethanol-blending programme — correspondingly generates on the order of 98 tonnes of CO₂ a day as fermentation off-gas.

That raw CO₂ is not food-grade on its own; it carries ethanol vapour, water, and trace sulphur and hydrocarbon compounds picked up in the fermenter. Getting it to food and beverage grade is a standard industrial-gas process: water-scrubbing strips out the ethanol and water-soluble organics (the recovered ethanol is typically routed back into the main process rather than wasted), an oxidiser or catalytic bed deals with residual hydrocarbons and sulphur compounds, activated-carbon or molecular-sieve beds dry the gas to a very low dew point, and the cleaned gas is compressed and liquefied through a refrigeration loop. Praj Industries, India's largest ethanol-technology and equipment provider, markets a "Bio CO₂" product line built to do exactly this, describing its output as ISBT-compliant, food and beverage grade; India Glycols Limited separately markets its own "Bio-Carbon Dioxide" from its distillery operations, describing a purification system built around washing, scouring, absorption and NOx removal. The purity bar both are aiming for is the one the International Society of Beverage Technologists sets for beverage-grade CO₂: a minimum of 99.9 per cent purity by volume, with oxygen capped at 30 parts per million. India's food regulator, FSSAI, separately designates carbon dioxide as a permitted food additive under the code E290.

What this is not. This is not the CO₂ shortage story some readers may be thinking of. The well-documented CO₂ shortage of 2020 hit ethanol plants specifically in the United States, where roughly three-quarters of the CO₂-selling ethanol plants tracked by Reuters had closed or cut output as pandemic lockdowns collapsed fuel demand — a US, COVID-era, fuel-demand story, not an Indian one. India's own well-known 2021 supply crisis was in medical oxygen, a different gas entirely, sourced and distributed through a completely different industrial-gas chain than fermentation CO₂. Neither event is evidence of an Indian food-grade CO₂ shortage today, and this piece found none.

What that CO₂ is actually good for once it's food-grade

One real use for food-grade CO₂ is as a solvent, not just a gas. Above its critical point — 31.1°C and roughly 73.8 bar — carbon dioxide becomes a supercritical fluid: dense like a liquid, but able to diffuse through solids like a gas, and tunable by pressure to pick up different classes of compound. Peer-reviewed extraction research on spices reports pulling maximum curcuminoid yield from turmeric at around 300 bar and 45°C, and maximum oleoresin-plus-carotenoid yield from red pepper and capsicum at pressures reported anywhere from roughly 200 to 500 bar depending on the study, cultivar and target compound — there is no single universal number, only a family of process conditions tuned to what's being extracted. What makes supercritical CO₂ attractive against the older, more established alternative — hexane or other organic-solvent extraction — is well documented in the academic literature independent of any equipment vendor's marketing: it avoids the toxic-solvent-residue removal step entirely, it runs at low enough temperatures to protect heat-sensitive aroma and colour compounds from degrading, and depressurising the finished extract back to a gas leaves nothing but the oleoresin itself, with no evaporation step for a hazardous solvent.

India already has a real, sizeable industry built on exactly this: Synthite Industries, based in Kochi, describes itself as the world's largest producer of natural spice extracts and a pioneer of supercritical-CO₂ spice extraction in India since 2007; Akay Group, Plant Lipids and Ozone Naturals are other named Indian producers in the same space, with equipment supplied by specialist vendors including Buffalo Extraction Systems (Pune) and Mech-O-Tech LLP (Hyderabad). India's spice-extract market is estimated at roughly $169.7 million in 2025, projected to reach $347.8 million by 2033 — and India is commonly cited in industry market reports as producing the majority of the world's traded spice oleoresins, though this piece did not find that specific majority-share figure independently confirmed by a government or trade-body source rather than a market-research estimate.

Two real industries, and no documented deal between them

Here is the structural tension this piece actually set out to find: India's ethanol sector, in 2025 reporting, hit its 20 per cent ethanol-blending target roughly five years ahead of its original 2030 schedule — but is also reported, in industry coverage, to be sitting on close to seven billion litres of installed capacity against current blending demand, with distilleries running at only around 60 per cent utilisation. If that figure holds, it implies more spare fermentation capacity, and more unsold byproduct CO₂, than the sector currently has a buyer for — the opposite of a shortage, and exactly the condition a "monetise your CO₂" pitch to distillers is built around. Meanwhile India's CO₂-extraction spice industry is real, growing, and by definition needs a steady food-grade CO₂ supply to run.

What this piece could not find, after specifically searching for it, is a single named transaction: no ethanol distillery, sugar mill or grain-based plant documented as selling its recovered CO₂ to a named oleoresin or spice-extract manufacturer, and no oleoresin producer named as sourcing its process CO₂ from a specific Indian ethanol plant. Both industries are well documented on their own. The link between them, in the specific form of a disclosed supply relationship, is not. That doesn't mean no such deal exists — merchant industrial-gas distributors sit between many gas producers and end users and wouldn't necessarily be named in either industry's own reporting — but it means this piece is presenting the connection as a plausible, currently undocumented opportunity, not an established fact, and readers should treat it exactly that way.

The one-line version. India has a byproduct in apparent oversupply and an industry that needs precisely that byproduct, both real and both sizeable — and, as far as this piece could establish, no one has put a name and a number on the deal that would connect them.

What would fix this

  • FSSAI or the Spices Board of India could publish sourcing data on where India's supercritical-CO₂ extraction plants actually obtain their process gas — recovered from a domestic distillery, imported, or bought from a merchant gas supplier with an undisclosed upstream source — so this specific linkage becomes traceable rather than something a researcher has to infer from two unconnected industry pages.
  • Ethanol distilleries reporting spare capacity have a direct monetisation incentive to actively market recovered CO₂ to nearby extraction and food-processing plants, rather than assuming buyers will find them; Praj's and India Glycols' own "monetise your CO₂" framing suggests the supply side already sees this as underexploited.
  • A public byproduct-CO₂ registry, on the model of the Fertiliser Ministry's own bulk-sale gazette notifications for CBG-plant digestate — which this blog has covered separately — would make cross-industry byproduct flows like this one visible in the public record instead of requiring a researcher to stitch together vendor pages and trade press.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Sources and caveats

The fermentation stoichiometry (glucose to ethanol plus CO₂, ~0.95 kg CO₂ per kg ethanol) is basic, independently verifiable chemistry. The 200 KLPD-to-98 TPD CO₂ conversion is a commonly cited industry rule-of-thumb from pre-feasibility and environmental-clearance-style filings for Indian ethanol plants, not one single named primary document. Praj Industries' and India Glycols Limited's CO₂ purification product descriptions are the companies' own stated claims (Praj.net's "Bio Co2" page; India Glycols' own product material), corroborating but not independently verified beyond what each company states about its own technology; both are named, publicly known engineering/chemical companies rather than pure marketing sites. The ISBT beverage-grade CO₂ standard (99.9% purity, ≤30ppm oxygen) is drawn from Food Safety Magazine's description of the ISBT Carbon Dioxide Guidelines, an independent technical standard. FSSAI's E290 food-additive designation for carbon dioxide is stated per general regulatory reference; this piece did not pull the specific FSSAI clause document directly and recommends direct verification against fssai.gov.in before any regulatory citation. CO₂'s critical point (31.1°C, ~73.8 bar) is standard physical chemistry, cross-checked against academic and reference sources independent of any vendor. The specific supercritical-extraction pressure/temperature figures for turmeric and capsicum/red pepper are drawn from peer-reviewed academic extraction studies (indexed via ScienceDirect), which vary by study and should be read as a range rather than one settled industry constant; readers should note that a range this wide reflects genuinely different studies, cultivars and target compounds, not an error. Synthite Industries, Akay Group, Plant Lipids and Ozone Naturals are named per their own corporate self-description as Indian supercritical-CO₂ spice-extraction producers; Buffalo Extraction Systems and Mech-O-Tech LLP are named equipment vendors per their own websites, and specific process claims attributed to either (such as claimed bioactive-compound retention percentages) are vendor marketing claims, not independently tested figures, and are flagged as such rather than stated as fact. The reasons CO₂ extraction is preferred over solvent extraction (no toxic residue, low-temperature processing, clean depressurisation) reflect general academic consensus in peer-reviewed extraction literature, not any single vendor's claim; this piece explicitly did not find evidence that CO₂ extraction has overtaken solvent extraction as the dominant method by volume, in India or globally, and does not claim that it has. India's spice-extract market size ($169.7 million 2025, projected $347.8 million by 2033) is a market-research-firm estimate, not a government-audited figure. The ~7-billion-litre ethanol overcapacity and ~60% distillery utilisation figures are drawn from a single industry-news source (Whalesbook) and should be treated as indicative rather than confirmed against a primary government statistic; this piece did not find corroboration from a second independent source. Most importantly: this piece's central structural claim — that ethanol-plant CO₂ recovery and India's oleoresin-extraction industry represent an unrealised synergy — is this piece's own inference from two independently well-documented but separately-sourced industries, not a documented fact; no named transaction, supply contract, or company statement connecting a specific ethanol plant's CO₂ to a specific oleoresin extractor's feedstock was found, despite a specific search for one, and readers should not treat the connection as established.
