India's Constitution has set its own deadline. Articles 82 and 170 freeze the allocation of Lok Sabha and Assembly seats only "until the relevant figures for the first census taken after the year 2026" are published. Census 2027 ends that freeze whether or not Parliament has agreed on what replaces it. So far it hasn't. In April 2026 the government's attempt failed in the Lok Sabha, 54 votes short.

This post separates the reforms that independent sources keep flagging (statutory commissions, Law Commission reports, Supreme Court benches) from the ones that are merely talked about. On that test they fall into three groups. One is forced by the calendar. Two are in committee. The rest have been recommended for decades and never drafted.

Updated 7 November 2026. Three open points are now settled. The One Nation One Election committee has not reported: on 29 July 2026 the Lok Sabha extended its deadline to the last week of the 2026 Winter Session (Deccan Herald). The challenge to the 2023 Election Commission appointments law produced a split verdict on 23 September 2026 on whether a larger bench should hear it, and the matter went to the Chief Justice; there is no ruling on the Act itself, and a recall plea is pending (Vision IAS summary of The Indian Express; LiveLaw). The Lok Sabha Deputy Speaker post is still vacant; opposition MPs raised it again in March 2026 (India Today NE). The text and scorecard have been updated.

1. Forced by the calendar: delimitation and women's reservation

Delimitation and women's reservation are now one problem. If seats follow population, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan gain heavily, while Tamil Nadu and Kerala stand still or lose. States are effectively penalised for controlling population growth, which was the stated reason for the 1976 freeze and its 2001 extension (84th Amendment). Meanwhile the 106th Amendment (Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, 2023) reserves one-third of seats for women, but only after the next delimitation.

The Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026 tried to settle both. It would have enlarged the Lok Sabha from 543 to 850 seats and brought women's reservation forward using 2011 Census data. It was defeated on 17 April 2026 by 298 to 230, against the 352 it needed. Southern and north-eastern parties objected that using 2011 data would cut their share. The government then withdrew the companion Delimitation Bill.

Where that leaves things. India now has a seat allocation that unfreezes after Census 2027, no agreed formula, and women's reservation that some estimates push to around 2034. Every exit needs an amendment: extend the freeze again, raise the cap on House size, or adopt a formula that limits the shift, such as degressive proportionality (where smaller states get proportionally more seats per person) or a rebalanced Rajya Sabha. Doing nothing is also a choice, because without an amendment the freeze simply lapses.

2. Bills already in committee

One Nation One Election, the Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill, 2024. It follows the Kovind Committee (2024), the Law Commission's 170th report (1999) and the 22nd Law Commission's work on holding Lok Sabha and Assembly elections together. It was introduced in December 2024 by 269 votes to 196 and sent to a Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC), which was still taking evidence from legal experts in 2025. Critics point to two issues: the bill cuts short the terms of state assemblies, and it creates a "mid-term" assembly that sits only for the remainder of a dissolved one's term. It had not reported by November 2026: on 29 July 2026 the Lok Sabha extended its deadline again, to the first day of the last week of the 2026 Winter Session.

Removing a jailed PM, CM or minister, the Constitution (130th Amendment) Bill, 2025. Under this bill, a minister held in custody for 30 consecutive days on a serious charge would cease to hold office. In July 2026 the JPC deferred adopting its draft report. Leaked accounts, not an official text, say the draft softens "removal" to "suspension" and adds automatic reversal and fast-track courts. On 31 July 2026 the Lok Sabha extended the deadline to the last week of the Winter Session. Most INDIA bloc members are boycotting the committee, and the NDA does not have two-thirds in either House.

3. Recommended for decades, never drafted

Governors. The Sarkaria Commission (1988), the National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC, 2002) and the Punchhi Commission (2010) all asked for time limits on assent to bills, security of tenure and a consultative appointment process. In April 2025 the Supreme Court set timelines in the Tamil Nadu case. Its Presidential Reference opinion of 20 November 2025 reversed that course: no judicially imposed timelines, no "deemed assent", and only a narrow opening for "prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction". That puts the fix squarely with Parliament. A fixed timeline under Article 200 now needs a constitutional amendment.

The anti-defection law (Tenth Schedule). The Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990), the Law Commission's 170th report and NCRWC all recommended taking disqualification decisions away from the Speaker. The Supreme Court suggested an independent tribunal in Keisham Meghachandra (2020). Later cases, including Maharashtra in 2022–23, showed Speakers sitting on petitions for months. The fixes that keep recurring are an independent adjudicator, a time limit on deciding petitions, and limiting the whip to confidence and money votes.

Article 356 (President's Rule). S.R. Bommai (1994) curbed abuse through the courts. Sarkaria and Punchhi asked for the safeguards to be written into the Constitution: a floor test first, and the reasons placed before Parliament. They have not been.

Judicial appointments. The Supreme Court struck down the National Judicial Appointments Commission in 2015 while conceding the collegium needs reform. A decade later the Memorandum of Procedure, the government–judiciary agreement on how appointments are made, is still unsettled. NCRWC proposed a National Judicial Commission. Everyone agrees on more transparency. The disagreement is over who has the final say.

Election Commission appointments. In Anoop Baranwal (2023) the Supreme Court put the Chief Justice on the selection panel until Parliament legislated. The Chief Election Commissioner Act, 2023 then replaced the Chief Justice with a Union minister, and that Act is under challenge. The Goswami Committee, the Law Commission's 255th report (2015) and NCRWC each asked for a bipartisan appointment process protected in the Constitution, which means amending Article 324.

The Deputy Speaker. Article 93 says the Lok Sabha "shall" choose a Deputy Speaker. The post has been vacant since June 2019, and opposition MPs were still raising the vacancy in March 2026. A deadline or an enforcement mechanism would also need to be written in.
