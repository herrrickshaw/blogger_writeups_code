Four blending mandates, one country, and only one chemistry problem. Ethanol in petrol and isobutanol in diesel both carry less energy than the fuel they're cut into, so every litre goes a little less far. CBG in CNG and SAF in ATF are close to true drop-ins — the same energy, delivered the same way — so their cost shows up as a price premium on the input, not fewer kilometres per rupee. The blend chemistry decides who pays and how; the mandate percentage is almost beside the point.

Blending on a Budget: Energy Density and Cost Per Kilometre, Across Four Fuel Pairs

The pattern in one line: ethanol carries 66% of petrol's energy per litre and isobutanol carries 74% of diesel's, so both mandates cost the driver mileage. CBG is chemically almost identical to the fossil gas it joins, and SAF is a hydroprocessed molecule built to match jet fuel spec for spec — so neither costs a single kilometre; their cost instead shows up as a higher price for the blend component itself.

1. The four blends, at a glance

India now has one live nationwide fuel-blending mandate (E20 petrol), one live but narrower gas-blending mandate (the Compressed Biogas Blending Obligation), one proposed-but-not-yet-notified diesel mandate (isobutanol), and one long-horizon aviation mandate that starts small (Sustainable Aviation Fuel, from 2027). All four get described in headlines as "blending X% of a greener fuel in," as if the mechanics were the same each time. They aren't. What decides whether a mandate costs the end user mileage or money is a single physical fact: how close the blend component's energy density sits to the fuel it's replacing.

2. Which standards actually govern each fuel

"Blending mandate" is a policy percentage; the number that makes a blend legal to sell is a specification number, set by a named standards body. India's own Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) covers the domestic road fuels; the two international bodies that matter most for the aviation pair are ASTM International, whose specifications set the compositional bar SAF has to clear, and the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), whose CORSIA framework sets the policy timeline SAF blending is scaling against, not a fuel-composition spec. The American Petroleum Institute (API) is not a fuel-composition regulator for any of the four pairs here — its relevant standards (API 1543, API 1595) govern aviation-fuel storage, handling and quality-monitoring infrastructure, not what's chemically allowed in the tank.

3. Ethanol in petrol — the fuel that actually loses mileage

This is the mandate that costs drivers real, measured money, because it's the only one where the blend molecule is a genuinely different, lower-energy fuel from the one it joins. Petrol carries about 32.4 MJ per litre; pure ethanol carries about 21.3 MJ per litre — roughly two-thirds of petrol's energy, a figure that shows up identically in the NITI Aayog Roadmap for Ethanol Blending and in the US Department of Energy's own fuel-property tables. Blending is linear, so E20 (20% ethanol, 80% petrol) works out to about 30.2 MJ/L, a theoretical ceiling of roughly 6.9% less energy per litre than pure petrol.

Real-world mileage loss sits at or below that ceiling. ARAI's controlled testing found a 2–6% efficiency reduction on E20; SIAM cites 2–4%. Newer, E20-compliant vehicles (roughly 2023 onward) sit at the low end because their engines are tuned to exploit ethanol's higher octane and oxygen content and claw back most of the energy-density gap; older, non-compliant vehicles can lose the full theoretical amount, or slightly more in real-world driving. At a representative 4% drop, the extra fuel cost works out to about ₹0.27–0.33 per kilometre for a hatchback or compact SUV, or roughly ₹3,900–4,900 a year for an average driver on a Delhi fuel-price basis — a small but real, permanent tax on every petrol kilometre driven, paid in mileage rather than at the pump, since E20 sells at the same price as the petrol it replaced.

4. CBG in CNG — the blend nobody notices, because it isn't really a blend

Compressed Bio-Gas is not chemically a different fuel dosed into CNG the way ethanol is dosed into petrol — it is, once purified, close to the same fuel. Both fossil CNG and CBG are overwhelmingly methane; CBG is defined under India's Bureau of Indian Standards specification (IS 16087:2016) as biogas upgraded to at least 90% methane purity, with the balance mostly inert CO₂ and moisture rather than a lower-energy fuel component. Published energy-content ranges put fossil CNG at roughly 50–56 MJ/kg and purified CBG at roughly 47–52 MJ/kg — a gap driven almost entirely by CBG's slightly lower share of heavier hydrocarbons and any residual inert gas, not by methane itself carrying less energy than methane.

The Compressed Biogas Blending Obligation (CBO), approved by the National Biofuels Coordination Committee, mandates CBG blending into the CNG (transport) and PNG (domestic) streams of city gas distribution starting at 1% of consumption in FY2025-26, rising to 3% in FY2026-27, 4% in FY2027-28 and 5% from FY2028-29 onward. Because CBG is metered into the same pipeline network and sold at the same per-kilogram CNG price, with no separate CBG retail rate, there is no cost-per-kilometre penalty for the consumer to detect — the running-cost figures already published for CNG vehicles (roughly ₹2.97–3.78/km for a Delhi hatchback or compact SUV) already implicitly include whatever share of CBG is in the pipeline blend, indistinguishably.

Ethanol and isobutanol are alcohols asked to stand in for hydrocarbons that hold more energy than they do. CBG is methane asked to stand in for other methane. That difference in kind — not the blend percentage — is why one mandate shows up as a permanent mileage tax and the other doesn't show up at all.
5. Isobutanol in diesel — not law yet, and a gentler penalty when it arrives

Unlike E20, isobutanol-diesel blending is not currently in force in India. Road Transport and Highways Minister Nitin Gadkari announced in June 2026 — at the launch of Maruti Suzuki's first flex-fuel passenger vehicle — that the government is working toward blending isobutanol into diesel at up to 15%, targeted from 2027, after acknowledging that an earlier attempt to blend ethanol directly with diesel had not produced the desired results. The Ministry's own secretary has since said a formal blending mandate is "quite likely" before the end of 2026, but as of this writing no notification, blend percentage or timeline has been formally issued — everything below is a projection built on the same energy-density method used for ethanol, not a measured, live cost.

The chemistry is more forgiving than ethanol's. Isobutanol carries about 26.6 MJ per litre against diesel's 35.8 MJ/L — roughly 74% of diesel's energy density, well above ethanol's 66% of petrol's, because a four-carbon alcohol simply holds more energy per litre than a two-carbon one. Blended linearly at the mandate's stated ceiling of 15%, that implies a theoretical mileage-loss ceiling of about 3.9% — nearly half of E20's 6.9% ceiling despite isobutanol displacing a deeper share of the base fuel. Early trials reported in the trade press, at a much shallower pilot-scale blend of 2–5%, put the real-world mileage loss at just 1–2%. Translated to cost on a Delhi diesel basis (₹4.33–5.01/km for a sedan or compact SUV today), that pilot-scale loss works out to roughly ₹0.04–0.09 extra per kilometre, or about ₹650–1,300 a year for an average driver — smaller than the ethanol penalty, and smaller still than what the 15% ceiling would eventually cost if the full mandate lands as announced.

6. SAF in ATF — energy parity, priced differently

Sustainable Aviation Fuel breaks the pattern entirely, because it isn't blended in the way ethanol or isobutanol are — it is manufactured, via hydroprocessing routes such as HEFA (from fats and oils) or Alcohol-to-Jet (from ethanol, the pathway India is best positioned to scale given its ethanol surplus), to meet the same ASTM D7566 specification that, once blended, must in turn meet the finished-fuel spec for conventional Jet A-1, ASTM D1655. The result is closer to a manufactured substitute than a blend component: published comparisons put ATJ-pathway SAF at roughly 44.0 MJ/kg against Jet A-1's 43.3 MJ/kg — SAF carries marginally more energy per kilogram, not less, though it can run marginally lower on a per-litre basis because SAF blendstocks are typically less dense than conventional jet fuel. Either way, the difference is small enough that SAF blending carries no meaningful range or mileage penalty for an aircraft, which is the entire point of designing it as a drop-in.

India's SAF mandate starts at 1% blending from 2027, rising to 2% by 2028 and 5% by 2030, aligned with ICAO's CORSIA Phase II timeline. Because there's no efficiency penalty to translate into a cost-per-kilometre figure, the economics show up somewhere else entirely: price. SAF traded globally in 2026 at roughly $1,817 per tonne, on the order of five times a conventional jet-fuel benchmark in tight markets, though India-specific projections for power-and-biomass-to-liquids (PBtL) SAF put the cost closer to $1.4 per litre today, falling toward $1.0/L by 2049, against a conventional ATF benchmark of roughly $0.6–0.8/L — a narrower, though still real, two-to-two-and-a-half-times premium rather than the five-times figure sometimes quoted for tighter European HEFA markets. Cost-per-kilometre simply isn't the right lens for this pair: airlines buy fuel by the tonne and price it into fares, not into a driver's fuel-gauge arithmetic, and at a 1% blend share the fare-level effect of even a five-times SAF premium is a rounding error against total fuel cost.

7. Why the mandate percentage is the wrong number to watch

Line the four pairs up and a single variable explains almost everything: how far the blend molecule's energy density sits from the fuel it displaces. Ethanol sits 34 percentage points below petrol and the driver pays for every one of those points, permanently, in mileage. Isobutanol sits 26 points below diesel and pays a proportionally smaller mileage bill. CBG sits within a few points of CNG, because it's chemically the same gas purified to spec rather than a different fuel altogether, so there's no bill to pay in mileage at all. SAF sits at parity or better by mass, so its cost shows up as a straightforward price premium on the input rather than a hidden tax on the output. None of this tracks the headline blend percentage — CBG's mandate tops out lower than SAF's, yet CBG is the more complete drop-in of the two; isobutanol's proposed 15% ceiling is far deeper than E20's 20% actual blend suggests it should be gentler, yet it is, because the chemistry, not the percentage, sets the price.

What this piece does not establish. The isobutanol-diesel mandate is proposed, not notified; this piece's cost figures for it are a projection built on the same energy-density method used for E20, not a measured running cost, and the actual blend percentage, timeline and real-world mileage impact could all still change or be reported differently once a formal notification exists. No Bureau of Indian Standards fuel specification for isobutanol-diesel blends was found in the sourcing available to this piece, unlike the BIS specs already in force for E20 and CBG — if one exists, it was not located. SAF pricing is volatile and quoted across very different bases (per-tonne spot, per-litre pathway-specific projections, and blended-fuel benchmarks); the figures here should be read as an order-of-magnitude premium, not a precise, current India price. CBG's energy-density range is a published band, not a single certified figure, and the exact ratio to fossil CNG will vary by feedstock and plant.
