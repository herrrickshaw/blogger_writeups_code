Coal gasification can, in principle, make almost anything a refinery or gas plant can: ammonia and urea, methanol and its whole derivative family (DME, acetic acid, formaldehyde), monoethylene glycol, olefins, synthetic natural gas, even diesel. What it can't yet do, on the industry's own admission, is make most of that cheaper than simply importing it. This piece maps the full product tree coal gasification can reach, then sorts it — using India's actual import dependence, current global prices, and each product's coal intensity — by which ones come closest to paying for themselves at today's coal price, and which ones are, for now, a policy bet rather than an economic one.

What syngas can actually become

Gasifying coal — partial oxidation under heat and pressure, rather than combustion — produces synthesis gas (syngas): a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen that is chemistry's most versatile building block. From that single starting point, three broad families of products branch out:

Direct syngas products: hydrogen (separated out on its own), ammonia (syngas nitrogen-shifted and run through Haber-Bosch, then usually onward to urea and other fertilisers), synthetic natural gas (SNG, via methanation), and Fischer-Tropsch liquids (diesel, naphtha, waxes, built up directly from CO and H₂).

Methanol and its derivative tree: methanol itself (syngas run through a copper-catalyst synthesis loop) is the single largest coal-gasification product globally and the hub for a further family of derivatives — dimethyl ether (DME, a diesel/LPG substitute), acetic acid (via methanol carbonylation), formaldehyde, and, through the Methanol-to-Olefins (MTO) route, ethylene and propylene.

Oxygenates and other syngas-route chemicals: monoethylene glycol (MEG, feeding the polyester and PET chain), oxo-alcohols (via hydroformylation, feeding plasticiser and solvent chemistry), acetic anhydride, and ammonium nitrate (an ammonia derivative used as an industrial explosive and, in India specifically, the subject of a dedicated coal-gasification joint venture, discussed below).

The finding that has to come before any ranking: it mostly doesn't pay for itself yet

Before sorting these products by relative viability, the honest starting point is that none of them clear a straightforward cost test today. The US-India Strategic Partnership Forum's own analysis of India's coal-gasification opportunity states it plainly: "at present prices of natural gas and imported products, the coal gasification route is not feasible" without some form of fiscal support or import protection. That is not a critics' assessment — it is the conclusion of a report making the broader case for India's coal-gasification push. The ranking that follows should be read in that light: as a comparison of which products come closest to viable, and which the government has already put real capital behind, not a claim that any of them beat imports on price alone right now.

India's extra handicap: coal that's cheap but hard to gasify

India's coal is genuinely inexpensive by global standards — representative e-auction prices for non-coking coal have run around ₹1,800–1,900 a tonne for mid-grade coal in early-to-mid 2026, a fraction of the roughly ₹9,000–11,600 a tonne landed cost of imported sub-bituminous or thermal coal. But that headline price understates what a gasification plant actually needs to pay, because Indian coal carries a structural handicap that coal-to-chemicals programmes in China rarely had to solve at the same scale: ash content running up to 40%, against washing that "is not economic and feasible" below roughly 30% ash for some coal types even after processing, per government-directed washing compliance that itself remains low. The practical workarounds — fluidised-bed gasifiers designed to lift coal out of its own ash before combustion, and blending high-ash coal with lignite or petroleum coke — are described as viable, mature technology (Air Products, Siemens, and other established gasifier vendors are cited as capable), but they add real capital and operating cost on top of the raw coal price, a cost this piece could not find a single, India-specific rupees-per-tonne figure for.

Reading the table: the government's money already agrees with the coal-intensity math

Ammonia/urea and synthetic natural gas are where India has actually committed capital — the Talcher Fertilisers revival, the Coal India–BHEL ammonium-nitrate joint venture (Bharat Coal Gasification & Chemicals Limited, building at Lakhanpur, Odisha), and the Coal India–GAIL SNG joint venture. These are also, not coincidentally, among the least coal-intensive products on the list, the ones with the clearest Chinese commercial precedent at scale (75% of China's ammonia/urea runs on coal), and in ammonia/urea's case, the ones the government has explicitly prioritised in its own gas-allocation policy — directing scarce natural gas to fertiliser producers ahead of methanol producers, which by extension makes the case for a coal-based methanol alternative stronger even as gas-based methanol capacity is left underserved.

BCGCL's Lakhanpur project, specifically, is the furthest along of any coal-to-chemicals project in the country, and its own disclosures are a useful real-world check on the coal-intensity math used elsewhere in this piece. The joint venture (Coal India 51%, BHEL 49%) signed its formal JVA on 28 February 2024; Mahanadi Coalfields Limited (MCL) signed a land-leasing agreement on 1 April 2026 for roughly 350 acres of coal-bearing land at Lakhanpur, Jharsuguda district, Odisha; and President Droupadi Murmu and Prime Minister Narendra Modi remotely laid the project's foundation stone on 20 June 2026. The plant is designed to produce 0.66 million tonnes a year (2,000 tonnes a day) of technical-grade ammonium nitrate using indigenously developed Pressurised Fluidised Bed Gasification (PFBG) technology, fed by 0.79 million tonnes a year of washed coal plus 1.19 million tonnes a year of reject coal from MCL's Ib Valley washery — a combined ~1.98 million tonnes of coal input against 0.66 million tonnes of product, or roughly 3.0 tonnes of coal per tonne of ammonium nitrate, the one figure in this piece drawn from an actual India-specific, high-ash-coal project rather than China-derived literature. All four Lump Sum Turnkey (LSTK) execution packages have been awarded — two to BHEL, two to Larsen & Toubro — and the project has received Single Window Clearance from Odisha's High-Level Clearance Authority plus a ₹1,350 crore financial incentive from the Ministry of Coal. Its estimated cost has also grown substantially since the 2024 JVA: from ₹11,782 crore at signing to a precise ₹25,016 crore in the PM's Office's own pre-event release, a more-than-doubling this piece treats as a documented cost escalation rather than an error in its earlier reporting. Target commissioning is September 2029. One detail meaningfully de-risks the project's demand side, and this piece did not have it in its first pass: Coal India itself has committed to buying the entire ammonium-nitrate output, for use as mining explosive in its own coal operations — a captive offtake arrangement, not a sale into the open commodity market this piece's viability framework otherwise assumes for every other product on this list.

Methanol sits in an interesting middle position: it has by far the largest import-dependence of anything on this list (over 90%, versus ammonia's roughly 13%), meaning the addressable substitution opportunity is large in absolute terms, and China's own 70%-coal-based methanol industry proves the chemistry works at scale. But it hasn't attracted the same scale of dedicated Indian coal-gasification capital that ammonia/urea and SNG have — JSL's 8 MTPA of syngas capacity is a byproduct of a steel-sector gasification project rather than a purpose-built coal-to-methanol plant, and this piece did not find an equivalent dedicated methanol joint venture in the National Coal Gasification Mission's published project list.

MEG and olefins were, in this piece's first pass, the two products with no funded Indian project behind them at all — and are the two most coal-intensive products on the list (3–3.5 and 6.2 tonnes of coal per tonne of output, respectively), the ones China's own industry describes as "less mature" or "facing economic challenges" even after years of buildout. That "no project" reading for MEG specifically no longer holds, as the next section describes — a private developer, not a PSU, turned out to be the one moving on it.

BCGCL is one of several named projects PIB has disclosed, and the fuller roster is worth listing because it shows the government's revealed priorities more completely than any one project can. Beyond BCGCL/Lakhanpur (ammonium nitrate, Odisha), the Coal India–GAIL synthetic-natural-gas joint venture — formally incorporated as Coal Gas India Limited (CGIL), CIL 51%/GAIL 49% — has a name and a number this piece did not have before: it is sited at Sonepur Bazari, in ECL's Raniganj coalfield area, Burdwan district, West Bengal, targeting 1.83 million standard cubic metres a day (80,000 Nm³/hour, 633.6 million Nm³ a year) of SNG at a cost of ₹13,052.81 crore (CIL's own equity share, ₹1,997.08 crore at a 70:30 debt-equity ratio), also targeted for FY29 commissioning at the time its JVA was signed on 5 August 2024.

Checking this project's actual progress against BCGCL's, specifically, is revealing. CGIL/Sonepur Bazari received the same ₹1,350 crore Category-I financial incentive as BCGCL, in the same 12 December 2024 batch of Letters of Award. But that is where the visible progress converges: as of the most recent public disclosures this piece could find, ECL had acquired roughly 190 of the 250 acres of tenancy land the project needs (about 76%), a technology-licensor selection process was still open as of an October 2024 bid deadline, and the detailed feasibility report — also assigned to PDIL, the same consultant used for BCGCL — was described as still "in progress" at JVA signing. This piece found no foundation-stone ceremony, no confirmed LSTK or EPC contract awards, and no updated project cost or commissioning date for CGIL comparable to what BCGCL received in April and June 2026. That matters because BCGCL's own commissioning target has itself already slipped from the "FY29" both projects shared in 2024 to the more specific "September 2029" cited in the government's own June 2026 release — a schedule that has moved backward even for the more visibly advanced of the two projects. Absent an equivalent public update, this piece cannot establish whether CGIL is still tracking its original FY29 target, has slipped further, or has simply not been the subject of a recent government announcement; it treats the absence of an update as itself informative, not as evidence either way. A second, separate ammonium-nitrate initiative, named "Utkarsh," is nominally under way at Western Coalfields Limited (WCL) — and checking its progress turned up a genuinely different commercial structure and a materially earlier stage than either BCGCL or CGIL. Utkarsh is not a PSU-to-PSU joint venture at all: WCL has allocated roughly 350 acres at its Juna Kunada OC Mine, Majri Area, Chandrapur district, Maharashtra, on a lease-rent basis, and is seeking a private developer to build, own and operate the plant (a BOO structure) under a 2022 Expression of Interest, converting raw coal into "ammonium nitrate-melt." The only capacity figure this piece found for it — 0.66 MTPA, matching BCGCL's — comes from a single, brief PIB mention with no accompanying cost, contractor, or land-possession update since. Separately, and confusingly sharing the same Majri Area, Coal India and Bharat Petroleum Corporation Limited signed a non-binding memorandum of understanding on 2 December 2024 for a different product entirely — a ₹12,214 crore, 633.6 million Nm³/year synthetic natural gas project, described in trade coverage as CIL's third gasification partnership (after BHEL and GAIL) and, as of the same month, only as far along as an in-principle Pre-Feasibility Report approval from the CIL Board — a stage behind even CGIL's signed JVA. A near-identically costed SNG project (₹12,214.86 crore, also 1.83 MMSCMD) received a ₹1,350 crore Category-I incentive in the same December 2024 LOA batch as BCGCL and CGIL, described there as being at "Niljai Ext. OC, Wani Area" rather than Majri; this piece cannot establish with confidence whether that is the same BPCL project under a different area name or a genuinely separate one, and flags the inconsistency rather than resolving it. Taken together, ammonium nitrate is still the product with the most named Indian projects behind it, but Utkarsh specifically is the least institutionally advanced of the three ammonium-nitrate/SNG projects this piece has now checked — a private-developer tender with allocated land and one cited capacity figure, rather than an incorporated joint venture with a signed JVA, an awarded LOA, or any confirmed capital cost. A prospective Coal India–SAIL collaboration at Durgapur, West Bengal, targeting syngas as a DRI-grade reducing gas for steelmaking, is the earliest-stage of every named project this piece has now checked: it has not been incorporated as a joint venture at all (unlike BCGCL and CGIL, both named legal entities), carries no disclosed investment figure, no disclosed CIL equity stake, and independent trade analysis describes it as remaining "at an early feasibility and planning stage" without formal project sanction. It nonetheless matches an item on the Coal Minister's own product list cited earlier in this piece, and it is not the only coal-derived-syngas-for-steel initiative in motion: on 12 August 2026 — only weeks before this update — Coal India signed a separate, also non-binding memorandum of understanding with ArcelorMittal Nippon Steel (AMNS) to explore a coal-gasification-with-carbon-capture facility adjacent to AMNS's Paradip pellet plant in Odisha, aimed at supplying syngas for steelmaking there too. That MoU's own text commits only to preparing a Preliminary Feasibility Report and discloses no capacity, cost, coal-sourcing plan, technology choice, timeline, or which CIL subsidiary would execute it — meaning India currently has two separate, uncosted, unincorporated syngas-for-steel explorations (Durgapur and Paradip) running in parallel, neither of which has reached the JVA stage the ammonium-nitrate and SNG projects passed years ago. NLC India's lignite-to-methanol project (syngas, diesel, and green hydrogen alongside methanol, from lignite rather than coal specifically) is the one project this piece found that does target methanol directly, which corrects this piece's earlier framing that no dedicated Indian methanol project existed. Checking further on the ethanol demonstration project first identified via PIB's December 2024 Letters-of-Award release turned up something considerably larger: New Era Cleantech Solutions, the private company behind that ₹667 crore, 18 KTPA coal-to-ethanol demonstration unit (capturing roughly 20,144 tonnes of CO₂ a year via CCUS), is also building what it describes as India's first greenfield Integrated Coal Gasification and Carbon Capture Complex at Bhadravati, in the same Chandrapur district. That complex is a different order of scale entirely: roughly 1,650 acres, a total investment of ₹20,000 crore across two phases, environmental clearance already obtained, a captive coal mine already allocated, and a design throughput exceeding 5 million tonnes of coal a year once fully operational — larger than any single PSU-led project profiled in this piece. Phase 1 targets ammonia, ammonium nitrate, and monoethylene glycol (MEG); Phase 2 adds DME, urea, sustainable aviation fuel, and ethanol. Maharashtra Chief Minister Devendra Fadnavis performed the Bhoomi Pujan (ground-breaking) ceremony on 15 March 2026, attended by Union Coal Minister G. Kishan Reddy — three months before BCGCL's own foundation-stone ceremony, making New Era Cleantech's complex the earliest of any project in this piece to reach visible on-ground construction. A separate PIB-disclosed Category-II incentive of ₹1,000 crore, against a ₹6,976 crore project cost, for New Era Cleantech ammonium-nitrate and hydrogen capacity (0.33 MMTPA and 0.1 MMTPA respectively) at the same Bhadravati site is, on the balance of the evidence — same company, same location — almost certainly a financing component of this same larger complex rather than an unrelated fourth project, though this piece did not find a single source explicitly confirming that the ethanol demonstration unit, the ammonium-nitrate/hydrogen award, and the ₹20,000 crore complex are formally one integrated project rather than co-located separate ones. Whichever way that resolves, this correction matters for the sorting table above: MEG is no longer a product with no funded Indian project — New Era Cleantech's Phase 1 targets it directly, alongside ammonia and ammonium nitrate, making this the first private-sector entrant on this piece's list to move faster on the ground than any of the PSU joint ventures it has been comparing it against. One clarification worth making explicit, to avoid a coincidence reading as a connection: New Era Cleantech's own ₹20,000 crore is that one company's total capital outlay across two phases of one complex, and is entirely separate from the similarly-sized, unrelated national CCUS Mission — a five-year, ₹20,000 crore Union Budget 2026-27 outlay covering CCUS technology deployment across Power, Steel, Cement, Refineries, and Chemicals nationally, reportedly nodal to the Ministry of Power rather than Ministry of Coal. This piece found no evidence that New Era Cleantech's project has received or applied for funding under that national scheme specifically; its CCUS component to date has been funded through the Ministry of Coal's own, separate ₹8,500 crore coal-gasification incentive scheme (the ₹100 crore ethanol-unit and ₹1,000 crore ammonium-nitrate/hydrogen awards discussed above). Readers wanting the fuller picture on the national CCUS Mission — including a striking contrast with India's own 2022 climate strategy document, which assessed CCUS as "not matured" and fit only for demonstration projects — can find it in this blog's separate piece on India's Net Zero scenarios. Separately from all of the above surface-gasification projects, the Ministry of Coal is also running a distinct, earlier-stage technology track: India's first Underground Coal Gasification (UCG) pilot, at the Kasta coal block in Jamtara district, Jharkhand, run by Eastern Coalfields Limited with CMPDI Ranchi and Canada's Ergo Exergy Technologies, gasifying coal in-situ rather than in a surface plant — a two-year, two-phase R&D project that began with borehole/feasibility work in June 2024 and has not, as far as this piece could establish, reached a commercial-scale product decision.

One further disclosure is worth including precisely because it cuts against this piece's own confident framing elsewhere: in a written Lok Sabha reply on 19 March 2025, the Ministry of Coal stated plainly that "the Government has not conducted any specific impact assessment of the financial incentive scheme for coal gasification projects." Every ranking and cost figure in this piece describes what the government has funded and disclosed, not an independently audited assessment of whether that funding is working — a distinction the government's own record keeps, and one this piece preserves rather than smooths over.

The scale the government is actually betting on

India's National Coal Gasification Mission targets 100 million tonnes of coal gasification capacity by 2030. As of the most recent reporting, roughly 22.6 million tonnes per annum is operational or under implementation — about 23% of the 2030 target — split across JSL's 8 MTPA, Talcher Fertilisers' 2.6 MTPA, and roughly 12 MTPA across eight projects approved under a dedicated ₹8,500 crore Scheme for Promotion of Coal/Lignite Gasification, sanctioned in January 2024 (7 of those 8 projects finalised, 3 with bhoomi pujan completed, as of April 2026). That ₹8,500 crore splits into three categories: ₹4,050 crore for government PSUs (up to three projects, each eligible for the lower of ₹1,350 crore or 15% of capex), ₹3,850 crore for private-sector and PSU projects (₹1,000 crore or 15% of capex per project), and ₹600 crore for demonstration and small-scale plants (₹100 crore or 15% of capex per project). This piece's earlier reporting flagged an unresolved discrepancy against a separately cited ₹37,500 crore figure; that discrepancy is now resolved by the government's own account: the ₹37,500 crore is a second, larger, separate Scheme for Promotion of Surface Coal/Lignite Gasification Projects, Cabinet-approved on 13 May 2026, targeting an additional 75 million tonnes of coal/lignite gasification (on top of the first scheme's contribution to the 100 MT goal), expected to mobilise ₹2.5–3.0 lakh crore in investment and create roughly 50,000 direct and indirect jobs across 25 projects. A PMO pre-event release ahead of the Lakhanpur foundation-stone ceremony puts the two schemes' combined cumulative outlay at up to ₹46,000 crore (₹8,500 crore plus ₹37,500 crore) — independent, primary-source confirmation that this piece's resolution of the earlier discrepancy is arithmetically correct, not just plausible. The government's own primary figure for the addressable import bill is considerably more precise than any industry estimate this piece found earlier: India's import bill for LNG, urea, ammonium nitrate, ammonia, coking coal, methanol, DME and related products stood at approximately ₹2.77 lakh crore in FY2025, per the Cabinet's own May 2026 press release — a figure this piece now treats as the authoritative baseline in place of the previously cited, weaker-sourced ₹60,000–90,000 crore industry estimate, which this piece no longer relies on.

What this piece does not establish. The coal-intensity ratios (tonnes of coal per tonne of product) used throughout this piece are drawn from general and China-specific coal-to-chemicals technoeconomic literature, not from India-specific plant data using India's actual high-ash coal — the real Indian ratios, once beneficiation and blending losses are accounted for, are likely higher than the ranges cited here, and this piece could not find a published India-specific figure to correct for that. This piece does not establish a verified, single production-cost-per-tonne figure for any product via India's coal-gasification route; the USISPF finding that the route is "not feasible" at present prices is one industry report's conclusion, not an independent cost audit this piece reproduced calculation-by-calculation. The ₹8,500 crore and ₹37,500 crore figures are now confirmed, via PIB, to be two separate, sequential schemes rather than one figure reported inconsistently — the earlier version of this piece flagged this as an unresolved discrepancy, which PIB's own record has since resolved. This piece does not independently verify BCGCL's escalation from ₹11,782 crore to over ₹25,000 crore beyond noting that both figures come from the project's own official disclosures at different dates; it does not speculate on the cause of the increase. The polyethylene price cited for olefins is a global reference price, not an India-specific CIF import figure, because this piece could not locate the latter with confidence. This piece does not establish that New Era Cleantech's ethanol demonstration unit, its ammonium-nitrate/hydrogen incentive award, and its ₹20,000 crore Bhadravati complex are formally one integrated project rather than three co-located but distinct developments — that connection is this piece's own inference from shared company and location, not a directly confirmed fact. This piece does not recommend any investment, business, or policy decision; nothing here is investment or financial advice.
