Do Cheap Stocks Stay Cheap? Price-Band Migration and What K-Means Clustering Actually Finds

Every piece in this price-band series so far has used a snapshot: where a stock's price sits today. This piece asks the question that snapshot can't answer — how much do stocks actually move between price bands over time, and does an algorithm that doesn't know about price bands at all, given only price, volatility, return and beta, group stocks any differently than the manual bands this blog has been drawing?

1. How much migration actually happens between price bands

Using the same 2,015-plus-stock local NSE price history as the rest of this series, this article tracked each stock's price band at the start of the window (January 2023) against its band at the end (August 2026), across the full 11-rung ladder used throughout this series — from ₹0–50 up to above ₹10,000:

Across the whole market, 27.1% of tracked stocks ended the window in a different price band than they started in — but that average hides a sharp split. The ₹0–50 band is the most immobile of all: only 7.7% of stocks that started there moved to a different band, and every one of those moves was upward — not a single stock that started under ₹50 ended the window in an even cheaper band, because there's nowhere cheaper to go. The bands from ₹100 to ₹5,000 show the most churn, with roughly half of every band's stocks changing bands at least once, and the ₹3,000–5,000 band showing the single highest migration rate (59.6%, though on a small base of 47 stocks). At the very top, the above-₹10,000 band is almost as immobile as the very bottom — just 2.1% moved at all, and every move was downward, which fits: a ₹1,32,700 stock like MRF has to fall a very long way before it crosses into a lower band.

2. What K-means clustering finds when it isn't told about price bands

The price bands used throughout this series are hand-drawn boundaries. To check whether the market actually organises itself along those same lines, this article ran K-means clustering (four clusters, standardised features) on 2015 stocks using four inputs: price (log-scaled), annualised return, annualised volatility, and beta — letting the algorithm decide the groupings with no knowledge of this series' own ₹250/₹1,000/₹10,000 boundaries.

The clustering agrees with this series' core finding rather than contradicting it — but it also sharpens the picture in a way manual price bands can't. Cluster 1 (median price ₹26, the algorithm's own "cheap and distressed" group) lines up closely with the ₹0–50 band from the companion piece on this blog. But look at the price ranges the clustering actually drew: Cluster 2 (the calmest, lowest-beta group) and Cluster 3 (the most volatile, highest-return group) have almost identical median prices — ₹537 and ₹562 — yet sit at opposite ends of the volatility and return spectrum. At a single price point around ₹550, the algorithm found both a nearly market-neutral, defensive stock and a high-beta momentum name equally likely. Price alone, even to an unsupervised algorithm given no band boundaries to work from, does not cleanly separate these two very different kinds of stock once you're above the very cheapest cluster.

3. Reconciling the two findings

Read together, the migration data and the clustering point at the same underlying story from different angles. The ₹0–50 band is immobile and the algorithm's own cheapest cluster is the most behaviourally distinct one — consistently distressed, high-beta, negative-return, with almost no stock in that price range crossing back out. That's a real, structural floor: whatever put a stock under ₹50 tends to keep it there, and keep it behaving badly while it's there. Above that floor, both migration (roughly half of every mid-price band changes bands over 3.5 years) and clustering (near-identical median prices producing opposite risk profiles) agree that price stops being a reliable organising variable. A stock trading at ₹550 today could plausibly be at ₹300 or ₹900 in three years, and knowing its current price tells you very little about which of the algorithm's four behavioural clusters it actually belongs to.

Verdict: The floor is real; everything above it is noisy. Only 7.7% of stocks priced under ₹50 ever cross out of that band, and K-means clustering — with no knowledge of price-band boundaries at all — independently rediscovers that same group as its most distinct, worst-behaving cluster. Above ₹50, though, roughly half of every price band's stocks migrate to a different band within 3.5 years, and the clustering finds nearly identical prices producing opposite risk-and-return profiles. The consistent finding across this entire price-band series holds again here: there is one real, structural break in the Indian market's cheapest stocks, and it sits near the very bottom of the price scale, not at any of the round-number boundaries (₹250, ₹1,000, ₹10,000) this series has used to organise the analysis.
