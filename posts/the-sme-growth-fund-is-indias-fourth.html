On 6 October the Cabinet approved a ₹10,000 crore SME Growth Fund. The government’s money goes into an alternative investment fund that will buy equity in growth-stage small and medium enterprises, with more than half meant for manufacturing and a mention of Tier-II and Tier-III industrial clusters. The Budget speech in February promised a fund “to create future champions”, with enterprises incentivised “based on select criteria”. The approval says who is eligible no more than the speech did.

The fund is the fourth government equity programme with a ₹10,000 crore government share in a decade: the first Fund of Funds for Startups (2016), the Self-Reliant India Fund for MSMEs (announced in 2020), the second Fund of Funds for startups (Cabinet in February 2026), and now this. Each reports its own progress in its own way, which makes the track record hard to read before putting a fourth fund beside it.

What the first fund shows about counting

The first fund of funds is the best-documented case, and it shows how one programme can carry several honest-looking numbers. At the end of 2022 the commerce ministry told Parliament that funds supported by the scheme had invested ₹14,077 crore in 791 startups. Behind that figure, the same reply said ₹7,980 crore was committed to 99 funds and only ₹3,400 crore had been disbursed to 72 of them. The ₹14,077 crore is everything the funds invested, including private money raised alongside; it is not what the government put in. The scheme requires funds to invest a multiple of the government commitment in startups (reported as at least twice), so a larger number is expected. A CRISIL assessment later put the total at ₹17,534 crore in 938 startups by November 2023.

Neither number is wrong. But a reader asking “how much of the ₹10,000 crore has reached firms?” gets a different answer from each, and the smallest, the government’s own disbursement, is the one that answers it. An early-2026 report in The Ken, citing a SIDBI spokesperson, put the amount handed to SIDBI at about ₹6,800 crore of the ₹10,000 crore. That is a single report from a spokesperson, not a parliamentary reply, and it was not checked; it is cited because it is the latest figure found.

The three figures may measure different things, and no source found gave the amount sanctioned or the share of the ₹10,000 crore government money actually drawn down.

The Self-Reliant India Fund, which backs MSMEs through daughter funds, is the closer comparison for the new fund. The three published figures above go from about ₹11,000 crore to about ₹16,700 crore of investment in MSMEs in ten months, but they are not obviously the same measure, and the fund’s own target of ₹50,000 crore (₹10,000 crore of government money and ₹40,000 crore from private funds) has no published progress against it. Whether the SRI Fund’s daughter funds have reached the medium-sized firms the new fund is meant for is not something the numbers answer.

The Cabinet decision was seen in press coverage only; the PIB release of 6 October is after the end of this blog’s PIB index, and PIB pages could not be opened.

The gap that matters is eligibility. The Budget speech and the Cabinet coverage describe a fund for enterprises that are already growing; the old funds were designed for startups and for MSMEs. Where the line falls decides whether the money goes to firms with ₹100 crore of turnover or ₹500 crore, and so how far it overlaps with banks and with the existing funds. The second startup fund already has an innovation-led manufacturing segment. How the new fund avoids competing with it for the same deals is not stated.

A second gap is the government’s share of the money. In the second startup fund the government can be at most half of any fund’s corpus. For the new fund the share of private money is not stated, and with it the incentives of whoever manages the fund.

What would fix this

  • The Department of Financial Services and the Ministry of MSME should publish the fund’s terms before its first close: the investment manager and how it is chosen, the fund’s life, the private-capital share required, the fees, and a written eligibility test tied to the Udyam size classes.
  • SIDBI and NSIC Venture Capital Fund should report each quarter, for every government equity programme, four numbers on one page: amount committed to funds, amount drawn down by funds, amount invested in firms, and how much of each is government money, so “invested” can no longer mean three different things.
  • The Ministry of MSME should commission and publish an independent evaluation of the Self-Reliant India Fund before the new fund’s first investment, as the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade did for the first startup fund of funds with CRISIL.
  • The Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade and the Ministry of MSME should keep one public register of every fund and manager supported across the four programmes, so overlap between funds and managers can be seen.
  • The Ministry of Finance should state in the fund’s framework how the SME Growth Fund differs from the SRI Fund and the innovation-led manufacturing segment of Fund of Funds 2.0, with the size of firm each is meant to serve.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
