Top line

  • 256 organisations are on the NABET portal’s accredited list (27 November 2026), up 41% from 182 in February 2022. Of those 182, 163 are still listed, 19 are not, and 91 on today’s list were not there then.
  • 109 of the 256 (43%) show a validity date that has passed (27) or falls by 27 November 2027 (82), counting any extension letter.
  • 4 organisations are accredited for nuclear power and fuel processing, 6 each for soda ash and ship breaking, and 2 for aerial ropeways, against 207 for building and construction. Accreditation is sector by sector, so the choice in a thin sector is a handful.

How it became a condition: voluntary in 2007, a memorandum in 2009, a rule in 2016

  1. August 2007NABET, part of the Quality Council of India (QCI), launches a voluntary accreditation scheme for EIA consultant organisations.
  2. 2 December 2009A Ministry office memorandum says EIA and EMP reports from consultants not registered with QCI or NABET will not be considered after 30 June 2010 (as recited by the Delhi High Court and by CAG).
  3. June and November 2010Memoranda let 157 and then 265 applicants appear before the committees while their applications were pending (as recited by the Delhi High Court).
  4. 30 June and 30 September 2011The Ministry issues the final scheme memoranda. NABET’s own history dates version 3 of the scheme to June 2015.
  5. 5 October 2011The Ministry makes the project proponent answerable for an EIA’s contents after finding data copied from other reports (as quoted by India Together).
  6. 3 March 2016S.O. 648(E) inserts para 13 into the EIA Notification: only organisations accredited for the sector and category may prepare the EIA and EMP and appear before the EAC or SEAC.
  7. 10 March 2017CAG Report 39 of 2016 is tabled. It finds EIAs prepared by consultants not registered for the activity in a sample of clearances granted 2011 to July 2015.
  8. 27 May 2026The National Green Tribunal dismisses an application asking for disciplinary action against an accredited consultant, holding the grievance outside its jurisdiction (Original Application 137 of 2026).

What it means: Accreditation moved from a voluntary scheme in 2007 to a ministry memorandum in 2009 and to a rule in the EIA Notification in 2016, but the enforcement record in this article runs through audits and complaints, not through published sanctions.

What to do: Applicants should treat accreditation as a legal condition of the EIA and verify it themselves, because the audit and tribunal record below shows the check was not always made.

What it means: Each organisation is accredited sector by sector (median 7, up to 31, with 59 listing three or fewer and 96 listing ten or more), so a nuclear-power, ship-breaking or soda-ash project has between 4 and 6 accredited organisations to choose from.

What to do: A proponent in a thin sector should confirm the organisation’s approved EIA coordinator for that exact sector and category on the portal before signing, because losing that one expert can cancel the sector.

Renewals: 109 of 256 certificates have lapsed or fall due within a year

What it means: Accreditation lasts three years with a surveillance assessment at 18 months, so 109 organisations (43%) are lapsed or due for renewal inside the next year, and 27 of those already show a passed date with no valid extension letter.

What to do: Anyone relying on an organisation should record the validity date on the day they sign and recheck the portal before the EIA is filed, because the scheme can move an organisation to a non-accredited list if a shortfall is not met within 90 days.

What it means: A CAG audit tabled in March 2017, covering clearances granted before the 2016 rule, found 28 of 86 projects in three sectors with a consultant not registered for the activity, and the sources found here record complaints and an appeal for action but no published list of suspended or debarred organisations.

What to do: NABET and the Ministry should publish suspensions, cancellations and debarments with their reasons and dates, so that the scheme’s own enforcement powers can be checked from outside.

How to check a consultant on the portal

  1. Find the organisation on the NABET portal list and note its certificate number, category and validity date. Check any extension-letter date too.
  2. Match the sector. The scope table lists the sectors it covers and the Schedule item for each. The organisation must be accredited for your sector and for your project’s category.
  3. Read the category marks. Category A organisations can take category A and B projects. Where a sector shows A followed by a star, the expert qualified for category A but the organisation as a whole scored under 60%, so it cannot take category A projects in that sector.
  4. Read the remarks (for example ‘Conditions apply’) and the Human Resource link, which shows the approved EIA coordinator and experts.
  5. Ask for the organisation’s accreditation certificate and attach the sector and category to the application. A complaint about an organisation can go to the NABET CEO.

What would fix this

  1. NABET: print an ‘as on’ date on the list, flag entries whose validity date has passed, and publish a dated list of suspensions, cancellations and debarments with reasons.
  2. MoEFCC: require the EIA Notification’s accreditation check to be made on PARIVESH, by matching the consultant, sector and category against NABET’s list when an application is filed, which the CAG audit found was not always done on paper.
  3. NABET: publish, per organisation and sector, the number of EIAs submitted and the outcome of appraisal, so that experience is visible and not only accreditation.
  4. The Ministry: state where a complaint against an accredited consultant should go, given that the NGT held in 2026 that its own jurisdiction did not cover one.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.

Method and definitions
  • The register counts come from one fetch of the NABET portal page ‘Accredited EIA Consultant Organisations’ on 27 November 2026. Names, certificate numbers, States, categories, dates and sector scopes are saved with this piece; contact details are not.
  • State is the State named in each organisation’s registered address, read by this blog (twelve addresses were assigned by hand from the town named). It is not where the organisation works.
  • Sector counts are the number of organisations whose scope table lists the sector. The ‘category A for the sector’ column counts entries marked A without a star.
  • Effective validity is the later of the certificate date and any extension-letter date. ‘Lapsed’ means that date was before 27 November 2026.
  • Turnover since 2022 matches NABET organisation codes between the 14 February 2022 list and the 2026 list; two 2026 entries show no code and are left out of the match.
  • Audit figures are the CAG’s counts in Table 2.8; the 28 of 86 sums the three sectors where the audit states a count of consultants not registered for the activity, and does not cover the other four groups.
What this piece could not establish
  • Whether the lapsed or soon-expiring entries have renewals in progress. The portal page’s own ‘as on’ date was blank, so how current the list is was not established.
  • Whether the 2013 and 2015 counts match the 2022 and 2026 lists in definition. They come from an article and the scheme text, and are shown as given.
  • Where organisations actually work. Registered addresses are a rough proxy, and one organisation may serve several States.
  • How many organisations, if any, have been suspended, cancelled or debarred. No published list was found, and absence from the list is not evidence that none was taken.
  • How many EIAs each organisation has prepared, or how appraisal committees rated them. The portal does not show it.
  • The merits of any complaint. The 2026 Tribunal order dismissed an application on jurisdiction and made no finding on the allegations; no organisation is named here for that reason.
  • Whether accreditation has improved EIA quality. The CAG audit predates the 2016 rule and covers files from 2011 to 2015; no later audit of the same kind was found.
  • An NGT appeal rejoinder of September 2026 quotes an audit as observing 21 approvals where the consultant was non-accredited or not accredited for the sector. Only a search excerpt was seen, the audit it quotes was not identified, and it is not used here.
  • One organisation (entry 128) shows no sector lines on the portal page, and two show no organisation code.
