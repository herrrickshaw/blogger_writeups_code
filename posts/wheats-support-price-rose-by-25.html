On 30 September 2026 the Cabinet set the minimum support prices for the crops farmers will sow this winter and sell from April 2027. Wheat went up by ₹25 a quintal, from ₹2,585 to ₹2,610. That is a rise of 1.0%. The year before, it had gone up by ₹160, or 6.6%.

On this blog’s count, using the support prices for every rabi marketing season back to 2008-09, no year since then has seen a smaller percentage rise; in rupees, only the ₹20 rises of 2010-11 and 2011-12 were smaller. The other five rabi crops rose by between 1.4% and 10.3%. The reason is not hard to find in the government’s own granaries.

Wheat’s small rise does not make it unprofitable. At ₹2,610 against a cost of ₹1,264, its margin is 106%, the widest of the six crops, and the government buys it in bulk in Punjab, Haryana and Madhya Pradesh. What the small rise does is narrow the gap between wheat and the crops the government wants farmers to plant more of: the oilseeds safflower and mustard, and the pulse masur, all of which got rises of 5.6% to 10.3%. The National Mission on Edible Oils needs oilseed output to grow about 10% a year, as this blog’s edible oil piece found.

Nineteen years of wheat support prices

The closest parallel is 2012. With stocks above the buffer norms, the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices recommended leaving wheat’s support price unchanged at ₹1,285 for 2013-14; the government overrode it and set ₹1,350. This time, the small rise was the government’s own choice; this blog could not find the commission’s report for the season to see what it recommended.

India has had a good run of wheat harvests. Production reached a record 120.66 million tonnes, according to reports of the export decision, and the government bought about 34 to 35 million tonnes in the 2026-27 season, against a target first set at 30.3 million and later raised to 34.5 million: the most since 43.3 million tonnes in 2021-22. On 1 September the central pool held 480 lakh tonnes of wheat, more than twice what the buffer norms ask for on 1 October.

The rest of the policy record over the past year points the same way. Stock limits on wheat traders, imposed in May 2025 to hold down prices, were withdrawn in February 2026. Exports, banned since May 2022, were reopened in stages and then fully on 24 August 2026. Food Corporation auctions of surplus wheat drew so few buyers at the reserve price that they were suspended, and the food secretary was reported as saying domestic prices were depressed and exports would help farmers. A large rise in the support price would have added to stocks the government is already trying to sell.

What would fix this

  • The Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices should publish its rabi price-policy report before the Cabinet decides, and the Cabinet release should say where it departed from the recommendation, so a 1% rise reads as a stated stock-management choice rather than something inferred from the granary figures.
  • The Department of Food and Public Distribution should review the buffer norms set in January 2015, which still define “surplus” a decade later, against current offtake under the free-grain scheme, and publish the method.
  • The Directorate General of Foreign Trade and the food department should publish a stock-linked rule for wheat exports, such as opening exports when central pool stocks exceed a stated multiple of the buffer norm, so traders and farmers are not working from a four-year ban followed by a sudden reopening.
  • The Food Corporation of India should publish quantities offered and sold at every open-market sale auction, with the reserve price, so the gap between stocks and what the market will buy is on record.
  • The Department of Agriculture should publish procurement as a share of production by state for each crop, so it is clear how much of the crop the support price actually reaches outside the main procuring states.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
