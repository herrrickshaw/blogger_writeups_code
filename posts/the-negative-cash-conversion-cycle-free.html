Most companies have to pay for raw materials, wages and stock long before customers pay them. That gap has to be financed with the company’s own cash or with borrowing, and it grows as the business grows. A few companies run the other way. A supermarket sells a tin of beans within days of receiving it, takes cash at the till, and pays the supplier a month or two later. The faster it grows, the more cash it holds. Its suppliers and customers are, in effect, lending it money for free.

This piece explains how the cycle is measured, the three routes to a negative one, what real companies’ numbers look like, and a checklist for telling a strong negative cycle from a dangerous one.

How the cycle is measured

The cash conversion cycle, a measure introduced by Verlyn Richards and Eugene Laughlin in 1980, counts the days between paying for inputs and collecting cash from customers. It has three parts:

Cash conversion cycle = inventory days + receivable days − payable days  (CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO)
  • Days inventory outstanding (DIO): how long stock sits before it is sold. Inventory divided by cost of goods sold, times 365.
  • Days sales outstanding (DSO): how long customers take to pay. Trade receivables divided by revenue, times 365.
  • Days payables outstanding (DPO): how long the company takes to pay its suppliers. Trade payables divided by cost of goods sold, times 365.

A cycle of 60 days means the company’s cash is tied up for two months on every sale. A cycle of −30 days means it holds customers’ cash for a month before suppliers are paid.

Two practical points matter. First, use averages of opening and closing balances where possible, because a year-end figure can be dressed up by a well-timed payment run. Second, the cycle is meaningful only for businesses that buy and sell goods. For banks, insurers and most pure software companies, inventory and payables are tiny or mean something else, and the measure tells you little.

Three routes to a negative cycle

A cycle turns negative when payable days exceed inventory days plus receivable days. There are three ways to get there, and they are not equally healthy.

1. Selling fast for cash. Supermarkets, fast-food chains and online retailers turn stock in weeks and collect payment at the till or the checkout. Their receivable days are close to zero, so even ordinary supplier terms leave the cycle near or below zero. This is the most durable route, because it comes from how the business operates.

2. Getting paid in advance. Some businesses collect cash before they deliver anything: subscriptions, airline tickets, education fees, and engineering or construction contracts with customer advances. The prepaid cash shows up as deferred revenue or contract liabilities, which the standard formula ignores, so the true position is often even better than the cycle suggests. The risk is that the company owes the customer a product or service it has not yet provided.

3. Paying suppliers late. A large buyer can insist on long payment terms because suppliers cannot afford to lose its business. Apple and Hindustan Unilever, for example, take well over 100 days to pay. When it reflects genuine bargaining power, this is a real advantage. When it reflects a company stretching suppliers because it is short of cash, it is a warning sign. The two can look identical on the balance sheet.

The health test is simple to state and hard to apply: is the negative cycle produced by the business model, or by financial pressure on suppliers? The checklist later in this piece is built to answer that.

The negative cycles come from different places:

  • Apple (−72 days) holds very little inventory (11 days) and takes about 115 days to pay suppliers. That is bargaining power: suppliers compete to be in its products.
  • Hindustan Unilever (−65 days) pays suppliers in about 137 days while its products sell through distributors within weeks. India’s largest consumer-goods company has the scale to set terms.
  • Amazon (−42 days) pays suppliers in about 111 days, roughly three times as long as its inventory sits. Its receivable days (31) are higher than a typical retailer’s, probably because a large share of its revenue comes from business customers, such as cloud clients, who are billed on account.
  • Dell (−30 days) pioneered the model in personal computers: build to order, collect quickly, pay suppliers later.
  • Larsen & Toubro (−26 days) is an engineering contractor. Its negative cycle comes partly from paying subcontractors slowly and partly from customer advances, and the formula captures only part of either.
  • Maruti Suzuki (−15 days) collects from its dealers within about 12 days and pays its parts suppliers in about 51.

The positive cycles are just as instructive. Walmart and Costco, the classic examples of retail float, sit close to zero, not deeply negative: they pay suppliers about as fast as they sell. DMart pays its suppliers in about a week and so runs a positive cycle of 27 days despite selling for cash. Paying early usually buys a retailer lower purchase prices, a trade-off the cycle alone does not show. Caterpillar and Titan tie up cash for about six months, Caterpillar in machines and dealer credit, Titan in gold jewellery inventory.

The lesson is that the cycle is a description of a business model, not a score. A negative number is good only if it comes from something the company can sustain.

How to analyse a company with a negative cycle

A negative cycle deserves the same scrutiny as a high profit margin: first ask where it comes from, then whether it will last.

  1. Break it into its three parts. Work out which component makes the cycle negative. Low inventory and receivable days point to a fast, cash-based business. Very high payable days point to supplier terms, which need a closer look.
  2. Compare with direct competitors. A cycle is only meaningful against companies with the same business model. A 60-day payable period is long for a supermarket and short for a construction firm.
  3. Track the trend over five years or more. A cycle that has been stable for years reflects the business. One that suddenly turns more negative while profits are flat often means the company has started paying suppliers later.
  4. Compare payables growth with sales growth. If payables grow much faster than revenue or cost of goods sold, the company is stretching suppliers. Ask why.
  5. Read the supplier finance note. Under rules that took effect in 2024, companies reporting under IFRS must disclose supplier finance (also called reverse factoring or supply-chain finance) arrangements: their terms, the amounts involved and how much the bank has already paid suppliers. US companies have a similar requirement. Payables that a bank has already settled behave like short-term debt.
  6. Check operating cash flow against profit. A growing company with a negative cycle should generate operating cash flow above its net profit, because working capital releases cash. If cash flow falls behind profit, the working-capital tailwind may be reversing.
  7. Ask what happens if growth stops. The cash released by a negative cycle depends on growth. When sales flatten, the float stops growing; when sales fall, it shrinks and the company has to pay out cash it had been using. A business that has spent its float on expansion or buybacks can find itself short.
  8. Watch supplier health. Late payment pushes a company’s financing need onto its suppliers. If they are small and fragile, the supply chain becomes the weak point. Supplier failures, disputes or a regulator’s attention to payment practices are warning signs.
  9. Look for the hidden float. Customer advances, deferred revenue and contract liabilities are left out of the standard formula. For subscription businesses, airlines and contractors, adding them gives a truer picture, which is often even more negative.
  10. Value the float properly. Interest-free financing from suppliers and customers is worth something, especially when interest rates are high. But treat it as a liability that has to be maintained, not as spare cash. Counting the float as cash in a valuation inflates the result.

When it goes wrong: Carillion

The clearest warning is Carillion, the British construction and outsourcing group that collapsed into liquidation in January 2018. A joint report by two House of Commons committees, published in May 2018, described how it used its suppliers as a source of finance.

Carillion had signed the government’s Prompt Payment Code, but its standard payment terms were, in the committees’ words, “an extraordinary 120 days.” Suppliers could be paid in 45 days through a bank-run early payment facility, but only by accepting a discount. The committees concluded that this “opened a line of credit for Carillion, which it used systematically to shore up its fragile balance sheet.”

On the standard measures, Carillion’s long payable days would have made its cycle look efficient. In reality, the money owed to suppliers was functioning as borrowing that did not appear as debt. When the company failed, that burden fell on its suppliers and subcontractors.

Accounting standard-setters have since responded to investors’ concerns that such arrangements were hard to see. In May 2023 the International Accounting Standards Board issued amendments to IAS 7 and IFRS 7 requiring companies to disclose supplier finance arrangements, the liabilities involved, the amounts banks have already paid suppliers, and the range of payment due dates. The rules apply to annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2024. An analyst looking at a company with very long payable days should now be able to see how much of that is really bank funding.

Method and limits

  • Data source. Cycles were computed from the latest annual income statements and balance sheets as published on Yahoo Finance, using averages of opening and closing inventory, trade receivables and trade payables. Financial years end between September 2025 and August 2026. Figures were not checked line by line against each company’s annual report, and data providers classify items differently, so treat each number as approximate to within a few days.
  • Classification. Amazon’s and Apple’s cost of goods sold, and the split of payables between trade and other items, follow Yahoo Finance’s classification. Larsen & Toubro’s contract assets and customer advances are not included.
  • Missing data. Nestle India was dropped because the required fields were missing.
  • Association, not cause. Research on working capital and profitability, starting with Marc Deloof’s 2003 study of Belgian firms, finds that companies with shorter cycles tend to be more profitable. That is an association across companies, not proof that cutting the cycle raises profits.
