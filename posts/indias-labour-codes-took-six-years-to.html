India's four labour codes — the Code on Wages (2019), and the Industrial Relations, Social Security, and Occupational Safety, Health & Working Conditions Codes (all September 2020) — consolidate 29 separate central labour laws into four. Parliament finished passing all of them more than five years ago. They only came into legal force on 21 November 2025, and that date is itself a starting gun, not a finish line: the notification that made them "in force" carried no operative rules, and India's labour-code rollout has been happening in slow, uneven layers ever since.

Since 22 October. Punjab’s Code on Wages and Social Security rules were notified on 31 October 2026 and published on 5 November, when they came into force. Maharashtra’s Wage Rules also came into force on 5 November, with its other three codes still at draft.

At the Centre, the new EPF scheme was notified in June, and in September the EPFO wage ceiling rose from Rs 15,000 to Rs 25,000 a month, up 66.7%, which Business Standard estimates brings 5.1 million more workers under social security. Establishments registered under the earlier central laws had until about 8 November, six months after the Central Rules, to update their particulars on the Shram Suvidha portal.

Where a state has no final rules, the Ministry’s FAQ of 30 December 2025, as quoted by the Labour Codes Library, says the old rules continue to the extent they are in line with the codes; that is the Ministry’s explanation, not the law.

What would fix the counting problem.

  1. The Ministry of Labour and Employment should publish one dated list, by state and code, of final and draft rules with gazette references, updated at least monthly.
  2. The Ministry should say whether the 31 October target was met and name the ten states cited on 24 September.
  3. Each state labour department should publish final rules in its gazette with a stated commencement date and keep a code-wise status page on its website.
  4. The Ministry should notify the transitional position for jurisdictions without final rules, now set out only in an FAQ.

Six years from passage to "in force"

The government's original target was to bring all four codes into effect from 1 April 2021. That date was deferred because most states had not yet framed their own rules, which they must do because labour is on the Concurrent List, and later dates in 2021 slipped too amid opposition from trade unions over job-security and worker-rights concerns. The codes stayed passed but dormant for years. The government notified them into force on 21 November 2025 — the date most coverage cites as "when India's labour reform happened."

What "in force" did not mean. A code "coming into force" is a legal trigger, not an operating system. The November 2025 notification activated the codes' text, but none of the detailed rules — wage calculation methods, dispute-resolution procedures, registration and compliance forms — that employers and regulators actually need to apply them existed yet.

"In force" without rules: the five-and-a-half-month gap

Draft Central Rules were published for public comment on 30 December 2025 — a 30-day objection window for the Industrial Relations Code's rules, 45 days for the other three. Government officials indicated the final rules would be notified by 1 April 2026. They were not. The Ministry of Labour and Employment actually notified the final Central Rules under all four codes on 8 May 2026 (G.S.R. 343(E)) — five weeks past even the extended target, and five and a half months after the codes' own "in force" date.

Central Rules cover a minority of India's workforce

The May 2026 Central Rules apply only where the central government is the "appropriate government" under India's labour-law framework: banking, insurance, telecommunications, mining, air transport, railways (including metro), major ports, oilfields, and central public-sector undertakings. Labour is a Concurrent List subject in India's constitution — the much larger share of the workforce, employed in sectors that fall under state jurisdiction, is governed by separate State Rules that each state and union territory has to frame and notify on its own. The Central Rules notification did not, by itself, operationalise the codes for most Indian workers.

The state rollout, state by state

Tracking exactly how many of India's 28 states have finished this is harder than it should be: no single, continuously updated government tracker was found, and secondary trackers disagree with each other depending on their snapshot date and on whether "notified" means all four codes or just some. The most recent single-date count located, dated 9 July 2026, put 11 states at final-rules-notified: Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Gujarat, Karnataka, Haryana, Uttarakhand, Jharkhand, Odisha, Bihar, Chhattisgarh and Assam. An earlier March 2026 snapshot listed a different, overlapping set of six states with all four codes' rules notified (Gujarat, Arunachal Pradesh, Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra) — the discrepancy between the two lists is itself evidence of how unevenly this is being tracked, not just how unevenly it is being rolled out. Separately, as of the most recent Parliament reply located, at least 17 states had taken some concrete implementation step (draft or final rules for at least one code), out of India's 28 states.

Reading this section. Treat the 11-state and 17-state figures as snapshots from specific, cited dates, not as a live count. Given the pace of notifications through 2026, either number is likely stale by the time this is read — the direction (most states not yet fully done, more than nine months after the codes' "in force" date) is the reliable part, not the precise count.

What the reform is meant to deliver, once complete

The government's stated goals for full rollout: formal protection for more than 90% of informal workers, a rise in the overall formal-workforce share from an estimated 60.4% to a targeted 75.5%, and social-security coverage reaching 85%. Every one of these is a target tied to full implementation — Central Rules plus every state's own rules — not a description of where the rollout stands today.

The net read

"India's labour codes took effect on 21 November 2025" is true and also not the whole story. The Central Rules that actually operationalise the codes took another five and a half months, and even they only reach the minority of the workforce under central jurisdiction. For most Indian workers, whether and when the codes change anything in practice depends on their own state government finishing a rulemaking process that, nine months after the "in force" date, a majority of states had not yet completed.

What would fix this

  • The Ministry of Labour and Employment should maintain a public tracker showing, for every state and union territory, the gazette date of draft and final rules under each of the four codes, so the 11-state and 6-state counts from private trackers stop being the only way to see where the rollout stands.
  • The Ministry of Labour and Employment should name the states, code by code, whenever it reports implementation progress to Parliament, so a figure such as 'at least 17 states have taken some step' can be checked and compared with later replies.
  • The Ministry of Labour and Employment should agree and publish target dates with each state that has not notified final rules, since the central rules missed their 1 April 2026 target by five weeks and most workers depend on state rules that have no published deadline.
  • The Ministry of Labour and Employment should publish an official baseline and annual figures for formal-workforce share and social-security coverage, so the stated goals of moving from 60.4% to 75.5% formal employment and 85% coverage can be tracked against government data rather than one bank's research note.

These are proposals from this blog, not recommendations any agency has adopted.
