The World Bank expects commodity prices to rise 16 percent in 2026, but most of the jump comes from the blocked Strait of Hormuz, not from a new demand-driven supercycle. Whether a rise is a shock or a cycle decides who gains and who pays, and that depends on how much a country buys from, or sells to, the rest of the world.

In March 2026 the war with Iran effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz. The World Bank called the result the largest oil supply loss on record, about 10 million barrels a day. Brent crude went from $72 at the end of February to $118 at the end of March, its biggest monthly rise ever. Fertilizer prices followed. Metals were already climbing, and gold had nearly doubled in a year.

Since then the word "supercycle" has been everywhere. This piece asks three questions. What do economists actually mean by a supercycle? Are we in one? And what happens to companies whose inputs or customers sit abroad when commodity prices swing?

  • A supercycle is a decades-long, demand-driven swing. The 2026 energy and fertilizer spikes are supply shocks. History says supply shocks fade, while demand booms last.
  • Metals are the real candidate for a new upswing. Base metals are about 30 percent above their 2025 level in real terms, pulled up by grids, storage and data centres.
  • A company's home country matters as much as its industry. Across 2,560 listed companies in 19 markets, stocks track commodity prices more closely when their country exports that commodity, and less closely when it imports it.

What economists mean by a supercycle

A supercycle is a rise and fall in real commodity prices that lasts 20 to 70 years. It is far longer than an ordinary boom and bust. The upswing usually runs for one to three decades, and the downswing about as long.

The pattern is driven by demand. A large economy industrialises and needs steel, copper, oil and grain on a scale the world has not supplied before. Mines and oilfields take five to fifteen years to plan and build, so prices stay high for a long time. When the new supply finally arrives, often just as the demand boom slows, prices slide for years.

Bilge Erten and José Antonio Ocampo traced real commodity prices back to 1865. They found four supercycles of 30 to 40 years each, with prices swinging 20 to 40 percent above and below their long-run trend. For commodities other than oil, the cycles followed world GDP, which means they were driven by demand. They also found that each cycle's average was lower than the one before, especially for farm goods. Economists call that slow decline the Prebisch–Singer pattern.

Their four cycles, measured trough to trough, line up with familiar periods:

  1. 1894–1932: late industrialisation in the United States and Germany, and the First World War.
  2. 1932–1971: rearmament, the Second World War and post-war reconstruction.
  3. 1971–1999: the 1970s commodity boom, including the oil shocks, and the long slide that followed.
  4. 1999 onward: China's industrialisation.

Two other findings matter for 2026. Martin Stuermer studied copper, lead, tin and zinc prices from 1840 to 2010. He found that long price swings come mainly from persistent demand shocks, while supply shocks matter much less. Lutz Kilian showed that an oil price rise caused by lost supply affects prices and the economy differently from one caused by strong global demand.

So the useful question is not whether prices are high. It is whether they are high because the world needs more, or because something is blocking supply.

Where the cycle stands: 1960 to 2026

The long trends tell their own story. Real agricultural prices have drifted down about 4 percent a decade since 1960, as Prebisch and Singer predicted. Real energy prices have drifted up. The cycle itself explains 15 to 34 percent of the movement in each index; the rest is trend and short booms.

Two cautions. A filter like this is least reliable at the end of a data series, and 66 years of data covers only about two full cycles, so the 2020–23 trough is a provisional call. The 2026 figures are my estimate: January to September prices, adjusted for inflation using the 2025 deflator.

2026: a shock on top of a cycle

The 2026 surge is really two stories moving at different speeds.

Energy and fertilizers: a supply shock. Before the war, ships passing through Hormuz carried close to 35 percent of the world's seaborne crude, 20 percent of refined products and 20 percent of liquefied natural gas, according to the World Bank's April outlook. The Gulf is also a major source of urea, sulfur and aluminium. When traffic stopped, the World Bank's energy index rose from 92 in February to 147 in April (2010 = 100). Its fertilizer index went from 145 to 209.

Prices eased in June and July as some shipping resumed. Then renewed strikes on tankers and Gulf infrastructure in September pushed the energy index back to 149, above its April peak. Fertilizers, by contrast, have fallen back to 147.

The World Bank describes these as dislocations: the world has enough production capacity, but access to it is blocked. That is the textbook supply shock. On Stuermer's evidence, such shocks fade once the blockage clears, though nobody can say when shipping through Hormuz will return to normal.

Metals: the demand story. Base metals have risen more steadily and without a single trigger. The index is about 30 percent above its 2025 level in real terms and at a record in nominal terms. Electricity grids, battery storage and data centres all need copper and aluminium, while new mines take a decade or more to open. The World Bank warned that base metals "could increase more than anticipated amid inflexible supplies and resilient demand." That is what the early years of a demand-led supercycle look like.

The case is not settled. Forecasts for the 2026 copper market range from a deficit to a surplus, and China, whose buying drove the last supercycle, is growing more slowly. Gold and silver are a separate matter: the World Bank's precious metals index rose 84 percent between early 2025 and early 2026 on safe-haven buying and speculation, not industrial demand.

Who depends on foreign suppliers, and who on foreign buyers

A commodity price rise is a windfall for a country that sells the commodity abroad and a tax on one that buys it. To see who is on which side, I took each market's 2023 trade with the whole world from UN Comtrade and computed a net trade score for four commodity groups: exports minus imports, divided by exports plus imports. In dollars, China has the largest net energy import bill, at $455 billion, followed by Japan ($183 billion), India ($131 billion) and South Korea ($117 billion). Saudi Arabia ($231 billion), Canada ($105 billion) and Australia ($87 billion) are the biggest net exporters. The United States now exports slightly more energy than it imports.

Some countries sit on both sides of the same shock. Brazil exports food worth a net $132 billion but imports almost all its fertilizer. Chile exports copper but imports nearly all its energy. India exports food on balance but depends on foreign suppliers for oil, gas, metals and almost every tonne of fertilizer it uses.

What stock prices show

Trade figures say who should gain or lose. Stock prices say whether investors agree. I took 2,841 large listed companies across the same markets and measured how each one's share price has moved with commodity prices since 2015. Of those, 2,560 had at least five years of monthly data.

For each stock I estimated a commodity beta: how much its dollar return moves with a 1 percent change in the World Bank's energy, metals, food and fertilizer price indices, after allowing for the world stock market. A metals beta of 0.2 means a 10 percent rise in metal prices comes with a 2 percent extra return on the stock.

Industry matters first, as you would expect. Oil and gas producers have the highest energy betas, with a median of 0.37 for exploration and production companies. Miners lead on metals at 0.72, followed by steelmakers at 0.36. Software and healthcare-products companies move the other way when metals rise.

But the home market matters too. Materials companies in metal-exporting countries track metal prices much more closely than materials companies in metal-importing ones.

Materials stocks track metals more where the home market exports metals. Each dot is one market's average metals beta for its materials stocks; the dashed line is the fitted slope of 0.21 (t = 2.6).

Miners in Chile, South Africa and Australia gain the most when metal prices rise. Materials companies in Japan, France and the Netherlands, which mostly process imported inputs, barely move. China is the outlier: its materials companies track metals closely despite heavy ore imports, because its own mining and smelting industries are so large.

The same pattern holds for companies that use energy rather than produce it. Industrial, consumer, utility and materials companies in energy-exporting markets have higher energy betas than the same kinds of companies in energy-importing markets. The slope is 0.05 (t = 2.6) across 19 markets. In Brazil and Saudi Arabia, an oil price rise brings money into the domestic economy, which helps local companies. In South Korea, Switzerland and Germany, the extra cost of oil leaves the country.

Two weaker results point the same way. Energy producers in energy-exporting markets move a little more with oil (t = 1.9), and food companies in food-exporting markets move a little more with food prices (t = 1.5). With only 13 to 19 markets in each test, these are suggestive, not proof.

One surprise: heavy energy users such as airlines, basic chemicals and carmakers show almost no energy beta of their own once the world market is accounted for. Airlines come in at −0.02, statistically zero. Their cost shock seems to reach them through the broad market sell-off and through passing costs on to customers, rather than as a separate exposure.

The Hormuz test

The closure of Hormuz was a natural experiment. If trade dependence matters, stocks in energy-exporting markets should have done better than stocks in energy-importing markets in March and April 2026.

Saudi stocks rose 6.6% as Hormuz closed and Japan's fell 7.4%, but the pattern was loose. Average dollar return by market, end-February to end-April 2026. Blue = net energy exporter, grey = net energy importer.

The broad direction held. Saudi Arabia, the most energy-exporting market in the sample, was the best performer. Japan, the most energy-dependent, was among the worst. Chile, which imports almost all its energy, fell 5.6 percent.

But the pattern was loose. Indonesia, a coal and gas exporter, fell 10.5 percent, the worst of any market. Across all 19 markets, the link between energy trade position and returns was not statistically clear (t = 1.6). For the renewed attacks in August and September it was close to zero (t = 0.3).

The sector split was sharper. Energy stocks rose 7.3 percent across all markets in March and April, while consumer discretionary stocks fell 7.6 percent. Technology stocks rose 11 percent, and in many markets that swamped the commodity effect.

The lesson is that a country's trade position shapes how its stocks behave over many years, but one shock, however large, adds too little information to separate winners from losers reliably.

Do small companies react late to their trade partners?

A well-known finding in finance says they should. Lauren Cohen and Andrea Frazzini showed in 2008 that US stocks are slow to react to news about their major customers: when a customer's shares fall, the supplier's shares tend to follow over the next month. The lag is strongest where investors pay the least attention. If that applied across borders, a small Indian steelmaker might trail a sell-off in its Korean customers by weeks.

I tested that on every listed company in a goods-producing industry across the 20 markets, not just the large caps used above. Of 18,358 firms in 42 industries, 8,414 passed basic data filters, giving 684,837 firm-months from 2016 to 2026. For each firm I built two signals from last month's returns of the same industry abroad: a supplier signal weighted by where the home country imports that industry's products from, and a customer signal weighted by where it exports them. Small firms are those in the bottom third by trading value in their market, with a median of about $3 million traded a month, against $304 million for the top third. Trading value stands in for analyst coverage.

The answer is no. With global industry and country moves removed, neither signal reliably predicts next month's return for small firms:

Three things stand out. First, the extra effect for small firms is negative, the opposite of the slow-reaction story: what little signal there is sits in larger companies. Second, a trading strategy inside the small-firm group, buying the third with the strongest partner signal and selling the weakest, lost 0.1 to 0.3 percent a month, though none of these losses is statistically significant. Shuffling each country's trade partners at random did about as well as the real partners (placebo p-values of 0.09 to 0.27).

Third, small firms do lag something: their own country's industry. Last month's return of the same industry at home predicts small firms strongly (t = 3.37) and large firms not at all. That matches Kewei Hou's 2007 finding that news reaches small firms late from their domestic industry leaders. Country-level trade flows are probably too coarse to stand in for a real customer list: a steelmaker's share of national steel exports to Korea says little about which Korean buyer it actually supplies.

Limits. The firm list is today's, so delisted firms are missing. A planned check of analyst counts against trading value was blocked by Yahoo's rate limits, so the coverage proxy is not validated. Trade weights cover only trade among the 20 markets.

What it means for companies that depend on foreign trade

Tell the cycle from the shock. A company facing higher energy or fertilizer costs in 2026 is dealing with a blockade, and history suggests those prices fall back once supply flows again. A company exposed to copper or aluminium may be facing the first years of a longer, demand-driven rise. The two call for different responses: hedging and riding it out in the first case, long-term contracts and investment in new supply in the second.

Producers in exporting countries capture most of the gain. The companies whose shares rise most with commodity prices are miners in Chile, South Africa and Australia, and oil producers in Canada and the United States. Materials companies in Japan, France and the Netherlands, which mostly process imported inputs, capture far less.

Importers pay through the whole economy. In markets with big energy import bills, such as Japan, South Korea, Germany and India, a price spike drains money from the domestic economy. Energy-using companies there show lower energy betas than the same industries in exporting countries.

India is among the most exposed large economies. It is a net importer of energy, metals and, almost entirely, fertilizers, but a net exporter of food. An energy- or fertilizer-led spike like 2026's is a direct loss in its terms of trade. A food-led spike helps its farm exporters. A metals-led supercycle would raise input costs for its manufacturers while doing little for most of its listed companies.

The United States is in a more comfortable position than in past oil shocks. It exports slightly more energy than it imports, so an oil price rise is roughly a wash for the economy as a whole: it helps producers and hurts consumers. But it imports metals and food on balance, so a metals supercycle would raise costs for its manufacturers and builders.
