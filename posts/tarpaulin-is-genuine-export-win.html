Tarpaulin Is a Genuine Export Win. Geotextiles for India's Highways Are the Opposite Story

Two government segment labels, not one "agriculture textiles" category. The National Technical Textiles Mission splits this space into Agrotech (crop covers, shade nets, mulch mats, fishing nets, tarpaulins, soil-stabilisation fabrics) and, separately, Geotech (geotextiles for roads, railways, embankments, tunnels and erosion control — including canal lining, which is agriculture-adjacent but officially a different segment). Following the same pattern as this blog's automotive-textiles piece: one of these is a genuine India strength, the other is where the real import dependency sits.

1. Tarpaulin: a real, world-leading export category

By shipment count, India is the world's largest exporter of HDPE tarpaulin — 6,763 shipments, against Vietnam's 991 and China's 166 — moving mainly to the UAE, UK and Bhutan. This isn't a category India is trying to build; it's one it already leads. Standard 200 GSM waterproof HDPE tarpaulin with eyelets and hems runs roughly $0.22-0.32 per square metre FOB from Nhava Sheva or Mundra, with premium UV-stabilised heavy-duty grades up to $0.45/sqm — Gujarat alone hosts dozens of HDPE tarpaulin weaving-and-lamination facilities, a manufacturing-cluster economics story similar to other Gujarat-based technical-textile clusters this blog has covered.

The reason tarpaulin doesn't carry a feedstock-dependency problem the way some other technical textiles do: India has ample domestic HDPE and polypropylene granule production — IOCL, Reliance, GAIL and OPAL all supply it — keeping feedstock prices comparatively stable, and the government has additionally granted full duty relief on commodity PE/PP imports to keep supply flush for downstream weavers. Tarpaulin is, in other words, secure on both ends: it exports the finished product and doesn't depend on imported polymer to make it.

2. Shade nets, mulch film and crop covers: mostly domestically served

Beyond tarpaulin, Agrotech's other major products — shade nets, mulching film, crop covers, anti-hail nets, nonwoven row covers — are made by a deep bench of Indian manufacturers: KaveriAgri Products (Tamil Nadu, since 1987), Parry Enterprises, Shalimar Thermoforming, Spunweb, GreenPro Ventures, Sunsafe Agrotextiles and CTM Technical Textiles among others, serving a domestic market estimated at roughly ₹487 crore. This is a small category — agro-textiles account for only around 1.5% of India's total technical-textile production — but it's one where the manufacturing base already exists at the scale the domestic market needs, not one waiting on import substitution.

The trade numbers back this up. This site's own TradeStat-based segment mapping (see the companion piece on India's technical-textiles feedstock loop) puts Agrotech at a stable net export surplus: FY2025-26 exports of $193M against imports of $100M, a net +$93M — down from FY25's $231M/$92M as exports softened 16.4% year-on-year, but still comfortably in surplus, tagged "Maintain" priority rather than a segment needing rescue.

3. The real import story sits in Geotech, not Agrotech

Geotextiles — woven and nonwoven fabrics used for canal lining, erosion control, road-base stabilisation and embankment reinforcement — are officially a separate NTTM segment from Agrotech, even though canal lining is directly an irrigation/agriculture application. And this is where the import dependency this piece went looking for actually lives: less than 30% of geotextile demand for National Highway Authority of India projects — a mandatory requirement under Ministry of Road Transport and Highways rules — is met by Indian MSMEs. The gap is filled by imports, with named international suppliers including Germany's Huesker and the Netherlands' TenCate active in the Indian market, alongside broader sourcing from China and South Korea for high-specification geosynthetics.

Tarpaulin is the segment India built its own supply chain for, feedstock included. Geotextiles are the segment still being filled in from Germany, the Netherlands, China and South Korea — two products woven from similar-sounding fibres, opposite trade positions.
4. What this piece does not establish

The "less than 30% MSME-fulfilled" figure is specific to National Highway projects, not to agriculture-sector geotextile demand (canal lining, farm-pond liners) specifically — this piece could not find a canal-lining-specific import share, and the highway figure is used here as the best available proxy for India's geotextile-manufacturing capacity gap generally. The claim that India imports "substantial volumes" of agrotextiles and geotextiles from China, South Korea and Germany combines both NTTM segments in the original source; it should be read as directional, not as a precise Agrotech-only import figure — Section 2's own TradeStat-based Agrotech numbers (a genuine surplus) are the more reliable segment-specific figure here. The ₹487 crore domestic shade-net/mulch-film consumption estimate's exact base year was not stated in the sourcing found for this piece.
