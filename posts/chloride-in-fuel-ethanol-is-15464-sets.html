Chloride is a trace contaminant in fuel ethanol, measured in milligrams per kilogram, and it matters well out of proportion to its quantity. With water and dissolved oxygen, it drives pitting and stress corrosion cracking in steel tanks and pipes, and it turns up in clogged filters and fuel-injector deposits. In July and August 2026 it became the centre of a public argument over E20 petrol: automakers reported readings in the hundreds of parts per million, and oil companies said their tests found almost nothing.

This piece sets out what the Indian standard actually requires, how it compares with Europe, the United States and Brazil, what each side reported in the dispute, and what water used to clean tanks and storage vessels can and cannot add.

IS 15464 sets a chloride limit for 2G ethanol and none for 1G ethanol

Ethanol that oil companies blend into petrol must meet IS 15464:2022, Anhydrous ethanol for use as blending component in motor gasoline, the first revision of the 2004 standard. Its requirements table has two columns: one for first-generation (1G) ethanol, made from molasses, cane juice and grain, and one for second-generation (2G) and off-gas fermentation (OGF) ethanol. For inorganic chloride, the 2G/OGF column sets a maximum of 3.0 mg/kg, tested by ASTM D7319 or EN 15492. The 1G column shows a dash. The same pattern holds for sulphate (4 mg/kg for 2G, none for 1G) and pHe, and water content for 1G is only “to report” where 2G is capped at 0.3%.

Almost all ethanol India blends today is 1G. The standard’s own foreword says contaminants “like chloride” are important to define ethanol quality and were “hence included”; in the published table they apply to 2G only. The BIS standards portal lists no amendment to IS 15464:2022.

Two other details point the same way. In a Rajya Sabha answer of 27 July 2026, the petroleum ministry listed what ethanol suppliers check before each tank truck: purity of at least 99.6%, water, relative density, appearance and sulphur. Chloride was not on the list. And three of the BIS laboratory scope pages this blog read, including one Indian Oil laboratory, list the inorganic chloride clause of IS 15464 among the tests the laboratory is not recognised to perform.

In August, however, the ministry and the three state oil companies said the government “has prescribed a stringent chloride specification for ethanol used in petrol blending”, and cited 3 ppm as the limit. This blog could not find the instrument that applies it to 1G ethanol, whether an amendment, a gazette notification or a clause in the oil companies’ purchase contracts.

Europe and Brazil set tighter chloride limits for blending ethanol

Brazil, which has run high-ethanol fuel for decades, sets 1 mg/kg for both anhydrous and hydrated ethanol under ANP Resolution 907 of 2022, along with limits on sulphate, iron, sodium and copper. Europe’s EN 15376 sets 1.5 mg/kg. The US standard ASTM D4806 allows 6.7 mg/kg (5 mg/L) in the version summarised by the Renewable Fuels Association in 2018. India’s 3.0 mg/kg for 2G ethanol sits between them. The automakers’ body SIAM has proposed 3 mg/kg for all ethanol used for blending and less than 1 mg/kg for finished E20 and E85.

The 2026 dispute: hundreds of ppm in automakers’ samples, under 3 ppm in oil-company tests

  • Weeks before the SIAM letter. Mercedes-Benz India raised fuel-contamination concerns and asked for tighter quality checks, according to Autocar India. SIAM says automakers had raised chloride at the BIS fuel committee for more than two years without a decision.
  • Late July. SIAM wrote to the petroleum ministry. It reported chloride as high as 500 mg/kg in fuel drawn from vehicle tanks and up to 350 mg/kg in samples from fuel stations, deposits containing chloride on failed injectors, fuel pumps and EGR valves, and moisture above 10,000 mg/kg in many cases against a 3,000 mg/kg limit. It asked for mandatory chloride limits in the BIS fuel standards, including diesel, where the test is optional. Reuters later reported that a Maruti Suzuki executive said the findings had been discussed privately with the ministry on 26 July.
  • 4 and 5 August. SIAM withdrew the letter, saying some numbers needed authentication and there was “no cause for concern”.
  • 7 and 8 August. Indian Oil, BPCL and HPCL published nationwide test results. Petrol from refineries was at or below 1 ppm. Ethanol from 80 distilleries and more than 80 terminal samples were below 3 ppm. Of more than 1,000 fuel-station samples, 160 reports analysed so far were 0 to 3 ppm, and a small number of outlets with elevated chloride were suspended: two in the companies’ statement as reported by BusinessLine and the Indian Express, four in the ministry’s release on PIB. About 90,000 outlets began checking underground tanks for water 8 to 12 times a day.
  • 13 August. Reuters published the automakers’ internal emails, with state-wise chloride ranges: 6 to 570 ppm in Rajasthan, 1.4 to 420 ppm in Delhi and 10 to 357 ppm in Maharashtra.

The two sets of numbers were not taken the same way. The oil companies sampled ethanol at distilleries, fuel at terminals and fuel at the pump. The automakers’ highest readings came from fuel drawn out of vehicles that had already failed. Neither side has published its sampling method or laboratory reports in full; the oil companies say their tables are on the website of the Centre for High Technology, which this blog could not open.

Chloride harms fuel systems at levels of a few mg/kg

Research on fuel ethanol since the early 2000s has found that carbon steel can crack in ethanol that meets the US specification. The main driver is dissolved oxygen, and chloride makes it worse: one study cited in a 2021 review found intergranular stress corrosion cracking when chloride in ethanol was below 1 mg/L, and transgranular cracking at 32 mg/L. Brazilian pipeline studies ranked chloride ions, with aliphatic hydrocarbons, as the most aggressive contaminants. ASTM’s test method for chloride in fuel ethanol states the reason for testing plainly: sulphates and chlorides “may be found in filter plugging deposits and fuel injector deposits”.

Stainless steel is not immune. Chloride causes pitting and crevice corrosion in grades 304 and 316, especially where water stands and evaporates, which is why tank standards set limits on the water used to test and rinse them.

Chlorinated water for cleaning tanks: what the rules say

Most tanks, tank trucks and vessels in India are washed with ordinary treated water. IS 10500, the drinking-water standard, allows 250 mg/L of chloride (up to 1,000 mg/L where there is no other source) and requires at least 0.2 mg/L of free residual chlorine where water is chlorinated. That water is safe to drink and disinfects well. It is not chloride-free.

  • Oil-company practice. The ministry’s July 2026 answer says ethanol tanks at depots must be segregated, water-free and cleaned once every two years or as needed, with cleaning certificates kept and dates marked on the manhole cover. After a tank is cleaned, the first receipt is tested before any blending, vents carry silica-gel traps, and stock dormant for three months is retested. The answer does not say what water is used for cleaning or whether it is tested for chloride.
  • Tank construction standards. API 650, the international standard for welded oil-storage tanks, sets rules for stainless steel tanks in its Annex S: test water must be potable and treated with at least 0.2 ppm free chlorine but contain less than 50 ppm chloride; potable test water may stand for no more than 21 days; and the tank must then be drained completely and dried, with attention to low spots and crevices. Earlier editions limited rinse water to 200 ppm chloride below 40 °C.
  • Disinfection. UK water-industry guidance accepts short exposure of stainless steel to strongly chlorinated water during sterilisation, 25 to 50 mg/L of chlorine for 24 hours, provided it is “well flushed through the system immediately afterwards or pitting can occur”.
  • Distilleries. Chlorine dioxide is one of the biocides used to control bacteria in ethanol fermentation. Salts such as sodium chloride do not boil off with ethanol, so chloride from the fermentation side should stay behind in the spent wash; the risk is chloride picked up after distillation, from wet tanks, tankers and hoses.

The quantities show how far rinse water can and cannot explain the numbers. Take a 20,000-litre underground tank of E20, about 15 tonnes of fuel. If chloride reaches the fuel only through water left in the tank and the chloride dissolves fully into the fuel:

A few dozen litres of tap water left in a tank are enough to push E20 past SIAM’s proposed 1 mg/kg or the 3 mg/kg ethanol limit. Readings of several hundred mg/kg cannot come from cleaning water: they would need thousands of litres of water in a 20,000-litre tank. Such readings point to a far saltier source, to water that had separated and been sampled on its own, or to a measurement problem. This is arithmetic, not a finding about any particular outlet.

What would fix this

  • BIS should amend IS 15464 to apply the 3.0 mg/kg inorganic chloride limit, and the sulphate and water limits, to 1G ethanol, or publish the instrument that already does. A limit cited in press statements but absent from the standard cannot be enforced against a supplier.
  • Add a chloride limit to the standard for finished E20 petrol, as SIAM asked, so fuel can be tested at the pump against a published number.
  • Put chloride on the supplier’s certificate for every tank truck, next to purity, water, density, appearance and sulphur.
  • Specify the water used to clean ethanol and E20 tanks: demineralised or low-chloride water, a chloride limit on rinse water as API 650 does for stainless tanks, complete draining and drying, and a chloride test on the first receipt after cleaning.
  • Make sure BIS-recognised laboratories can run the chloride test (ASTM D7319 or EN 15492), and publish the oil companies’ and automakers’ results with sampling points and methods.

What this piece could not establish

  • The legal basis of the 3 ppm limit for 1G ethanol. No amendment, notification or contract clause was found.
  • The Centre for High Technology’s test tables, which the oil companies cite, could not be opened, so the outlet-level results and the two-versus-four count of suspended outlets were not checked.
  • The automakers’ sampling method. The withdrawn SIAM letter and the emails Reuters reported are known only through press coverage.
  • Current editions of foreign standards. The US figure comes from the Renewable Fuels Association’s 2018 summary of ASTM D4806; the 2025 edition was not read. China’s GB 18350 was not checked.
  • What water oil companies and dealers use to clean tanks, which the ministry’s answer does not say.
