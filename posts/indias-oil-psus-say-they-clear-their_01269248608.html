India's oil PSUs report clearing their mandatory 25% MSME procurement quota by a wide margin — the aggregate, government-wide numbers run into the 40s. Only one of the five major companies actually publishes its own individual figure. A federal audit found two of them hadn't even uploaded their procurement plans as required. And a government portal built specifically to make each PSU's compliance public doesn't, on the evidence checked for this piece, actually show it.

The short version.

  • Every central PSU, including the five major MoPNG oil & gas companies (IOCL, BPCL, HPCL, GAIL, ONGC), operates under the Public Procurement Policy for Micro and Small Enterprises (MSE) Order, 2012 (amended 2018): a minimum 25% of annual procurement value from MSEs, with 4% reserved for SC/ST-owned MSEs and 3% for women-owned ones. Aggregate, government-wide CPSE compliance runs comfortably above target: 36.06% in FY2023-24 and 43.58% in FY2024-25, per Ministry of MSME year-end figures.
  • Two of the five major oil PSUs publish an individual, checkable figure: IOCL reports 51.07% total MSE procurement for FY2025-26 (4.03% SC/ST, 3.39% women, both above the mandated sub-targets), and ONGC reports 29.35%, up from ₹262 crore when it began tracking this in 2013-14 to ₹1,378 crore by the most recent year covered in an undated ONGC press release. No comparably specific figure was found for BPCL, HPCL or GAIL, despite each publishing its own procurement-plan document (linked in the piece below).
  • The MSME SAMBANDH portal (sambandh.msme.gov.in), launched 8 December 2017 specifically to make CPSE-by-CPSE MSE compliance public, is real and does host pages built for exactly this purpose — a compliant-CPSE list, an all-CPSE list, an SC/ST-procurement page — tracked quarterly against the annual target. This piece could not access the portal directly to pull individual PSU percentages for FY2025-26, and no source found for this piece reports what those figures actually show, which is itself worth naming: a transparency tool exists, but this piece could not verify it is functioning as one for the companies in question.
  • Two specific claims circulating about this system needed correcting. The often-cited "358 reserved items" list — goods legally barred from large-industry bidding, reserved exclusively for MSEs — is real but dated: it includes now-obsolete categories (glue, blotting paper, tin boxes for postage stamps), and the Ministry of MSME has a revised, larger 469-item list out for consultation to replace it. And a claim that MoPNG procurement tenders "up to ₹1 crore" are exempt from local-content requirements does not match any verifiable rule: the actual thresholds are ₹5 lakh (below which the national Make in India procurement order doesn't apply at all) and ₹5 crore/₹10 crore (the tiers separating self-certified from independently-verified local-content claims under MoPNG's own local-content policy and the national order respectively) — no rule found anywhere uses ₹1 crore specifically.
  • A 2018 CAG audit found real cracks under the compliance surface: the body meant to enforce MSE procurement (DC-MSME) has no statutory power to penalise a non-compliant CPSE; eight audited CPSEs — including both ONGC and IOCL — hadn't uploaded their annual MSE procurement plans as required; and the grievance mechanism for MSEs denied a tender relaxation has no defined response timeline, with CAG finding MSEs "rarely receive a response before the tender is awarded." That's the same IOCL now separately reporting a 51% compliance figure — a reminder that a strong headline number and a documented process failure can coexist at the same company in different years.

The rules, layered: MSE procurement and local content are two separate systems

Every central government ministry, department and public sector enterprise in India, MoPNG's oil and gas PSUs included, operates under the Public Procurement Policy for Micro and Small Enterprises (MSE) Order, 2012, as amended in 2018. Its core requirement is a minimum 25% of a procuring entity's total annual purchase value sourced from MSEs, split into two protected sub-targets: 4% specifically from SC/ST-owned MSEs, and 3% from women-owned MSEs. To make that target achievable for smaller vendors competing against much larger industrial suppliers in a capital-intensive sector, the policy carries real concessions: MSEs don't pay for tender document sets, a validly Udyam-registered MSE is exempt from submitting an Earnest Money Deposit or bid security on items covered by its registration (a universal rule under General Financial Rules 2017, Rule 170, combined with the MSE Order, not something MoPNG invented for its own tenders), and a defined list of items is reserved for exclusive MSE procurement, where a large industrial bidder is legally barred from competing at all.

Layered on top of that, but running as an independent system rather than a merged one, is MoPNG's own Policy on Purchase Preference linked with Local Content (PPLC), Cabinet-approved on 12 April 2017 and applicable to all MoPNG PSUs, their wholly-owned subsidiaries, and joint ventures with 51% or greater PSU equity. It sorts bidders by local-content share into three classes — Class-I (50% or more local content), Class-II (20-50%), and non-local (20% or less) — with a purchase preference for Class-I bidders quoting within a defined price band of the lowest bidder. Nothing found for this piece shows how these two preference systems interact when the same bidder qualifies as both an MSE and a Class-I local-content supplier on the same tender — whether the preferences stack, or are evaluated as two separate, sequential filters. That's a genuine, documented gap in the public record, not a simplification this piece is making for length.

The MSE Order 2012/2018's 25%/4%/3% targets, the free-tender-document and reserved-item provisions, and the general shape of the policy are corroborated across multiple secondary legal and procurement-advisory summaries of the order. The EMD exemption's basis in GFR 2017 Rule 170 combined with the MSE Order, its Udyam-registration requirement, and its exclusion of resellers (non-manufacturers) from exemption on an item they don't themselves produce, are similarly corroborated across secondary procurement-compliance sources; this piece did not read GFR 2017's own text directly. MoPNG's PPLC policy, its 12 April 2017 Cabinet approval, its three-class local-content structure, and its applicability to PSUs/subsidiaries/51%-plus JVs are drawn from IOCL's own published copy of the policy order and corroborating coverage; the exact current price-preference band was not independently re-verified in the most recent check for this piece and should be confirmed against IOCL's or MoPNG's own current text before being cited as a precise figure. This piece searched specifically for, and could not find, any MoPNG or Ministry of MSME text describing how the MSE and PPLC preference systems interact on a single tender where both could apply.

The price-preference mechanism, and what's real about the "358 items" and "₹1 crore" claims

The MSE Order's actual counter to pure lowest-price ("L1") tendering is a specific price-matching mechanism: an MSE bidder quoting within L1+15% is given the chance to match the L1 price, and on doing so is entitled to at least 25% of the tendered value at that price — a minimum guaranteed share, not a fixed ceiling, and if multiple MSEs qualify simultaneously, that 25% is split proportionately among them by quantity; where the tendered item can't be divided, the full order can go to the single qualifying MSE instead. That detail, sometimes cited loosely, checks out as accurate against multiple independent secondary legal summaries of the order.

Two other specific, widely-repeated figures needed direct checking, and one of them doesn't hold up. The "358 reserved items" list — goods that large industrial suppliers are legally barred from bidding on, reserved exclusively for MSE procurement — is a real Ministry of MSME list, and its category examples (agricultural implements, electrical transmission products, conductors, low-tech engineering and chemical goods) are broadly consistent with what gets cited about it. But 358 is the older baseline count, not a current, stable figure: the list includes now-clearly-obsolete entries (glue, blotting paper, tin boxes for postage stamps are among the examples found), and the Ministry has a revised, larger 469-item list of goods and services out for public consultation specifically to replace it. Citing "358" as today's authoritative count risks understating both the list's age and the direction it's moving (expanding and modernising, not shrinking). Separately, a claim that MoPNG tenders "up to ₹1 crore" are exempt from strict local-content constraints does not match any verifiable rule found for this piece: the actual monetary thresholds in play are ₹5 lakh (below which India's national Make in India procurement order doesn't apply to a purchase at all) and ₹5 crore/₹10 crore (the tiers at which local-content claims move from self-certification to requiring independent statutory or cost-auditor verification, under MoPNG's own PPLC policy and the national order respectively). No rule located anywhere in this research uses ₹1 crore as a threshold of any kind, and that specific figure should be treated as an error rather than repeated.

The L1+15%/minimum-25%-share/proportionate-split mechanism is corroborated across multiple independent secondary legal summaries of the MSE Order 2012/2018; this piece did not read the order's own gazette text directly. The 358-item list's existence and example categories are from a Ministry of MSME-hosted PDF (dcmsme.gov.in) and secondary coverage; the 469-item revised list intended to replace it, and the stated rationale (obsolete entries in the current list), are from a separate Ministry of MSME consultation document and MyGov's public-consultation listing. The ₹5 lakh national-order exemption threshold and the ₹5 crore/₹10 crore self-certification-versus-verification tiers are corroborated across DPIIT's and MoPNG-linked procurement-policy secondary sources; the specific ₹1 crore figure was searched for directly and not found in any rule text or reliable secondary source, so it is reported here as an identified error rather than an unconfirmed claim.

What the compliance numbers actually show, and the one company with real figures

At the aggregate, government-wide level, CPSE compliance with the 25% MSE target looks strong and improving: Ministry of MSME's own year-end figures put total MSE procurement across all central ministries, departments and CPSEs at ₹82,630 crore (36.06% of total procurement) in FY2023-24, rising to ₹93,017 crore (43.58%) in FY2024-25 — both comfortably above the mandated floor. SC/ST-owned MSE procurement specifically, across the same CPSE universe, is reported to have grown roughly 37-fold over a decade, from ₹99.37 crore in 2015-16 to ₹3,731.47 crore in 2024-25. Set against those numbers, though, is a separate, much smaller figure from trade press: a reported 43% year-on-year decline in CPSE procurement from MSEs specifically, to ₹773.39 crore in FY24 from ₹1,369.45 crore in FY23, across 188 CPSEs. This piece could not reconcile that figure with the ₹82,630 crore aggregate — it likely describes a narrower sub-category of procurement rather than contradicting the larger number outright, but the exact scope wasn't confirmed, and both figures are reported here rather than one being silently preferred.

Among the five major MoPNG oil and gas PSUs specifically, two publish an individual figure specific enough to check. IOCL's own procurement disclosure states 51.07% of its eligible annual procurement came from MSEs in FY2025-26, including 4.03% from SC/ST-owned MSEs and 3.39% from women-owned MSEs — both above their respective sub-targets, comfortably clearing the overall 25% floor as well. ONGC's own site, in a press release titled "ONGC Strengthens the Ecosystem for Micro and Small Enterprises," reports its MSE procurement grew from ₹262 crore when it began tracking this in 2013-14 to ₹1,378 crore by the most recent fiscal year covered in that release — 29.35% of total eligible procurement, also above the 25% target, though this piece could not pin down which specific fiscal year "most recent" refers to in an undated release, so the figure should be read as a real but not precisely dated data point. No comparably specific, sourced figure was found for BPCL, HPCL or GAIL, despite each company maintaining its own procurement-plan disclosure (linked in the note below), and despite the existence of the MSME SAMBANDH portal — a government platform launched on 8 December 2017 specifically to publish CPSE-by-CPSE compliance factsheets, tracked quarterly against the annual target, with pages built for exactly this purpose (a compliant-CPSE list, a full CPSE list, an SC/ST-procurement breakdown). This piece could not directly access that portal to pull current figures for the remaining three companies, and found no secondary source reporting what it currently shows for them — which means the honest state of the evidence is that a transparency mechanism exists on paper, but this piece cannot confirm it is currently surfacing usable, company-specific data for three of the five PSUs in question.

The compliance process itself has documented cracks, at these same companies

A 2018 Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) audit report (Report No. 18 of 2018) looked underneath the aggregate compliance numbers at the process meant to produce them, and found real structural weaknesses. The office responsible for monitoring MSE procurement compliance, DC-MSME, has no statutory power to penalise a CPSE that falls short of the target — CPSEs sit administratively under the Department of Public Enterprises, outside DC-MSME's enforcement reach. Among the CPSEs the audit examined, eight had not uploaded their annual MSE procurement plans as required — a list that names both ONGC and IOCL specifically, alongside NLC, RINL, BHEL, SAIL, Balmer Lawrie and Coal India. Separately, eight of the eighteen CPSEs audited had run no vendor development programme at all despite a 2016 directive requiring one, and the grievance mechanism meant to handle an MSE's complaint when a tender relaxation is denied has no defined response timeline — CAG's own finding was that MSEs "rarely receive a response before the tender is awarded," which makes the L1+15% remedy described in Section 2 largely theoretical for a vendor whose relaxation request gets stuck in that gap. The audit also flagged that procuring entities frequently invoke a "public safety" or "critical equipment" exception, with no objective criteria defined for when it applies, to deny MSE relaxations outright — a mechanism plausibly easy to reach for in a technically demanding sector like oil and gas equipment, though this piece found no source stating that oil-sector procurers specifically have used it more than other sectors.

Naming ONGC and IOCL in the CAG's 2018 finding isn't offered here as proof either company is non-compliant today — the audit describes a specific compliance-process failure (not uploading a procurement plan) in a specific year, and IOCL's own current disclosure reports a strong 51% figure years later. The point worth taking from putting the two facts next to each other is narrower: a company can report a strong headline compliance number in one year while having a documented, process-level compliance gap in another, and neither fact cancels the other out. Readers should treat IOCL's 51% figure as real and current on its own terms, and treat the CAG finding as a real, if now-dated, illustration of how the underlying enforcement machinery can fail even at large, well-resourced PSUs — not as evidence about IOCL's or ONGC's compliance today specifically.

CAG Report No. 18 of 2018's findings on DC-MSME's lack of penalty authority, the eight CPSEs (including ONGC and IOCL by name) that hadn't uploaded MSE procurement plans, the vendor-development-programme gap, the grievance-mechanism timeline gap, and the undefined "public safety"/"critical equipment" exception are drawn from secondary legal and procurement-advisory coverage summarising the CAG report; this piece did not read the CAG report's own full text directly in this session. No source found for this piece states that oil-and-gas-sector procurers specifically invoke the safety/critical-equipment exception more often than other sectors; that connection is this piece's own contextual observation, explicitly flagged as such, not a claim any source makes directly.

What doesn't follow from any of this

None of the above should be read as proof that India's oil PSUs are failing their MSME obligations today — the aggregate CPSE-wide numbers, IOCL's own figure, and ONGC's own figure all clear the 25% target with real margin, and nothing found for this piece contradicts any of those numbers directly. What this piece can't do is extend that same confidence to BPCL, HPCL or GAIL individually, because no comparably specific, sourced compliance figure exists for them in the material this piece could access, and the government portal built to supply exactly that transparency could not be verified as currently doing so. The CAG findings in Section 4 describe a 2018 audit, not a current-year assessment, and shouldn't be read as describing today's compliance state at any named company. Similarly, the interaction between the MSE procurement preference and MoPNG's own local-content preference (Section 1) remains a genuine unanswered question in the public record this piece searched, not a gap this piece is choosing not to resolve for space. Readers relying on any specific figure, threshold, or company's compliance status here for a real vendor-registration or procurement decision should verify it directly against that company's current MSE procurement-plan disclosure, MoPNG's own current policy text, or the MSME SAMBANDH portal itself, rather than this summary.
